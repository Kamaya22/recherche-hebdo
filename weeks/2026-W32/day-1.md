# Antibiotic resistance — Day 1/7: What We Are Fighting and Why It Matters

## Context (2–3 paragraphs)

Antibiotic resistance is a phrase that appears regularly in news coverage yet rarely receives the depth of explanation it deserves. Most reporting focuses on individual incidents — a hospital cluster of an untreatable organism, a celebrity warning about "superbugs" — without conveying the underlying biology, the historical trajectory, or the genuine scale of the threat. This week repairs that deficit by building the subject systematically from the ground up: today's reading establishes the foundations that all subsequent days will draw on.

The basics are essential but genuinely interesting. Antibiotics are molecules — mostly derived from soil bacteria and fungi, occasionally fully synthetic — that interfere with processes bacteria need but human cells do not: building cell walls, synthesising proteins on ribosomes, replicating DNA, maintaining membrane integrity. The genius of penicillin, when Fleming stumbled upon it in 1928 and Chain and Florey later purified and tested it in the 1940s, was that it targeted the cross-linking of peptidoglycan, the polymer mesh that forms the bacterial cell wall — a structure human cells simply lack. Resistance, in its simplest conception, is what happens when a bacterium survives antibiotic exposure and reproduces: the offspring inherit whatever trait allowed survival, natural selection does the rest, and a drug that once worked reliably no longer does. What makes resistance a genuine civilisational challenge is the combination of its speed, its global spread via horizontal gene transfer (bacteria directly sharing resistance genes across species), its convergence on last-resort drugs, and the near-total collapse of the antibiotic discovery pipeline since the 1970s.

Today's reading establishes the big picture: how we know what we know about the scale of the problem, what the numbers actually say, and why this constitutes an emergency that is both ongoing and qualitatively different from most other health crises. Days 2 through 6 will go progressively deeper — into the molecular mechanisms of resistance, the economics of drug discovery, global surveillance, the agricultural and environmental dimensions, and the most promising new strategies. Day 7 synthesises the week.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

### What antibiotics do — and where they come from

The word "antibiotic" literally means "against life," but in clinical use it refers to molecules that kill or inhibit the growth of bacteria while leaving human cells sufficiently intact. The key to this selectivity is exploiting structural or biochemical differences between bacterial and human cells.

The major drug classes each hit a different target. *Beta-lactams* (penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, monobactams) inhibit the enzymes that cross-link peptidoglycan, the polymer mesh of the bacterial cell wall; bacteria under osmotic pressure burst open without it. *Aminoglycosides* (streptomycin, gentamicin) and *macrolides* (erythromycin, azithromycin) bind to bacterial ribosomes — specifically the 30S and 50S subunits, which differ structurally from human ribosomes and can therefore be targeted selectively. *Fluoroquinolones* (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin) inhibit bacterial DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV, enzymes used to untangle DNA during replication; human cells use different topoisomerases that are not affected at therapeutic concentrations. *Glycopeptides* (vancomycin, teicoplanin) bind directly to the peptidoglycan precursor molecule rather than the enzyme, blocking wall assembly. *Rifamycins* (rifampicin) bind to bacterial RNA polymerase, blocking transcription. *Polymyxins* (colistin, polymyxin B) disrupt the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria by displacing divalent cations and inserting into the lipid bilayer.

The vast majority of antibiotic classes in clinical use today were discovered by screening soil microorganisms — primarily *Streptomyces* bacteria and various fungi — for compounds that could kill bacterial pathogens in laboratory tests. This approach, initiated systematically in the 1940s, yielded an extraordinary catalogue: streptomycin (1943), chloramphenicol (1947), tetracyclines (1948), erythromycin (1952), vancomycin (1953), rifampicin (1957), and dozens of others. The "golden age" of antibiotic discovery from the 1940s through the 1960s accounts for most of the antibiotic classes still used today. Fleming, accepting his Nobel Prize in 1945, specifically warned that misuse of penicillin would allow bacteria to develop resistance — one of the most precisely prescient scientific predictions in modern medicine.

### What resistance means — and why it is inevitable

Resistance is, at its core, a consequence of Darwinian natural selection operating at a timescale that humans find difficult to intuit. Any large population of bacteria harbours enormous genetic diversity, including rare variants with mutations or acquired genes that happen to confer some degree of tolerance to a given antibiotic. When that antibiotic is introduced, those variants have a dramatic fitness advantage: they survive and reproduce while their siblings die. Because bacteria can divide every twenty to forty minutes, within hours a population can shift from nearly all susceptible to predominantly resistant. This is not a pathology of bacteria — it is the ordinary logic of selection applied to organisms with enormous population sizes and short generation times.

What makes bacterial resistance exceptional compared with other forms of drug resistance (such as antiviral or antifungal resistance) is *horizontal gene transfer* (HGT): the direct passage of genetic material between cells that are not parent and offspring. Bacteria exchange genetic elements via three main routes. *Conjugation* involves one bacterium forming a physical bridge (pilus) with another and transferring plasmids — circular DNA elements that often carry multiple resistance genes together in a single package. *Transformation* occurs when a bacterium takes up naked DNA from its environment, including DNA released by dead bacteria. *Transduction* is mediated by bacteriophages (bacterial viruses) that accidentally package and deliver host DNA, including resistance genes, to new cells. In all three cases, the recipient can be a completely different bacterial species, genus, or even phylum from the donor.

The practical consequence is dramatic. A carbapenem-resistance gene (such as *bla*NDM-1, encoding the New Delhi Metallo-beta-lactamase) that emerges in *Klebsiella pneumoniae* in a hospital can spread via conjugative plasmid to *Escherichia coli*, *Acinetobacter baumannii*, and *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* within the same patient, the same ward, or the same hospital sewage system — and via global travel and trade, to other continents within months. The mobilome — the totality of mobile genetic elements in a microbial community — is effectively a global commons for resistance genes, and any use of antibiotics anywhere enriches it.

### The scale of the crisis: what the numbers say

Before 2022, the most widely cited quantitative estimate of AMR's burden came from a 2014 economic review commissioned by the UK government: "10 million deaths per year by 2050." That figure was influential but was not derived from systematic epidemiological analysis. The 2022 GRAM study changed that. Using a systematic analysis of patient records, microbiology data, and statistical modelling across 204 countries, it estimated that in 2019 alone:

- **1.27 million deaths were directly attributable to AMR** (i.e., the infection would not have been fatal, or would have been treated, without resistance)
- **4.95 million deaths were associated with AMR** (resistant infections contributed to death alongside other causes)

For comparison: HIV/AIDS killed approximately 860,000 people in 2019; malaria killed approximately 627,000. AMR had already overtaken both as a direct cause of death, and the data had simply not been compiled until that point.

The burden was not uniformly distributed. Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia faced the highest mortality rates — above 75 deaths per 100,000 population in some countries — compared with high-income countries (roughly 12–28 per 100,000). Three infectious syndromes accounted for nearly 80% of the directly attributable deaths: lower respiratory infections (including pneumonia), bloodstream infections (sepsis), and intra-abdominal infections. Just six pathogens — *Escherichia coli*, *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Acinetobacter baumannii*, and *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* — were responsible for the majority of AMR deaths. *E. coli*, which is ubiquitous in the gut and environment, accounted for 216,000 directly attributable deaths in 2019 — largely through its resistance to fluoroquinolones and extended-spectrum beta-lactamases, which has become nearly routine in many countries.

The 2024 GRAM forecast study deepened this picture by extending the analysis back to 1990 (revealing a steadily rising burden) and projecting forward to 2050. The projections are sobering:

- More than **39 million deaths directly attributable to AMR** are projected between 2025 and 2050
- **169 million deaths associated with AMR** over the same period
- AMR-attributable deaths will reach **1.91 million per year by 2050**, up 70% from 2022
- Deaths in **children under five** from AMR have fallen substantially (reflecting vaccination gains and improved sanitation in low-income settings)
- Deaths in **people aged 70 and over** will rise by 146%, driven by ageing populations in Asia and elsewhere

That last point is particularly significant. The good news story of declining childhood mortality from AMR reflects genuine public health progress in low-income countries. But as those same countries develop, their populations age, and older people are simultaneously more likely to require surgery, hospitalisation, and antibiotic treatment — exactly the conditions that generate selection pressure for resistant pathogens. The AMR burden is tracking the global epidemiological transition.

### Where the pipeline broke

The golden age of antibiotic discovery ended in the late 1960s and early 1970s. The reasons are both scientific and economic. Scientifically, systematic soil screening had exhausted the most accessible organisms: new isolates increasingly produced compounds already known, a problem researchers called "dereplication." The chemical space of natural antibiotic scaffolds accessible to culture-based screening had, to a first approximation, been explored.

Economically, antibiotics face a structural problem unique in medicine: the better they work, the more their use should be restricted. A highly effective new antibiotic should ideally be held in reserve for resistant infections that cannot be treated with existing drugs — which means low sales volumes, which means poor return on the enormous investment required to develop a new drug. Unlike a drug for a chronic condition (taken daily for decades), antibiotics are taken for five to fourteen days. The combination of high development costs, short treatment durations, deliberate restriction of use, and the near-certainty that resistance will eventually erode efficacy made antibiotic R&D an unattractive investment for shareholders. Major pharmaceutical companies — including Eli Lilly, Bristol-Myers Squibb, and Roche — exited the field between the 1980s and 2000s.

The WHO's 2023 clinical pipeline review recorded 97 agents in clinical development: 57 traditional antibiotics and 40 non-traditional therapies (including bacteriophages, monoclonal antibodies targeting toxins, and microbiome-modulating approaches). Of the 57 traditional antibiotics, fewer than a quarter represent genuinely novel mechanisms, new chemical classes, or validated new targets; the majority are derivatives of existing scaffolds, against which resistance can emerge rapidly through mechanisms that already exist in clinical pathogens. The most critical gap — consistently identified by WHO's Bacterial Priority Pathogen List — is against carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative organisms: *Acinetobacter baumannii*, carbapenem-resistant *Klebsiella pneumoniae* and *E. coli*, and *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*. These pathogens combine multiple resistance mechanisms and represent the clinical frontiers against which current options are most likely to fail.

The picture that emerges from today's readings is of a problem that is well-understood scientifically and deeply mismanaged institutionally. We know, in remarkable molecular detail, how bacteria become resistant and how resistance spreads — Days 2 and 4 go deeper into those mechanisms and their surveillance. We have the epidemiological data to describe the burden with confidence. And yet the institutional, economic, and policy responses have been insufficient to change the trajectory.

## Questions to think about

1. Fleming warned in 1945 that resistance to penicillin would emerge if the drug were used indiscriminately. Resistance was documented in *Staphylococcus aureus* within three years of introduction, and the pattern has repeated with every antibiotic class since. If resistance is essentially inevitable given sufficient selective pressure, does this change how you think about the goal of antibiotic development? Is the pipeline crisis about "finding better drugs," or about something more fundamental in how we use and govern drugs once found?

2. The 2024 GRAM study projects that AMR deaths in children under five are falling even as deaths in the elderly are rising sharply. What does this tell us about the relationship between economic development, healthcare infrastructure, and antibiotic resistance? Is it possible for a country to largely "solve" the paediatric burden of AMR while simultaneously creating the conditions for an elderly AMR crisis as it develops?

3. The WHO's 2023 pipeline review identifies 97 agents in clinical development, but notes that most are derivatives of existing antibiotic classes. Why does chemical novelty matter so much? If a new derivative of an existing cephalosporin works effectively against a currently resistant pathogen, why might that be insufficient as a durable long-term solution?

## This week's theme

**Antibiotic resistance — mechanisms, global surveillance, and the pipeline crisis** (2026-W32)

Antibiotics are the scaffolding on which modern medicine rests. They make surgery safe, allow us to treat pneumonias that were once fatal, protect immunocompromised patients receiving chemotherapy or organ transplants, and underlie the routine treatment of bloodstream infections that were once near-certain death sentences. The discovery of penicillin in 1928 and the "golden age" of antibiotic discovery between the 1940s and 1960s transformed medicine — and bacteria have been evolving counter-strategies ever since.

In 2019, bacterial antimicrobial resistance directly killed an estimated 1.27 million people and was associated with nearly 5 million further deaths — more than HIV/AIDS or malaria that year. A 2024 analysis in *The Lancet* projects that without coordinated action, more than 39 million people will die directly from antibiotic-resistant infections between 2025 and 2050. The crisis has two intertwined dimensions: bacteria continuously evolving resistance through mechanisms ranging from enzymes that dismantle drug molecules to gene-sharing networks that spread resistance across species, and a discovery pipeline that has nearly dried up because the economics of antibiotic development are deeply unfavourable to private investment.

Understanding this crisis requires fluency across molecular biology, infectious disease epidemiology, agriculture, economics, and global health governance. This week builds that understanding systematically — from the molecular mechanisms of resistance (Day 2), through the economics of the pipeline crisis (Day 3), global surveillance systems (Day 4), the One Health link between human medicine and agriculture (Day 5), and promising new strategies (Day 6), to a synthesis on Day 7 of what is settled, what is contested, and what must change.
