# Antibiotic resistance — Day 4/7: Surveillance and the Global Resistance Landscape

## Context (2–3 paragraphs)

Day 1 established the scale of the crisis — 1.27 million deaths directly attributable to antimicrobial resistance in 2019, with projections rising to 1.91 million per year by 2050. Day 3 explored why the pharmaceutical response has been so inadequate, tracing the structural market failure that drove most major companies out of antibiotic R&D and examining the push/pull incentive mechanisms being developed to address it. But implicit in both of those discussions was a question that today's reading answers explicitly: how do we actually know these numbers, and what do the surveillance systems that generate them look like?

Measuring antimicrobial resistance is harder than it sounds. To determine that a given bacterial pathogen is resistant to a given drug, you need a functioning clinical microbiology laboratory, standardised testing methods, a system for recording and reporting results, and the organisational infrastructure to aggregate those results across hospitals, districts, and countries. In practice, these prerequisites are unequally distributed in almost exactly the same way that the burden of AMR is unequally distributed: the countries facing the highest rates of resistant infection are also the countries with the fewest functioning laboratories and the most incomplete reporting. Understanding how global AMR is measured — and where the data are thin — is therefore both a scientific and a political issue.

Today's reading examines three interlocking topics. First: the WHO's Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System (GLASS), launched in 2015, which is the closest thing we have to a systematic global census of resistance in clinical pathogens. Second: the 2024 WHO Bacterial Priority Pathogens List, the framework that distils surveillance data into an actionable ranking of which organisms most urgently require new drugs, diagnostics, and interventions. Third: the global epidemiology of one of the highest-ranked organisms — carbapenem-resistant *Acinetobacter baumannii* — as a case study in what high-quality regional surveillance reveals when it is available.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

### Why measuring resistance is a political act as much as a technical one

Quantifying antimicrobial resistance requires a chain of steps that most of the world's health systems cannot reliably execute. A blood sample from a patient with sepsis must be drawn cleanly and transported quickly to a laboratory with biosafety-compliant culture equipment. The laboratory must incubate the sample, identify any growth to the species level, then run antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) using standardised breakpoints (the numerical thresholds below which an organism is classified "susceptible" and above which it is "resistant"). Results must be recorded electronically, cleaned, aggregated at the national level, and reported to an international system in a format that allows meaningful comparison across countries with different laboratory methods, patient populations, and clinical coding conventions.

Every link in that chain fails somewhere. Fewer than 1% of clinical laboratories across sub-Saharan Africa are set up to perform automated susceptibility testing. Large proportions of South and Southeast Asia lack the cold-chain and transport infrastructure needed to deliver viable blood cultures. Many countries that do have functioning laboratories use different AST breakpoints (EUCAST versus CLSI standards) or different antibiotic panels, making their data incomparable without harmonisation. And even where data are collected, national health systems vary enormously in their willingness to report unflattering surveillance figures to international bodies.

What this means is that "global" AMR surveillance data is, to a significant degree, a picture drawn by the countries with the best laboratories — which are also, broadly, the countries with the lowest AMR burden. Countries reporting the most data to GLASS include high-income European nations, North America, and parts of Asia-Pacific. Countries reporting little or nothing to GLASS include much of sub-Saharan Africa, large parts of South Asia, and much of Latin America. The picture we have of global AMR is therefore systematically biased toward regions where resistance is lower and laboratory infrastructure is stronger.

### What GLASS is and how it works

WHO GLASS (Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System) was established in 2015 by the World Health Assembly as part of the Global Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance. The system was designed to solve a structural problem that had bedevilled all previous AMR monitoring efforts: different countries collected different data, using different methods, on different pathogens, without any mechanism for integration.

GLASS standardises the submission of national AMR surveillance data around a defined set of pathogen-drug combinations, four core clinical syndromes (bloodstream infections, urinary tract infections, gastrointestinal infections, and gonorrhoea), and agreed laboratory and reporting protocols. It also, since the 2022 report cycle, collects national antibiotic consumption (AMC) data alongside resistance data — a pairing that allows researchers to directly measure whether the correlation between antibiotic use and resistance is visible at the national level (it is, clearly and robustly, as discussed below).

GLASS has grown substantially since its launch. In 2016, 25 countries contributed data; by end 2024, 130 countries and territories were enrolled, with 104 providing AMR data in 2023 — an increase of over 300% in participating countries across the period. The 2025 report analyses more than 23 million laboratory-confirmed infections, the largest global AMR dataset assembled to date. That said, the headline participation figures obscure important limitations: many countries participate but report data from only a small number of sentinel hospitals rather than nationally representative samples, and the quality and completeness of what is reported varies enormously.

### What the GLASS 2025 data show

The GLASS 2025 report's headline finding — presented at the WHO's press conference in October 2025 — is that approximately **one in six laboratory-confirmed bacterial infections globally is resistant** to the antibiotics used to treat it. This global average of 17.2% conceals very large regional variation:

- **Southeast Asian Region: 31.1% resistance** — the highest of any WHO region
- **Eastern Mediterranean Region: 30.0%** — nearly 1 in 3 infections
- **African Region: approximately 1 in 5 infections**
- **Americas Region: approximately 1 in 7 infections**
- **European Region: approximately 10.2%** — among the lowest globally
- **Western Pacific Region: approximately 9.1%** — the lowest of any region

For urinary tract infections specifically — the most common indication for antibiotic prescribing — the rate of resistance is substantially higher in high-burden regions, reaching 1 in 3 infections or more.

The trend data are equally alarming. Between 2018 and 2023, antibiotic resistance increased in **more than 40% of the pathogen-antibiotic combinations monitored**, with an average relative annual increase of 5 to 15% per combination. This means that even the modest resistance rates recorded in some high-income countries are not static baselines — they are steadily rising year on year.

Earlier GLASS data (from the 2022 report, covering infections reported in 2020) had already established one of the most clinically significant benchmarks: **44.8% of bloodstream infections caused by *E. coli* were resistant to third-generation cephalosporins** — the standard first-line treatment for serious *E. coli* infections — and **55.2% of bloodstream infections caused by *Klebsiella pneumoniae* were resistant to the same drugs**. In Africa, resistance exceeded 70% for both pathogens. Globally, 8% of *K. pneumoniae* bloodstream infections were resistant to carbapenems — the treatment of last resort — with that proportion substantially higher in some countries. These numbers mean that in many clinical settings, empiric antibiotic therapy (the treatment a doctor starts before culture results are back) will fail in a majority of patients with serious bloodstream infections, unless the prescribing clinician happens to choose one of the few drugs that still work.

A peer-reviewed analysis of the GLASS 2022 data, published in *PLOS ONE* in 2024, confirmed a statistically significant positive relationship between national antibiotic consumption volumes and national resistance rates. Every one-unit increase in defined daily doses (DDD) of beta-lactams and cephalosporins was associated with an 11–22% increase in resistant *E. coli*/*Klebsiella* bloodstream infections across participating countries; for fluoroquinolone consumption, the associated increase in fluoroquinolone-resistant isolates was 31–40%. This finding — that more antibiotic use predicts more resistance, at the country level, in the very same drug classes — is perhaps the clearest direct empirical validation of the selection-pressure model of resistance described in Day 1.

### The ESKAPE concept and the 2024 WHO Bacterial Priority Pathogens List

The acronym ESKAPE was coined by infectious disease researchers at the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) in 2008 to call attention to six organisms that had become the leading causes of difficult-to-treat hospital infections: *Enterococcus faecium*, *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, *Acinetobacter baumannii*, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, and *Enterobacter* species. The name was deliberately chosen to emphasise that these organisms "escape" the action of most antibiotics available at the time. The ESKAPE acronym popularised the concept of a short-list of clinically critical resistant pathogens and influenced how both researchers and policymakers thought about the AMR problem for the following decade.

The 2024 WHO Bacterial Priority Pathogens List (BPPL) — first established in 2017 and updated using more rigorous methodology in 2024 — is the successor to this conceptual framing, replacing an informal list with a systematic multi-criteria decision analysis (MCDA). The 2024 update scored 24 resistant pathogens across 15 bacterial families against eight criteria: mortality, morbidity, trends in resistance, transmissibility, preventability, treatability, pipeline coverage, and one-health relevance. Each criterion was weighted by a panel of clinical and academic experts.

The results, published in *The Lancet Infectious Diseases* in 2025, confirmed the dominance of Gram-negative pathogens at the top of the list. The **critical priority** tier includes:

- **Carbapenem-resistant *Klebsiella pneumoniae*** — ranked first overall, with a composite score of 84%. Combines extremely high mortality in bloodstream infections, globally spreading carbapenemase genes (including KPC and NDM), very limited treatment options, and a severely inadequate pipeline.
- **Carbapenem-resistant *Acinetobacter baumannii*** — top-ranked for transmissibility and environmental persistence; the leading cause of ICU outbreaks worldwide.
- **Third-generation cephalosporin-resistant *Escherichia coli*** — the most common cause of community-acquired urinary tract and bloodstream infections globally; resistance is now routine in many countries.
- **Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales** (including *K. pneumoniae*, *E. coli*, *Enterobacter* spp. and related organisms when carbapenem-resistant) — the broadest category of urgent clinical need.
- **Rifampicin-resistant *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*** — a significant addition from the 2017 list, reflecting both the enormous global burden of drug-resistant TB (particularly in South and Southeast Asia and sub-Saharan Africa) and the overlap between AMR governance mechanisms and TB policy.

The **high priority** tier includes fluoroquinolone-resistant *Salmonella* Typhi (the cause of typhoid fever, newly highlighted for its community-transmission risk in low-income countries), fluoroquinolone-resistant *Shigella* spp. (a leading cause of diarrhoeal disease), and *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* with resistance to extended-spectrum cephalosporins (the last reliably effective antibiotic class for gonorrhoea treatment). Notable **deprioritisations** relative to 2017 include vancomycin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus* (VRSA) — which remains exceedingly rare globally despite early fears — and penicillin-resistant *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, where vaccines have dramatically reduced the disease burden.

### CRAB: a case study in global resistance epidemiology

Carbapenem-resistant *Acinetobacter baumannii* (CRAb) offers a particularly instructive case study in the geographic and epidemiological structure of AMR. Boutzoukas and Doi's 2025 review in *JAC-Antimicrobial Resistance* synthesises the global epidemiology of CRAb across 2014–2024, and several of its findings cut to the heart of what surveillance data does and does not tell us.

*A. baumannii* is an extraordinarily hardy environmental organism — it can survive on dry hospital surfaces for weeks, is intrinsically resistant to several antibiotic classes, and acquires carbapenem resistance through multiple mechanisms including OXA-type carbapenemases (particularly OXA-23 and OXA-24/40, which can be encoded on mobile genetic elements and spread readily between isolates). CRAb causes primarily healthcare-associated infections: ventilator-associated pneumonia, catheter-associated bloodstream infections, wound infections, and meningitis in post-neurosurgical patients. It is concentrated almost entirely in intensive care units, where patients are mechanically ventilated, immunocompromised, and receiving broad-spectrum antibiotics that create selection pressure for the most resistant organisms.

The global distribution of CRAb is strikingly unequal, even among high-income countries with functional surveillance systems. In Northern Europe — Finland, Norway, the Netherlands — carbapenem resistance rates in *A. baumannii* clinical isolates remain below 1%. In Southern and Southeastern Europe — Greece, Italy, Romania, Turkey — rates of ≥50% carbapenem resistance are common, with some settings approaching 80–100%. This stark gradient within a single high-income region where laboratory capacity and reporting are broadly comparable reflects genuine differences in infection prevention and control practices, antibiotic stewardship programs, ICU case-mix, and the historical importation of resistant strains through patient transfers and medical tourism. The COVID-19 pandemic substantially amplified this problem: ICU occupancy surged globally, patients were mechanically ventilated for extended periods, broad-spectrum antibiotics were used heavily (often irrationally, against a viral pathogen), and CRAb took advantage. Multiple studies documented CRAb ICU outbreaks during 2020–2022 in countries where CRAb had previously been uncommon.

Globally, CRAb causes an estimated **more than 50,000 deaths per year**, making it one of the leading individual pathogens in AMR mortality. Treatment options are profoundly limited: the polymyxins (colistin, polymyxin B) retain activity against most CRAb isolates but are nephrotoxic and available only intravenously. Cefiderocol, a novel siderophore cephalosporin approved in the US in 2019 and the EU in 2020, is active against most CRAb isolates and substantially less toxic than polymyxins — but resistance has already been reported, and the drug remains expensive and not widely available in low-income settings. Sulbactam-durlobactam, a newer combination approved by the FDA in 2023 specifically for CRAb, provides another option but at high cost and limited global availability. This treatment landscape — a handful of costly, partially effective options for an organism that is increasingly common and kills the most vulnerable patients — encapsulates the broader AMR crisis.

### The information paradox and what it implies

The most uncomfortable conclusion from Day 4's readings is that we are best-informed about AMR in exactly the settings where the problem is least severe, and worst-informed where it is worst. The 2025 GLASS data showing 10% resistance rates in Western Europe and 31% in Southeast Asia are not just a description of the two regions' epidemiology — they also reflect the fact that Western European countries have contributed richer, more reliable data to GLASS for a decade longer than most Southeast Asian countries. The true resistance burden in low-income settings may be substantially higher than what appears in GLASS, because the most resistant organisms are also the ones that cause infections in patients without access to a microbiology laboratory.

This matters for how we interpret burden estimates. The GRAM 2022 figures (1.27 million directly attributable deaths in 2019) were derived using sophisticated modelling to extrapolate from incomplete surveillance data — they are the best available estimates, and the modelling is rigorous, but the uncertainty intervals are wide, and they depend on assumptions about how resistance patterns in countries with laboratory data apply to countries without it. Improving that picture requires not just better drugs and better stewardship programs — it requires investment in laboratory infrastructure as a public-health good, in the same way that vaccination programs require investment in cold chains and health worker training.

## Questions to think about

1. The 2024 WHO Bacterial Priority Pathogens List adds rifampicin-resistant *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* to the critical tier, alongside hospital-acquired pathogens like CRAB and carbapenem-resistant *K. pneumoniae*. TB has its own dedicated global governance infrastructure (the Stop TB Partnership, the WHO End TB Strategy, the Global Fund). What does placing TB on the same priority list as ICU-acquired infections signal about how AMR governance is evolving — and is unifying these agendas under the AMR umbrella helpful, or does it risk diluting policy focus?

2. The GLASS data show a clear correlation between national antibiotic consumption volumes and national resistance rates — more consumption, more resistance, in the same antibiotic classes, measured across dozens of countries. Yet prescribing practices are shaped by dozens of factors, including whether antibiotics require a prescription, whether diagnostic infrastructure exists to confirm bacterial (vs. viral) infections, whether generic antibiotics are available without a doctor, and whether patients can afford to complete a full course. Which of these factors do you think contributes most to the consumption-resistance correlation in low-income settings, and what would effective stewardship look like in a country without functioning primary-care infrastructure?

3. Carbapenem-resistant *Acinetobacter baumannii* shows resistance rates below 1% in Finland and Norway, and above 50% in Greece and Romania — all high-income EU member states with comparable health expenditure and laboratory capacity. What does this intra-European variation tell us about the determinants of healthcare-associated infection rates beyond economic development? If a northern European country's surveillance programme shows CRAB rates starting to rise, what would be the most effective early-response measures — and which would require decades rather than months to show effect?
