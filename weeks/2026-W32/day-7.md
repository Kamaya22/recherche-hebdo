# Antibiotic resistance — Day 7/7: Synthesis — What We Know, What We Don't, and What Must Change

## Context (2–3 paragraphs)

This is the final reading of the week, and its job is different from the previous six. Days 1 through 6 built the subject one layer at a time: the scale of the crisis and its historical roots (Day 1); the structural market failure in antibiotic development and the push/pull incentive proposals being developed to repair it (Day 3); the global surveillance architecture and the WHO priority pathogen framework, with carbapenem-resistant *Acinetobacter baumannii* as a case study (Day 4); the One Health ecology of resistance, tracing how agricultural and environmental antibiotic use generates resistance that eventually reaches human clinicians (Day 5); and the diversified toolkit of non-conventional strategies — engineered bacteriophages, next-generation beta-lactam/inhibitor combinations, antimicrobial peptides, genuinely novel drug classes, and the economic models being designed to sustain future development — together with the stewardship infrastructure needed to deploy them wisely (Day 6). Each day added depth to the prior one, but synthesis remained deferred. Today collects the threads.

A synthesis reading has a specific intellectual task: distinguish what is well-established from what remains actively debated, identify the genuinely open questions that specialists themselves disagree about, and offer a map for going deeper. This is not a straightforward exercise for AMR, because the field sits at the intersection of microbiology, epidemiology, economics, international governance, agriculture, and environmental science — and the contested questions span all of these. The goal is not to arrive at a comfortable conclusion but to understand the problem at the level of resolution where productive disagreement lives: where the data become thin, where the interests conflict, where the science is clear but the political will is absent, or where the conceptual frameworks are themselves still being worked out.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

### The arc of the week: a collision between biology and institutions

The week opened with a foundational distinction that shapes everything that follows: resistance as biology versus resistance as a policy crisis. Biologically, resistance is the entirely expected outcome of natural selection applied to organisms with enormous population sizes, rapid reproduction, and access to horizontal gene transfer mechanisms that allow resistance genes to move across species in real time. Bacteria are not "fighting back" in any intentional sense — they are evolving, which is what biological organisms do when placed under selection pressure. What makes AMR a crisis is not the biology per se, but its collision with human institutions: a pharmaceutical industry that cannot profitably develop new drugs at the scale needed; surveillance systems that cannot see the burden in the settings where it is heaviest; agricultural systems that use antibiotics in ways that amplify resistance reservoirs across ecosystems; and international governance architectures that have, to date, produced principled declarations without translating them into action proportionate to the scale of the problem.

This collision was the dominant theme of every reading day, articulated differently in each context. Day 1 showed the gap between what epidemiological data establish (1.27 million directly attributable deaths in 2019, rising to a projected 1.91 million per year by 2050) and the proportionality of the response. Day 3 showed the collision between clinical urgency and investment economics, documented through the bankruptcies of companies whose drugs worked but whose markets did not. Day 4 revealed a surveillance paradox: we are best-informed about AMR in the countries where resistance is lowest. Day 5 showed the collision between agricultural economics and public-health ecology — resistance selected by livestock antibiotic use imposes costs on people who had no part in those agricultural decisions. Day 6 showed the innovation-access gap: the scientific toolkit is richer than at any point in AMR history, yet the GARDP 2025 finding tells us that, for most of the world's carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative infections, appropriate treatment is not received not because the drugs do not exist but because the access infrastructure does not.

Understanding AMR as a collision between biological reality and human institutions — rather than as a purely scientific problem awaiting a scientific solution — is the most important conceptual shift available. It explains why genuinely mature scientific understanding of resistance mechanisms has not produced a commensurate policy response. The science is not what is missing.

### What the science has settled

Some things about antimicrobial resistance are now so well-established that they function as foundational facts, not research frontiers.

**The molecular mechanisms are understood at atomic resolution.** The four major resistance strategies — enzymatic inactivation (beta-lactamases, aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes), active efflux (pump systems that eject antibiotics before they act), target-site modification (PBP mutations, ribosomal methylation, gyrase substitutions conferring fluoroquinolone resistance), and reduced outer membrane permeability (porin loss) — are catalogued in peer-reviewed databases such as CARD (Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database at McMaster University), their genetic determinants sequenced, and their three-dimensional structures solved by X-ray crystallography and cryo-EM. The crystal structure of KPC-2 carbapenemase, NDM-1, and OXA-48 are used directly to guide inhibitor design. This is not a scientific frontier; it is the foundation on which drug development now operates.

**Horizontal gene transfer is the primary amplifier of clinical resistance spread.** The movement of resistance genes across bacterial species via conjugative plasmids, integrons, and transposons explains why a new antibiotic class can face clinical resistance within three to five years of introduction — far faster than vertical chromosomal evolution alone could account for. Epidemiological data from hospital outbreaks documenting KPC carbapenemase genes moving between *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, *E. coli*, and *Acinetobacter baumannii* within single wards are now abundant and methodologically well-supported by whole-genome sequencing.

**Antibiotic use drives resistance at the population level.** The GLASS 2025 data (Day 4) confirmed a robust, statistically significant, dose-response relationship between national antibiotic consumption volumes and national resistance rates across dozens of countries. Every additional unit of beta-lactam consumption per 1,000 inhabitant-days was associated with an 11–22% increase in cephalosporin-resistant *E. coli* and *Klebsiella pneumoniae* bloodstream infections; for fluoroquinolones, the association with fluoroquinolone-resistant isolates was even stronger (31–40%). This is the population-level counterpart of what the molecular biology shows at the cellular level: selection pressure produces selection outcomes.

**The economic market failure in antibiotic development is documented, not speculated.** The cascade from low return on investment to pharmaceutical company exits to post-approval startup bankruptcies (Achaogen's plazomicin, Melinta's portfolio) is documented in regulatory filings, company announcements, and multiple independent peer-reviewed economic analyses. Antibiotics are, structurally, a poor commercial investment under current market arrangements — and this is a fact of economics, not a failure of entrepreneurial spirit.

**Antimicrobial stewardship reduces antibiotic consumption in hospitals without worsening patient outcomes.** Multiple systematic reviews confirm that structured stewardship programmes — prospective audit and feedback, formulary restriction, diagnostic-guided prescribing — reduce total antibiotic use by 10–30% in hospital settings, and in most studies improve outcomes by reducing adverse drug effects and *Clostridioides difficile* colitis. The evidence base for hospital-based stewardship is as strong as the evidence base for many standard clinical interventions.

**New-generation beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor (BLI) combinations substantially extend the therapeutic life of the beta-lactam class against carbapenem-resistant pathogens.** The seven combinations approved since 2014 (ceftazidime-avibactam, meropenem-vaborbactam, imipenem-relebactam, ceftolozane-tazobactam, cefiderocol, sulbactam-durlobactam, and cefepime-enmetazobactam) achieve clinical cure rates of 70–80% against pathogens where the previous standard of care (colistin-based combinations) achieved 40–60%. This is incremental progress at the frontier of the hardest treatment challenge in clinical infectious disease — which makes it, in context, significant progress.

### What remains genuinely contested or open

These are questions where specialists disagree, where data are incomplete or methodologically contested, or where the science is clear but the policy implications are genuinely unresolved.

**The precise quantitative contribution of agricultural antibiotic use to human AMR burden.** It is established that the link exists and that EU livestock antibiotic bans have produced measurable reductions in food-chain resistance rates. What is not established is how large the human-attributable clinical burden from agricultural resistance actually is. Genomic epidemiology can trace specific resistance gene clusters across bacterial lineages, but resistance genes move between lineages. Estimates of the fraction of human clinical AMR deaths directly attributable to agricultural selection pressure span an order of magnitude across published studies. Agricultural producers in low- and middle-income countries argue — with some validity — that prophylactic antibiotic use is often the only economically viable disease-prevention tool available in resource-constrained settings, and that bans without affordable alternatives shift costs to food-insecure consumers.

**The role of antibiotic tolerance and persistence in clinical treatment failures.** Resistance — the heritable genetic ability to grow in the presence of an antibiotic — is distinct from tolerance (the transient ability of an entire bacterial population to survive antibiotic exposure without being killed) and persistence (the ability of a metabolically dormant bacterial subpopulation to survive killing that the rest of the population cannot withstand). A 2024 *Cell Host & Microbe* study documented that host stress responses during infection systematically induce tolerance and persistence phenotypes, providing a mechanistic explanation for why antibiotic courses that should be curative based on in vitro susceptibility testing sometimes fail in clinical practice. How much of the burden attributed to "antibiotic-resistant infections" in surveillance systems is actually attributable to tolerance or persistence — which are not captured by standard minimum inhibitory concentration testing — remains poorly quantified and is an active area of fundamental microbiological research.

**Whether phage therapy can scale from salvage therapy to mainstream clinical tool.** The ELIMINATE trial and parallel randomised studies (Day 6) provide compelling early evidence. But the questions that separate cautious optimists from sceptics are not trivial: Can phage resistance be reliably controlled in clinical settings? Can manufacturing processes be standardised to regulatory quality standards across diverse phage strains? Can clinical trial designs be built that work for inherently pathogen-specific products? Is the right regulatory model a pharmaceutical one (standardised products for defined indications) or a more bespoke model (custom cocktails designed for individual patients)? These questions will be substantially answered in the next decade as phase 3 phage therapy trials begin — but they remain open.

**The right quantum and design of pull incentives for antibiotic development.** The UK subscription model and the proposed US PASTEUR Act both correctly identify delinkage of revenue from prescription volume as the key principle. But they differ substantially in the amounts offered (approximately £5–20 million per year for the UK pilot; $75–300 million per year under PASTEUR), and independent economic analyses suggest the UK payments alone are insufficient to change investment decisions at the margin for companies not already in the antibiotic field. The debate about what payment level would actually shift industry behaviour is unresolved, because it depends on industry hurdle rates and clinical trial cost structures that are not publicly available. Separately, the EU's proposed Transferable Exclusivity Voucher mechanism — explicitly rejected by a group of 28 leading AMR economists in a 2023 *Lancet* commentary — illustrates how contentious the institutional design of pull mechanisms remains.

**Whether the 2024 UNGA target of a 10% reduction in AMR deaths by 2030 is achievable — or sufficient.** The target is the first quantitative international commitment on AMR, and its adoption represents a governance advance. But 10% of 1.27 million directly attributable deaths is approximately 127,000 lives per year. The GRAM 2024 *Lancet* analysis projects a 70% increase in AMR deaths by 2050 under a reference scenario of inadequate action — meaning a 10% reduction by 2030 would represent a modest near-term deflection of a trajectory still rising steeply in the medium term. Whether the monitoring mechanisms established by the GAP-AMR 2026–2036 (GLASS, TrACSS self-assessments, WHO pipeline reviews) have sufficient resolution and accountability to detect progress toward the target — let alone to enforce it — is unknown.

### Five big open questions

These are genuine research and policy frontiers, not rhetorical provocations.

**1. What does the AMR burden actually look like in data-poor settings?**

The GRAM 2022 and 2024 analyses are the best available estimates of global AMR mortality, but they are epidemiological models built on surveillance data concentrated in high-income countries. "Laboratory-confirmed infection" requires a functioning microbiology laboratory. A majority of serious bacterial infections in low-income settings never reach one. The GARDP 2025 Lancet Infectious Diseases finding that only 6.9% of carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative infections in eight large LMICs received appropriate treatment also means that the pathogen causing the other 93.1% of infections was often never confirmed. The true AMR burden in the highest-burden regions is almost certainly a substantial underestimate of the GRAM figures. Resolving this requires investment in laboratory infrastructure — sequencing capacity, culture equipment, trained clinical microbiologists, standardised reporting systems — that is an act of global health policy, not primarily a scientific advance.

**2. Can AMR be meaningfully controlled at the level of environmental reservoirs?**

Days 4 and 5 described how resistance genes cycle between human hospitals, agricultural environments, wastewater systems, rivers, coastal ecosystems, and back again — sustained by selection pressures in each compartment. Some studies have documented high concentrations of resistance genes in effluent from antibiotic manufacturing plants in South and Southeast Asia, in agricultural runoff, and in the sediment of coastal waters receiving hospital discharge. These are not merely passive repositories; they are active selection environments. The question of whether it is possible — biologically, technically, politically — to reduce environmental resistance gene loads at a scale that would measurably affect human clinical outcomes has not been seriously attempted. It involves wastewater engineering, antibiotic chemistry, microbial ecology, and international trade regulation, and no coherent global strategy for addressing it at scale currently exists.

**3. Will the pipeline of genuinely novel antimicrobial approaches reach patients who need them most?**

Day 6 documented real scientific progress: the ELIMINATE phage therapy trial, gepotidacin and zoliflodacin for gonorrhoea (both FDA-approved December 2025), cefiderocol and sulbactam-durlobactam for carbapenem-resistant infections. But "FDA-approved" and "available to a patient in a district hospital in Bangladesh or Kenya" are not the same thing. Each newer agent faces the same access gap that the GARDP 2025 study documented for existing drugs. Without explicit access strategies integrated into drug development from the earliest stages — as GARDP attempts to do — new treatments will reach patients in high-income settings while patients bearing the largest disease burden continue to be treated empirically with drugs that may not work for their pathogen. The access question is structurally upstream of the innovation question.

**4. How do we design effective governance for a global commons problem?**

AMR is a global commons problem: resistance genes generated by antibiotic use in one country can appear in clinical isolates in another within months via travel, trade, and ecological pathways. Global commons problems are structurally difficult to govern through institutions built on national sovereignty. The GAP-AMR 2026–2036 framework, the GLASS surveillance system, and the TrACSS self-assessment mechanism represent genuine institutional investments — but they lack enforcement mechanisms, independent verification, and dedicated funding streams comparable to the Global Fund for AIDS, Tuberculosis and Malaria. In 2024, only 10% of countries reported dedicated domestic funding for their national AMR action plans. Whether AMR can be effectively addressed through the existing multilateral health architecture, or whether it requires new institutional forms — a dedicated AMR Fund, an intergovernmental scientific panel analogous to the IPCC — is both an institutional design question and a political economy question that the scientific community cannot resolve on its own.

**5. Is there a biological ceiling on resistance evolution, and where does it lie?**

Most discussions of AMR take for granted that resistance will eventually emerge against any antibiotic deployed at sufficient scale — and the historical record strongly supports this. But the evolutionary biology of resistance has nuances that remain unresolved. Fitness costs of resistance determinants theoretically allow susceptible populations to rebound when selection pressure is removed — the basis of antibiotic cycling strategies. Whether cycling works at clinical scale is contested: some studies show resistance reduction after reduced use; others show stable persistence, suggesting compensatory mutations have eliminated the fitness cost. The question of whether tolerance and persistence can evolve into stable heritable resistance — the tolerance-to-resistance pathway that laboratory studies suggest is feasible — is an active area of research with direct clinical implications. Resolving these questions may reveal leverage points for interventions that do more than delay an otherwise inevitable outcome.

### Where to go deeper

The readings this week were necessarily selective. For readers who want to follow specific threads further:

**The molecular biology of resistance**: Start with the CARD (Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database) at <https://card.mcmaster.ca> — a curated, peer-reviewed database of over 4,700 antimicrobial resistance genes with mechanism classifications, clinical breakpoints, and links to primary literature. The 2024 CARD update paper in *Nucleic Acids Research* (Alcock et al.) describes its scope.

**The global burden**: The GRAM Collaborators' 2024 *Lancet* paper (freely available via PubMed Central, PMC11718157) is the authoritative source for the 2025–2050 forecasts; the 2022 *Lancet* paper (PIIS0140-6736(21)02724-0) provides the 2019 cross-sectional analysis. Both have freely available supplementary data.

**The economics of development and incentive design**: Årdal et al., *Nature Reviews Microbiology* (2020), and Cama et al., *ACS Infectious Diseases* (2021, PMC7931625) cover the push/pull framework comprehensively. The Wellcome Trust's "Reframing Resistance" series and the ReAct Group (<https://www.reactgroup.org>) publish accessible policy analyses bridging the academic and advocacy dimensions.

**One Health and agriculture**: The 2022 WHO/FAO/UNEP/WOAH "One Health Joint Plan of Action" is the intergovernmental framework. The *Lancet Planetary Health* and *One Health* (Elsevier) journals publish the primary research on agricultural resistance and environmental AMR.

**Phage therapy**: The *PHAGE: Therapy, Applications, and Research* journal (Mary Ann Liebert) and the *PHAGOBIOTIC Foundation* clinical database are the most comprehensive entry points to the clinical evidence base.

**Governance and policy**: The One Health Trust (<https://onehealthtrust.org>) publishes independent quantitative policy analyses. The 2016 O'Neill Review on Antimicrobial Resistance remains the most widely cited comprehensive economic treatment. The text of the 2024 UNGA Political Declaration on AMR is the current intergovernmental commitment against which all subsequent action must be measured.

## Questions to think about

1. A recurring theme across the week has been the asymmetry between the quality of the scientific knowledge and the adequacy of the institutional response. If the molecular mechanisms are understood at atomic resolution, the economic market failure is documented in bankruptcy filings, and the epidemiological burden is quantified to the level of a systematic analysis across 204 countries — then what exactly is missing? Is the bottleneck primarily political (insufficient will to fund public goods at scale), economic (insufficient willingness to redesign incentive structures), institutional (the wrong organisations are in charge), or cognitive (the public and its representatives do not viscerally feel the urgency the numbers warrant)? Does your answer suggest different strategies for change?

2. The GARDP finding that only 6.9% of patients with carbapenem-resistant infections in eight large LMICs received appropriate treatment reframes the AMR problem in an unexpected direction. For most of the world, the primary bottleneck is not resistance per se but access — to functioning laboratories, appropriate diagnostics, and drugs that are already approved but not available. If you were designing a ten-year strategy to reduce AMR mortality by 20% globally, how would you divide resources across (a) accelerating new drug development, (b) improving access to existing drugs, (c) expanding laboratory and surveillance capacity, (d) hospital stewardship, (e) agricultural AMR control, and (f) environmental AMR reduction? What evidence would you use to justify each allocation?

3. The five open questions in this synthesis span timescales from the near-term actionable (pull incentive design) to the deeply long-term (evolutionary biology of resistance ceilings). But several are interdependent: improving surveillance in LMICs is a prerequisite for quantifying the agricultural AMR contribution; understanding tolerance and persistence is a prerequisite for designing better treatment protocols; building effective international governance is a prerequisite for addressing environmental AMR at global scale. If you had to identify the single constraint whose resolution would most unlock progress on the others — a kind of Archimedean lever for the whole AMR problem — which would it be, and why?
