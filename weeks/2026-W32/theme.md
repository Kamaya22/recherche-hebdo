# Week 2026-W32 — Antibiotic resistance

## Why it matters

Antibiotics are the scaffolding on which modern medicine rests. They make surgery safe by preventing post-operative infections, allow us to treat pneumonias that once killed young adults, protect immunocompromised patients receiving cancer chemotherapy or organ transplants, and underlie the routine treatment of bloodstream infections that were once near-certain death sentences. The discovery of penicillin in 1928 and the "golden age" of antibiotic discovery between the 1940s and 1960s transformed medicine — and bacteria have been evolving counter-strategies ever since. In 2019, bacterial antimicrobial resistance (AMR) directly killed an estimated 1.27 million people and was associated with nearly 5 million further deaths, making it a larger direct cause of death than HIV/AIDS or malaria that year. A 2024 analysis in *The Lancet* projects that without coordinated action, more than 39 million people will die directly from antibiotic-resistant infections between 2025 and 2050.

The crisis has two intertwined dimensions. First, bacteria continuously evolve resistance to every antibiotic class humans have developed — through mechanisms ranging from enzymes that dismantle drug molecules to molecular pumps that eject antibiotics before they can act, and crucially, through horizontal gene transfer that allows resistance to jump between species far faster than vertical evolution. Second, the innovation pipeline that once produced new drugs has nearly dried up: antibiotic discovery slowed dramatically after the 1970s, and the economics of drug development — short treatment courses, pressure to keep new drugs in reserve, rapid resistance emergence — led most pharmaceutical companies to abandon the field. The WHO's 2023 clinical pipeline review counted only 97 agents in development, with few representing truly novel mechanisms.

Understanding AMR requires fluency across molecular biology, infectious disease epidemiology, agriculture, economics, and global governance. It is precisely this breadth that makes it one of the most important and under-appreciated scientific challenges of our time.

## Tentative 7-day outline

- **Day 1 (Monday): Panorama** — What antibiotics are, how they work, what resistance means, and the scale of the global crisis.
- **Day 2 (Tuesday): Mechanisms of resistance** — The four main molecular strategies bacteria use to survive antibiotics: enzymatic inactivation, efflux pumps, target site modification, and reduced membrane permeability.
- **Day 3 (Wednesday): The discovery pipeline crisis** — Why the golden age of antibiotic discovery ended, the economics that make antibiotics a poor investment, and what the WHO's pipeline audit reveals about the innovation gap.
- **Day 4 (Thursday): Surveillance and the global resistance landscape** — How AMR is tracked (WHO GLASS), the ESKAPE priority pathogens, geographic hot spots, and what the data show about trends.
- **Day 5 (Friday): One Health — the wider ecology of resistance** — How antibiotic use in agriculture, livestock, aquaculture, and the environment selects for resistance that then reaches humans, and why this demands cross-sector policy.
- **Day 6 (Saturday): Beyond conventional antibiotics** — Phage therapy, antibiotic adjuvants, antimicrobial peptides, novel drug classes, stewardship programmes, and new economic models for funding R&D.
- **Day 7 (Sunday): Synthesis** — What is scientifically settled versus actively debated; the five most important open questions; how to go deeper.
