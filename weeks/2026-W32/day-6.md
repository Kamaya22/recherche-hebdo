# Antibiotic resistance — Day 6/7: Beyond Conventional Antibiotics

## Context (2-3 paragraphs)

This week has documented a crisis of compounding dimensions. Days 1 and 4 measured its scale: 1.27 million deaths directly attributable to antimicrobial resistance in 2019, rising to a projected 1.91 million per year by 2050, with the heaviest burden falling in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. Days 2 and 3 explained why it persists: bacteria evolve resistance to every drug class through an arsenal of molecular strategies — enzymatic inactivation, efflux pumps, target-site modification, membrane impermeability — and the pharmaceutical industry has been unable to keep pace because antibiotics are structurally poor investments. Day 5 widened the lens to show that one Health matters: a substantial fraction of clinically relevant resistance originates in agriculture and the environment, not just hospitals, and reaches humans via food, water, and ecological pathways. If today's reading is to be the most forward-looking of the week, it must answer the question that follows from all of this: if conventional antibiotic discovery is in long-term decline, and if resistance continues to accumulate faster than new drugs reach the clinic, what is actually being done about it beyond the standard antibiotic development model?

The answer is a diversified toolkit of approaches that do not fit the classical definition of "antibiotics" — molecules derived from microorganisms or chemistry that inhibit bacterial growth — but that can either complement or substitute for them. These fall into four rough categories. **Phage therapy** uses viruses that specifically infect and kill bacteria, an approach with a century-long scientific history that largely fell out of favour in the West when antibiotics arrived in the 1940s but is now being rigorously re-evaluated with modern methods. **Antibiotic adjuvants** — most prominently beta-lactamase inhibitors — do not kill bacteria themselves but restore the activity of existing antibiotics that bacteria have evolved to destroy; several new-generation adjuvants approved since 2014 have dramatically expanded the useful lifespan of old drug scaffolds. **Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs)** are short chains of amino acids — many naturally produced by the immune systems of animals, plants, and microbes — that kill bacteria by disrupting their membranes, with mechanistic features that make resistance evolution slower than to most conventional antibiotics. And a handful of genuinely **novel small-molecule drug classes** have recently entered or completed clinical development, targeting bacterial enzymes through mechanisms distinct from every existing antibiotic family.

Today's reading examines the clinical evidence for these approaches, their limitations, and the institutional infrastructure — antimicrobial stewardship programmes and new economic models — that is being built to ensure the alternatives that do reach the clinic are used wisely and that future developers can recoup their investments. This is the most solutions-oriented day of the week: not a reason for optimism exactly, but a reason to understand that the AMR challenge is being engaged on multiple fronts simultaneously, with a more sophisticated set of tools than existed even ten years ago.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

### The limits of the conventional playbook — and why alternatives matter now

Day 3 of this week established that the conventional model of antibiotic discovery — chemical synthesis or screening, followed by randomised controlled trials proving efficacy against a defined indication, followed by FDA or EMA approval, followed by commercial launch — is structurally broken as a business proposition. The return on investment is too low, the time to market is too long, the stewardship imperative deliberately caps sales volumes, and bankruptcy after approval has become an almost routine outcome for small biotech companies that persevere regardless. But the conventional model has another limitation that Day 3 did not emphasise: it is iterating within a constrained space. Beta-lactams, fluoroquinolones, glycopeptides, aminoglycosides — these are all members of antibiotic classes whose core scaffolds were discovered before 1970. Even the most innovative recent approvals (gepotidacin, zoliflodacin) represent new chemical series targeting bacterial topoisomerases, an enzyme class that has been the target of fluoroquinolones since the 1960s. Bacteria that have already evolved resistance to fluoroquinolones via target-site mutations can remain susceptible to gepotidacin because it binds at a distinct allosteric site — a genuine advance — but the underlying enzyme target is familiar. Truly novel mechanisms of action directed at bacterial biology untouched by existing drugs have been extraordinarily rare in the clinical pipeline. This is precisely why the alternatives examined today matter: they represent qualitatively different approaches, not just chemical refinements of existing scaffolds.

### Phage therapy: a century-old idea made rigorous

Bacteriophages — viruses that infect and kill bacteria — were discovered in the early twentieth century, predating antibiotics, and were used clinically in Eastern Europe and the Soviet Union throughout the twentieth century even as they were largely abandoned in the West after penicillin's arrival. The scientific reasons for Western scepticism were real: pre-antibiotic phage preparations were poorly characterised, trials were uncontrolled, and the immune system can neutralise phages, limiting their efficacy in some patients. What has changed over the past decade is the combination of genomics, synthetic biology, and rigorous clinical methodology that has made it possible to design phage cocktails precisely, engineer them to broaden their bacterial host range, and test them in randomised controlled trials.

The ELIMINATE trial is the most methodologically rigorous test of this approach published to date. LBP-EC01 is not a natural phage cocktail: each of its four phages has been modified using CRISPR-Cas3, a bacterial immune system repurposed as a genetic engineering tool that degrades the bacterial chromosome on entry rather than simply lysing the cell — a mechanism expected to reduce the emergence of phage-resistant bacterial mutants. The trial enrolled adult women with uncomplicated urinary tract infections and a documented history of drug-resistant *E. coli* infections, a population whose need for alternatives to antibiotics is both medically real and commercially legible. The results — 87.5% microbiological cure at Day 10, complete symptom resolution in all evaluable patients — are striking for a phase 2 trial in this indication. Critically, the safety profile showed no serious adverse events attributable to the phage therapy itself.

Parallel trials have extended the picture. A first-in-human randomised phase 1b/2a trial published in *Nature Communications* in 2025 tested BX004-A, a three-phage cocktail delivered by nebulisation for chronic *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* infections in cystic fibrosis patients, showing a 2.7 log₁₀ reduction in sputum bacterial burden (approximately 500-fold) compared to placebo with preserved healthy airway microbiome. A separate phase 2 randomised trial of BX211 for *Staphylococcus aureus* diabetic foot osteomyelitis demonstrated clinically significant reduction in ulcer surface area. These three trials span very different infection types, administration routes (intravenous/systemic, nebulised, topical), and pathogens — which is significant, because it suggests phage therapy's promise is not confined to a single niche.

What phage therapy cannot yet do is compete with antibiotics on breadth, simplicity, or scalability. Each phage product is specific to a narrow bacterial host range; treating an unknown infection requires diagnostic identification of the pathogen before phage selection can begin, demanding more sophisticated diagnostic infrastructure than most clinical settings have. Manufacturing, quality control, and regulatory pathways for phage products remain under active development: in 2024, the European Pharmacopoeia published a new chapter (5.31) specifically addressing phage therapy medicinal products, and the EMA and FDA have been engaged in ongoing consultations since late 2024. Phage therapy for now is best understood as a potential rescue therapy for drug-resistant infections where no antibiotic options remain — a role that is urgent and real, but not a replacement for broad-spectrum antibiotics in first-line treatment.

### Antibiotic adjuvants: saving the arsenal we already have

While phage therapy represents a departure from the antibiotic model, beta-lactamase inhibitors represent its opposite — a strategy of preserving the existing antibiotic arsenal by neutralising the resistance mechanisms bacteria have evolved against it. The logic is elegant. Beta-lactam antibiotics work by blocking the enzyme that cross-links the bacterial cell wall. Beta-lactamases are enzymes bacteria produce that destroy the beta-lactam ring, rendering the antibiotic inactive. A beta-lactamase inhibitor does not kill bacteria itself; it binds the beta-lactamase irreversibly (or with very high affinity), allowing the co-administered beta-lactam to reach its target unimpeded. The first-generation inhibitors — clavulanate (combined with amoxicillin as Augmentin, approved 1981), sulbactam, and tazobactam — were highly effective against the class A serine beta-lactamases that dominated clinical resistance in the 1980s and 1990s.

The crisis that required new-generation inhibitors was the emergence of extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs) and, most urgently, carbapenemases — enzymes capable of destroying the carbapenem antibiotics that had served as the last-resort backbone of Gram-negative infection treatment. The new inhibitors approved since 2014 address these threats through distinct chemical approaches. Avibactam (combined with ceftazidime, approved 2015) is a diazabicyclooctanone compound that inhibits class A and class C serine beta-lactamases, including the KPC carbapenemases that have spread through *Klebsiella pneumoniae* worldwide and the OXA-48 enzymes common in Mediterranean countries. Vaborbactam (with meropenem) targets the same enzyme classes through a cyclic boronate mechanism. Relebactam (with imipenem-cilastatin, approved 2019) adds coverage for *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* producing OprD-deficient strains. The most recent additions — durlobactam (with sulbactam, approved 2023 specifically for carbapenem-resistant *Acinetobacter baumannii*, the WHO's #1 critical-priority pathogen) and enmetazobactam (with cefepime, approved 2024) — extend the toolkit further. What none of these inhibitors can address are class B metallo-beta-lactamases (MBLs), enzymes containing zinc in their active site whose mechanism is fundamentally different: aztreonam-avibactam (approved 2024) partially bridges this gap by pairing a monobactam insensitive to MBLs with avibactam to block any co-produced serine beta-lactamases.

The clinical significance of this new adjuvant generation is substantial. For carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales producing KPC — historically one of the highest-mortality hospital-acquired infection syndromes — ceftazidime-avibactam has produced clinical cure rates of around 70–80% in observational studies and randomised trials where earlier salvage therapies (such as colistin-based combinations) achieved 40–60%. The arrival of sulbactam-durlobactam for CRAb is particularly significant in context of this week's readings: Day 4 documented CRAb as the WHO's top-priority pathogen, with pan-resistance increasingly common in intensive care units across Southern Europe, the Middle East, and Asia. Having even one reliably active agent changes the clinical management of a previously near-untreatable condition.

### Novel small-molecule drug classes: rare but real progress

Despite the pipeline pessimism documented on Day 3, a handful of genuinely new mechanisms have reached approval. Cefiderocol, approved by the FDA in 2019, uses an iron-chelating siderophore side chain to hijack the active iron-uptake systems bacteria use to sequester iron from the environment — piggybacking into the bacterial cell and evading efflux pump resistance in ways that standard cephalosporins cannot. Gepotidacin (Blujepa), approved by the FDA in March 2025, belongs to the triazaacenaphthylene class, the first genuinely new topoisomerase-inhibitor scaffold since fluoroquinolones, and binds bacterial type II topoisomerases at a novel site that confers activity against fluoroquinolone-resistant strains. Its phase 3 EAGLE-2 and EAGLE-3 trials demonstrated non-inferiority and superiority respectively against nitrofurantoin for uncomplicated urinary tract infections. Zoliflodacin (Nuzolvence), approved in December 2025, is a spiropyrimidinetrione antibiotic targeting gonorrhoeal DNA gyrase through yet another distinct mechanism, offering an oral single-dose treatment for gonorrhoea at a time when *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* resistance to fluoroquinolones exceeds 50% globally. These approvals — of new chemical entities targeting bacterial enzymes via mechanisms distinct from all existing drug classes — are genuine breakthroughs even if their initial indications are narrow. They demonstrate that the biological target space for antibiotics is not exhausted, and that properly funded discovery programmes can still find it.

### Antimicrobial peptides: biology's own defence system as a drug candidate

Antimicrobial peptides represent a different paradigm again. Rather than targeting a specific bacterial enzyme or biosynthetic pathway, most AMPs act by inserting into and disrupting the bacterial membrane — the phospholipid bilayer that is structurally essential and phylogenetically conserved across almost all bacteria. Because the mechanism targets the physical integrity of the membrane rather than a specific protein, bacteria face a harder evolutionary path to resistance: any mutation that alters the membrane sufficiently to prevent AMP insertion tends also to compromise membrane stability, imposing a steep fitness cost. This is why, despite billions of years of coevolution between AMPs and bacteria in nature, resistance to natural AMPs remains relatively uncommon compared to resistance to enzyme-targeting antibiotics.

The clinical pipeline contains several synthetic AMPs in phase 1 and 2 trials. The obstacles to clinical translation have been consistent across the class: AMPs are typically degraded by human proteases in the gastrointestinal tract, making oral delivery extremely difficult; intravenous delivery can produce kidney toxicity or immune activation; and manufacturing costs for peptide synthesis are substantially higher than for small-molecule antibiotics. Chemical modifications — cyclisation to resist protease digestion, lipid conjugation to improve membrane affinity, D-amino acid substitution to evade bacterial proteases — have partially addressed these limitations in specific candidates. The review in *Nature Reviews Microbiology* (2025) provides the most current map of where the class stands: several candidates have demonstrated antibacterial activity in phase 2 trials against skin and soft tissue infections and urinary tract infections, but none has yet reached phase 3 with a high probability of approval across a broad indication.

### Antimicrobial stewardship: making the alternatives last

None of the alternatives discussed above will remain useful indefinitely if deployed carelessly. Bacteria can evolve resistance to phages (via receptor mutation or anti-CRISPR proteins), to beta-lactamase inhibitor combinations (via secondary mutations that alter binding affinity), to novel small molecules (as has already been documented for gepotidacin against *E. coli* in in vitro studies), and theoretically to AMPs (though more slowly). This is precisely why antimicrobial stewardship programmes — structured institutional approaches to ensuring antibiotics and their alternatives are used appropriately, at the right dose, for the right duration, for the right pathogen — are the essential complement to any new therapy that reaches approval.

Stewardship programmes in hospitals typically combine several interventions: prospective audit and feedback (infectious disease specialists reviewing inpatient antibiotic prescriptions and recommending changes where evidence suggests de-escalation or an alternative), formulary restriction and pre-authorisation for last-resort agents, diagnostic-guided prescribing (using rapid diagnostics to narrow treatment to the confirmed pathogen rather than treating empirically), and education programmes for prescribers. A 2025 Nature Reviews Microbiology review on stewardship from a One Health perspective synthesises evidence from high- and low-income settings alike, finding that the combination of these interventions reliably reduces total antibiotic consumption by 10–30% in hospital settings without increasing adverse patient outcomes — and in most studies, reducing inappropriate prescribing actually improves outcomes by reducing adverse drug effects and *Clostridioides difficile* colitis. In low-income settings, the challenges are different: the primary risk is insufficient access to effective antibiotics (patients dying because they cannot afford or access the right drug), not overuse, and stewardship frameworks must be calibrated accordingly.

### Economic models: connecting the solutions to the market failure

Day 3 explained in detail why the conventional antibiotic business model fails. The tools emerging from Day 6's toolkit — engineered phages, next-generation BLI combinations, novel small-molecule classes — all face versions of the same problem: development is expensive, clinical trials for resistant pathogens are slow and costly, and stewardship principles deliberately limit the sales volumes that could recoup those costs. The policy response has been to develop "pull incentives" — payment mechanisms that delink revenue from volume by paying pharmaceutical developers for the *availability* of a valuable drug rather than for the number of prescriptions written. The UK's NHS England subscription model, piloted with ceftazidime-avibactam and cefiderocol in 2022, was expanded in 2024 with an annual budget of £100 million available for new subscription contracts covering additional antimicrobial products — each contract paying a fixed annual fee of £5 million to £20 million regardless of how much of the drug is used. In February 2026, the United States Congress reintroduced PASTEUR v2.0 (the Pioneering Antimicrobial Subscriptions To End Upsurging Resistance Act), the proposed subscription model that would pay eligible developers $75 million to $300 million per year for access to critically needed new antimicrobials. These mechanisms are designed to work equally well for conventional antibiotics, phage products, AMP-based drugs, and adjuvant combinations — the payment is for value to public health, not for chemical category.

## Questions to think about

1. Phage therapy is pathogen-specific: each phage cocktail targets a narrow bacterial host range. This specificity is a biological advantage (less disruption to the microbiome than broad-spectrum antibiotics) but a clinical disadvantage (you need a confirmed pathogen ID before selecting the right phage). Rapid diagnostic testing — culture-independent tools that identify a pathogen and its resistance profile in hours rather than days — is therefore a prerequisite for phage therapy at scale. Given that Day 4 showed diagnostic capacity is most limited in the countries bearing the highest AMR burden, what does the phage therapy opportunity actually look like for low-income settings? Is it a tool for high-income hospitals only, or can the technology be made available more broadly?

2. Beta-lactamase inhibitor combinations have, on multiple occasions, extended the clinical life of a drug class that was on the verge of being rendered obsolete by resistance — but each new generation of inhibitor has in turn driven the selection of bacteria carrying mutations that evade it. Avibactam-resistant KPC variants carrying secondary mutations at the KPC active site have already been documented in clinical isolates from patients who received ceftazidime-avibactam. Is the beta-lactam/BLI strategy a fundamentally sustainable approach, or is it a series of moves in an evolutionary arms race that bacteria will eventually win regardless? What would it mean to "win" versus "indefinitely delay losing" in this context?

3. Antimicrobial stewardship programmes have the strongest evidence base in high-income hospital settings. Their expansion to primary care (where the majority of antibiotic prescriptions are written globally), to veterinary and agricultural settings (as explored on Day 5), and to low-income health systems with limited diagnostic and prescribing infrastructure represents a fundamentally different challenge. If you were designing a stewardship intervention for a district hospital in West Africa with limited microbiology laboratory capacity and a high burden of antimicrobial-resistant sepsis, which elements of the hospital-based stewardship model would translate directly, and which would need to be redesigned from the ground up?
