# Antibiotic resistance — Day 2/7: How Bacteria Fight Back — The Four Molecular Strategies

## Context (2–3 paragraphs)

Day 1 established the scale of the antibiotic resistance crisis: 1.27 million deaths directly attributable to resistant bacteria in 2019, with projections reaching nearly two million per year by 2050. It also introduced the key actors — beta-lactams, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, and the rest — and explained how natural selection and horizontal gene transfer allow resistance to spread far faster than our intuitions about evolution might suggest. Today's reading goes one level deeper, into the molecular machinery that bacteria actually deploy to neutralise or evade antibiotics. Understanding this machinery matters for two reasons: it explains why resistance is so difficult to outpace through drug development, and it frames the logic behind the alternative strategies (beta-lactamase inhibitors, efflux pump inhibitors, novel drug targets) that Day 6 will explore.

Bacteriologists group the molecular strategies of resistance into four broad categories: *enzymatic inactivation* of the antibiotic molecule itself; *active efflux* of the drug before it can reach its target; *modification of the target site* so the drug no longer binds effectively; and *reduced membrane permeability* that keeps drugs from entering the cell in the first place. In practice, these categories are not exclusive — a clinical isolate of carbapenem-resistant *Klebsiella pneumoniae* commonly deploys all four simultaneously, which is one reason last-resort treatments can fail even when the pathogen is susceptible in isolation to two or three individual mechanisms. The synergy between mechanisms is as important as any single mechanism.

Today's readings focus on the molecular details of each strategy. The primary source — the 2023 *Nature Reviews Microbiology* update by Darby and colleagues — is the current gold-standard reference, updating a landmark 2014 review with nearly a decade of new structural biology, genomics, and clinical surveillance. The second source returns to the Mechanisms section of the *Lancet Microbe* concise update introduced on Day 1, which provides a more clinically oriented perspective on the same four strategies and explicitly links them to the priority pathogens on the WHO's list.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

### Setting the stage: intrinsic versus acquired resistance

Before exploring the four mechanisms, one distinction is worth establishing. *Intrinsic resistance* refers to characteristics that are always present in a bacterial species — the physical absence of a target, a structural feature of the cell that limits drug access, or the constitutive expression of efflux machinery. *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, for example, is intrinsically resistant to many penicillins because its outer membrane porin OprD has a very narrow channel that excludes large lipophilic molecules, and because it constitutively expresses efflux pumps with broad substrate specificity. Intrinsic resistance explains why certain drugs simply cannot be used against certain organisms regardless of clinical resistance pressure. *Acquired resistance*, by contrast, is the result of genetic change — either a mutation in the bacterium's own chromosome, or the uptake of a resistance gene from another organism via horizontal gene transfer. The four mechanisms described below can arise through either route, though some (especially enzymatic inactivation) are almost always acquired through gene transfer rather than de novo mutation.

### Mechanism 1 — Enzymatic inactivation: destroying or modifying the antibiotic molecule

The most clinically consequential form of enzymatic inactivation is the hydrolysis of beta-lactam antibiotics by *beta-lactamases* — enzymes that catalyse the opening of the beta-lactam ring, which is the functional core of the molecule and essential for binding to penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs). Beta-lactamases are classified into four molecular classes (A through D) based on their amino acid sequence and catalytic mechanism, and into two functional groups based on whether they use a serine residue or zinc ions in their active site.

*Serine beta-lactamases* (Classes A, C, and D) form a covalent acyl-enzyme intermediate with the beta-lactam, then hydrolyse it, releasing an inactive product and regenerating the active enzyme. *Metallo-beta-lactamases* (Class B) use one or two zinc ions to activate a water molecule that directly attacks the carbonyl carbon of the beta-lactam ring — a mechanistically distinct route that is important clinically because the classical beta-lactamase inhibitors (clavulanate, sulbactam, tazobactam) that block serine beta-lactamases by forming irreversible acyl intermediates do not work against MBLs.

Within Class A, the most important clinical subgroups are:
- **Extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs)** — primarily CTX-M enzymes, which emerged in clinical settings in the late 1980s and are now the dominant mechanism of third-generation cephalosporin resistance in *Escherichia coli* and *Klebsiella pneumoniae* worldwide. CTX-M-15 is the single most prevalent ESBL globally, encoded on highly mobile conjugative plasmids.
- **KPC (Klebsiella pneumoniae carbapenemase)** — first detected in North Carolina in 1996, KPC confers resistance to all beta-lactams including carbapenems, making it one of the most dangerous resistance mechanisms to reach clinical practice. KPC-producing *K. pneumoniae* have caused endemic outbreaks in the United States, Italy, Greece, and China, and are now found on all continents.

Within Class B (metallo-beta-lactamases), the most prominent are:
- **NDM-1 (New Delhi Metallo-beta-lactamase)** — first identified in 2009 in a Swedish patient who had received medical care in India. NDM-1 hydrolyses virtually all beta-lactams including carbapenems, leaving only the monobactam aztreonam unaffected (since monobactams lack the bicyclic ring structure MBLs recognise) — though NDM-producing isolates often acquire additional resistance to aztreonam through ESBLs or AmpC enzymes, closing even this gap. NDM-encoding plasmids have spread with remarkable speed and now circulate in Enterobacterales, *Acinetobacter*, and *Pseudomonas* globally.
- **VIM (Verona Integron-encoded Metallo-beta-lactamase)** and **IMP** — widely distributed in *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* and *Acinetobacter baumannii*, often encoded on integrons (mobile elements that can capture and assemble multiple resistance gene cassettes into a single package).

Among Class D, **OXA-48** deserves particular mention. OXA-48 is an oxacillinase with modest carbapenemase activity — alone, it raises carbapenem MICs moderately — but in combination with porin loss (mechanism 4 below) or co-expression of an ESBL, it can confer full carbapenem resistance. OXA-48 is the dominant carbapenemase in the Mediterranean, the Middle East, and South Asia.

Beyond beta-lactamases, other important enzymatic mechanisms include *aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes* (AMEs) — acetyltransferases, phosphotransferases, and nucleotidyltransferases that chemically modify hydroxyl or amine groups on aminoglycoside molecules, reducing their affinity for the 30S ribosomal subunit by orders of magnitude. AMEs are extremely diverse (over 100 have been characterised) and are frequently carried on the same plasmids as beta-lactamase genes, generating co-resistance to two major antibiotic classes in a single horizontal transfer event.

### Mechanism 2 — Active efflux: pumping the drug out before it can act

Every antibiotic must reach its target inside or on the bacterial cell at a sufficient concentration to be effective. Efflux pumps are transmembrane proteins that actively export antibiotics out of the cell against the concentration gradient, powered by the proton motive force (in most cases) or by ATP hydrolysis. By reducing intracellular drug concentrations below the threshold needed to inhibit the target, efflux pumps reduce drug efficacy without permanently modifying the antibiotic or the target.

Five superfamilies of efflux pumps are recognised in bacteria, but the **RND (Resistance-Nodulation-Division) family** is by far the most clinically important, particularly in Gram-negative bacteria. RND pumps are architecturally remarkable: they are tri-partite protein complexes that span both the inner membrane and the outer membrane, with a periplasmic adaptor connecting the two. This architecture allows them to export drugs directly into the extracellular space, preventing drug re-entry across the outer membrane. The three components — inner membrane transporter, periplasmic adaptor, and outer membrane channel — assemble into a tunnel through which antibiotics are transported.

The canonical example is **AcrAB-TolC** in *E. coli*: AcrB (the inner membrane transporter that actively captures substrate) + AcrA (the periplasmic adaptor) + TolC (the outer membrane channel). Cryo-EM studies have revealed how AcrB rotates through three sequential conformational states — loose, tight, and open — capturing substrate, compressing it through a hydrophobic binding pocket, and extruding it into the TolC tunnel. This alternating-access mechanism, powered by proton flux, can expel tetracyclines, macrolides, chloramphenicol, beta-lactams, and fluoroquinolones from a single pump. Critically, AcrB can export structurally diverse molecules simultaneously because its substrate-binding pocket is large and promiscuous — binding many different chemical scaffolds. This is why overexpression of a single RND efflux pump produces multidrug resistance rather than resistance to a single class.

In *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* — the paradigmatic multidrug-resistant Gram-negative — four RND systems are particularly important:
- **MexAB-OprM**: constitutively expressed; responsible for intrinsic resistance to beta-lactams, fluoroquinolones, and macrolides
- **MexCD-OprJ**: induced by certain membrane-active compounds
- **MexEF-OprN**: induced by chloramphenicol; linked to carbapenem resistance
- **MexXY-OprM**: induced by aminoglycosides; responsible for acquired aminoglycoside resistance

*Acinetobacter baumannii* relies heavily on AdeABC (RND), AdeFGH, and AdeIJK — combinations of which produce the broadly resistant phenotype seen in hospital outbreaks.

The **MFS (Major Facilitator Superfamily)** is the largest transporter family in bacteria and is important for tetracycline resistance via TetA and TetB pumps in Gram-negatives, and for fluoroquinolone and beta-lactam resistance via NorA in *S. aureus*. Unlike RND pumps, most MFS pumps are single-component (spanning only the inner membrane), limiting drugs to the periplasm rather than extruding them completely. This distinction is one reason MFS-mediated resistance in Gram-positive bacteria (which lack an outer membrane) operates differently from RND-mediated resistance in Gram-negatives.

Efflux pump expression is typically regulated by global transcriptional regulators. In *E. coli*, **MarA** (the multiple antibiotic resistance regulator) activates *acrB* transcription in response to salicylate, tetracycline, and other chemical cues — explaining the counterintuitive observation that subtherapeutic doses of antibiotic can actually induce broad efflux-mediated resistance to multiple drug classes. Clinically, efflux pump inhibitors (EPIs) that block pump function have attracted research interest as antibiotic adjuvants — a topic Day 6 will address.

### Mechanism 3 — Target site modification: making the drug's binding site unrecognisable

The third strategy alters the molecular target so that the antibiotic can no longer bind effectively, while the target retains enough function to carry out its normal role. This requires a more specific evolutionary solution than efflux or enzymatic inactivation, but once evolved, target modification can confer very high levels of resistance.

**MRSA and PBP2a** is the best-known example. Beta-lactams work by covalently binding to penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) — the enzymes that cross-link peptidoglycan strands during cell wall synthesis — forming an irreversible acyl-enzyme complex that permanently disables the target. Methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus* (MRSA) acquired the *mecA* gene (likely from a commensal staphylococcal species) encoding an alternative PBP, called PBP2a. PBP2a's active-site geometry is profoundly different from standard PBPs: the active site is physically obstructed when no substrate is present, and opens to accept the peptidoglycan substrate through an allosteric conformational change. Beta-lactams cannot trigger this conformational change and are therefore excluded from the active site — MRSA's PBP2a remains functional even in concentrations of methicillin, oxacillin, or virtually any penicillin or cephalosporin that would be far above the MIC for susceptible *S. aureus*. Since PBP2a can substitute for all essential PBPs, MRSA can synthesise cell walls normally even when its natural PBPs are completely inactivated by beta-lactams. The *mecA* gene is carried on the staphylococcal cassette chromosome *mec* (SCC*mec*), a mobile genetic element that integrates site-specifically into the *S. aureus* chromosome.

**Ribosomal modifications** are important for resistance to macrolides, aminoglycosides, and linezolid. Macrolides (erythromycin, azithromycin, clarithromycin) bind to the 50S ribosomal subunit, blocking the peptide exit tunnel. *Erm* (Erythromycin Ribosome Methylation) methyltransferases methylate a specific adenine residue in the 23S rRNA component of the 50S subunit, causing steric clashes that prevent macrolide binding. Critically, this single methylation confers resistance not just to macrolides, but to lincosamides (clindamycin) and streptogramin B antibiotics as well — the so-called MLS_B phenotype. *Erm* genes are among the most widespread resistance determinants in the world, present in staphylococci, streptococci, enterococci, and anaerobes. High-level aminoglycoside resistance can similarly arise through methylation of the 16S rRNA by *armA* or *rmt* methyltransferases — acquired genes that completely prevent aminoglycoside binding to the 30S subunit. These 16S rRNA methylases confer resistance to virtually all clinically used aminoglycosides simultaneously, including the newer drugs amikacin and plazomicin, and they are increasingly found on plasmids co-carrying carbapenemase genes.

**Fluoroquinolone resistance via gyrase and topoisomerase mutations** is one of the clearest examples of stepwise resistance acquisition. Fluoroquinolones inhibit two type II topoisomerases — DNA gyrase (the primary target in Gram-negatives) and topoisomerase IV (the primary target in Gram-positives) — that introduce and relieve supercoiling during DNA replication. Resistance typically accumulates through sequential point mutations in the *gyrA* gene (encoding the A subunit of gyrase) and the *parC* gene (encoding the A subunit of topoisomerase IV). The most common mutations are at codon 83 (Ser83→Leu or Phe) and codon 87 (Asp87→Asn or Gly) of *gyrA*. Each individual mutation reduces fluoroquinolone binding affinity by about 10-fold; combinations of two *gyrA* mutations plus a *parC* mutation reduce it by 1,000-fold or more, converting a susceptible organism to one with high-level clinical resistance. This stepwise mechanism — each mutation slightly reducing killing efficiency, allowing survival and further mutation under continued drug pressure — is precisely the pattern that makes subtherapeutic dosing and incomplete treatment courses dangerous: they provide enough selective pressure to select for resistance while allowing the bacterium to survive.

**Vancomycin resistance via *van* operons** is perhaps the most elegantly evolved of all target site modifications. Vancomycin does not bind to a protein enzyme but to the terminal D-Ala–D-Ala dipeptide of the peptidoglycan precursor. *VanA* and *VanB* gene clusters, acquired through horizontal transfer from inherently resistant environmental bacteria, encode an enzyme pathway that replaces the terminal D-Ala with D-Lac (lactate) — a chemically similar molecule but one that reduces vancomycin's binding affinity by approximately 1,000-fold. Vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE) — carrying *vanA* on the transposon Tn1546, often on conjugative plasmids — are now endemic in hospital settings in many countries, and the occasional emergence of vancomycin-resistant *S. aureus* (VRSA), which acquired *vanA* directly from VRE by conjugation, represents one of the most feared events in clinical microbiology.

### Mechanism 4 — Reduced membrane permeability: shutting the gates

The final mechanism operates at the level of drug entry rather than drug activity or target binding. Gram-negative bacteria possess an outer membrane — a second lipid bilayer surrounding the cell wall — that acts as a permeability barrier against many antibiotics, particularly hydrophilic molecules that cannot diffuse through lipids. Hydrophilic antibiotics (most beta-lactams, aminoglycosides) enter Gram-negative bacteria primarily through *porins* — protein channels embedded in the outer membrane that regulate the passage of small molecules. Reducing the number, size, or selectivity of porins therefore restricts antibiotic entry.

In *E. coli*, the two main porins are **OmpF** (a broader, less selective channel) and **OmpC** (a narrower channel preferentially expressed in high-osmolarity environments). Downregulation of OmpF — often triggered by *marA* activation in response to antibiotic stress — reduces intracellular concentrations of beta-lactams and fluoroquinolones below inhibitory levels, particularly when combined with efflux overexpression. The synergy is important: if efflux limits drug accumulation by a factor of 10 and porin loss reduces influx by another factor of 10, the net intracellular drug concentration drops 100-fold without either mechanism individually being sufficient for clinical resistance.

In *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, the equivalent porins are **OmpK35** and **OmpK36**. Loss of OmpK36 — through frameshifts, insertions, or promoter mutations — is particularly important because it is frequently found in clinical isolates co-producing KPC or OXA-48 carbapenemases. When KPC-producing *K. pneumoniae* additionally loses OmpK35 and OmpK36, carbapenem resistance becomes extremely difficult to treat: even drugs like meropenem that are not well hydrolysed by KPC at usual expression levels can fail because so little drug enters the cell.

In *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, the specific porin **OprD** is the primary entry route for carbapenems (imipenem and meropenem). OprD loss — through mutations, insertions, or transcriptional repression — regularly emerges during carbapenem treatment in patients with chronic *P. aeruginosa* colonisation (notably cystic fibrosis patients), and accounts for a substantial proportion of imipenem resistance in clinical isolates that do not produce carbapenemases. Because OprD is relatively specific for carbapenems (and structurally similar molecules), its loss produces resistance to carbapenems but not to most other antibiotic classes — a more targeted modification than efflux overexpression.

### Why combination matters: the arithmetic of stacked mechanisms

A recurring theme in clinical microbiology is that the most difficult-to-treat isolates combine mechanisms. Consider a real-world example from surveillance data: a carbapenem-resistant *K. pneumoniae* isolate that (1) produces KPC-3 (enzymatic inactivation); (2) overexpresses the AcrAB-TolC efflux system (active efflux); (3) has lost OmpK35 and OmpK36 (reduced permeability); and (4) carries mutations in the quinolone resistance-determining region of *gyrA* and *parC* (target site modification for fluoroquinolones). Each mechanism individually would be manageable with alternative antibiotic classes; together, they leave clinicians with very few options — often only the newest drugs (ceftazidime-avibactam, cefiderocol) or experimental combination regimens.

This is the clinical reality that the numbers in Day 1 describe: 1.27 million directly attributable deaths in 2019, concentrated in infections caused by six priority pathogens that have collectively accumulated multiple resistance mechanisms against most or all available antibiotic classes. The molecular biology today describes is not academic — it is the mechanistic substrate of those deaths. Days 3 through 6 will explore why the pipeline of new drugs to outpace these mechanisms has nearly stalled, how surveillance tracks the spread of resistance genes globally, what agricultural and environmental reservoirs amplify resistance, and what novel strategies are most promising for the future.

## Questions to think about

1. Most beta-lactamase inhibitors in clinical use (clavulanate, tazobactam, avibactam) work by occupying the serine-based active site, and therefore do not inhibit metallo-beta-lactamases like NDM-1. Why is the development of metallo-beta-lactamase inhibitors scientifically harder than developing serine-beta-lactamase inhibitors? What properties would an ideal MBL inhibitor need to have that complicate drug design?

2. Efflux pumps like AcrAB-TolC have broad substrate specificity — they export antibiotics from multiple drug classes simultaneously. But these same pumps likely evolved long before the clinical introduction of antibiotics. What do you think their ancestral biological functions might be, and how does knowing that functions existed before antibiotic use change how we think about using efflux pump inhibitors as drug adjuvants?

3. VanA-type vancomycin resistance converts the terminal D-Ala–D-Ala of peptidoglycan precursors to D-Ala–D-Lac, reducing vancomycin binding by 1,000-fold. The *vanA* gene cluster first arose in naturally resistant environmental organisms and then spread to clinical Enterococci. Why might bacteria in soil environments have evolved vancomycin resistance long before vancomycin was used in hospitals? What does this tell us about the reservoir of resistance genes that exists even before clinical antibiotic use?
