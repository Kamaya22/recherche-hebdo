# Antibiotic resistance — Day 5/7: One Health and the Wider Ecology of Resistance

## Context (2-3 paragraphs)

The previous four days of this week have focused primarily on antibiotics as a tool of human medicine: how they work, why bacteria evolve resistance to them, why the drug discovery pipeline has dried up, and how resistance is tracked across hospital-acquired pathogens. But the story told by those frameworks is incomplete. Approximately 70% of the antibiotics produced globally are not used in human medicine at all — they are administered to livestock, poultry, fish, and other food-producing animals, for a mixture of therapeutic, preventive, and in some countries still, growth-promoting purposes. That enormous reservoir of antibiotic use exerts a selection pressure on bacterial populations in animal guts, in farm soils, in rivers and coastal waters, and in food products that eventually reaches human consumers and communities. Understanding resistance without understanding this ecological dimension is like understanding climate change without understanding land use: the human-health effect is real, but the causal chain runs through a far wider system.

The concept that brings this wider system into focus is **One Health** — the recognition that human health, animal health, and environmental health are inextricably linked and can only be addressed together. The term has existed in veterinary and public health literature since the early 2000s, but for antimicrobial resistance specifically, it has moved from a useful rhetorical frame to a policy imperative. In 2022, the Quadripartite — the World Health Organization (WHO), the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH, formerly OIE), and the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) — formalised their collaboration in the One Health Joint Plan of Action, covering six domains, of which AMR is one. In May 2026, WOAH and the World Health Assembly simultaneously adopted an updated Global Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance (GAP-AMR) for 2026–2036, embedding One Health principles as the organising framework for the next decade's coordinated response.

Today's reading examines three interlocking questions. First: what does the scale and geography of antibiotic use and resistance in food-producing animals actually look like, and how has it changed as meat consumption has globalised? Second: through which specific pathways does animal-derived resistance reach humans — via food, via direct animal contact, or via the environment? Third: where policy interventions (bans on growth promoters, regulatory restrictions on medically important antibiotics, stewardship in farming) have been tried, what evidence exists that they work?

## Guided summary (~8 min)

### One Health: more than a slogan

The One Health concept rests on a deceptively simple observation: bacteria do not respect the boundaries between human medicine, veterinary care, and the natural environment. A resistance gene that emerges in the gut of a chicken in Shandong province can, via a conjugative plasmid transferred to *E. coli* in the bird's intestine, travel through the food chain, survive food processing, be transmitted to a person who handles raw poultry, and from there spread to other pathogens in that person's microbiome or in a hospital environment. The chain from farm to bedside is not theoretical — for several well-studied resistance determinants, it has been traced in molecular detail using whole-genome sequencing.

What One Health adds to this picture is the recognition that the drivers operate simultaneously at multiple scales. At the molecular scale, resistance genes jump between bacterial species via horizontal gene transfer. At the farm scale, the density of animals, the concentration of antibiotic metabolites in manure, and the proximity of water sources shape which resistance genes accumulate and spread. At the national scale, the presence or absence of regulatory restrictions on veterinary antibiotic use, the economic incentives facing livestock producers, and the quality of the food safety inspection system determine whether the farm-level accumulation is contained. At the global scale, international trade in live animals, in animal products, and in antimicrobial drugs itself links national systems together. No single intervention point is sufficient — which is precisely why One Health governance frameworks try to link human-health, animal-health, and environmental-health regulators into a single policy space.

### The scale of antibiotic use in food production

The numbers are striking. Estimates of global antimicrobial use in food-producing animals have varied across methodologies and baseline years, but converge on a figure substantially larger than human medicine. A 2019 study in *PLOS Global Public Health* estimated that approximately 99,502 tonnes of antimicrobials were consumed by food animals in 2020 (using WOAH data for 109 countries); a parallel analysis using the Livestock Biomass Conversion methodology placed the 2019 baseline at approximately 110,777 tonnes. For comparison, total global human antibiotic consumption is estimated at approximately 80,000–100,000 tonnes per year (using defined daily dose metrics converted to weight equivalents). On that basis, food animals receive more antibiotics by weight than humans — with the proportions in specific high-income countries even more skewed: in the United States, approximately 70% of medically important antibiotics sold are administered to animals, not people.

The geographic distribution is heavily concentrated. Van Boeckel and colleagues' 2015 *PNAS* data estimated that, in 2013, China, the United States, Brazil, and India collectively accounted for nearly three-quarters of global veterinary antibiotic use. Looking forward, the 2023 *Nature Communications* paper on "The future of antibiotic use in livestock" projects that without coordinated international policy, global veterinary antibiotic use could reach approximately 143,481 tonnes by 2040 — a 29.5% increase from the 2019 baseline — with growth concentrated in Southeast Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, and South Asia, all regions where both meat consumption and the density of unregulated veterinary antibiotic distribution are rising simultaneously.

Why do animals receive so many antibiotics? Three reasons, which are biologically and economically intertwined:

1. **Therapeutic use**: livestock develop bacterial diseases in exactly the same way humans do — respiratory infections, enteric disease, wound infections, mastitis. Treating sick animals with antibiotics is standard veterinary practice and is generally accepted as appropriate when a bacterial diagnosis is established.

2. **Prophylactic (preventive) use**: in intensive livestock systems — large numbers of animals in confined spaces — the risk of disease spreading through a flock or herd is high. Administering antibiotics to a batch of animals before any infection has been confirmed, to prevent an expected outbreak, is common in many countries and remains legal in several jurisdictions.

3. **Growth promotion (sub-therapeutic use)**: at sub-clinical doses, several antibiotic classes — particularly tetracyclines and ionophores — promote faster weight gain and improve feed conversion efficiency in pigs and poultry, through mechanisms that are still not fully understood but appear to involve suppression of subclinical gut microbiome-related inflammation. This practice, which has no therapeutic rationale and exposes billions of bacteria to sustained low-level selection pressure, is what drove the initial accumulation of resistance in livestock in the 20th century. It is now banned in the European Union (since 2006), phased out in the United States (the FDA's Veterinary Feed Directive, fully implemented by 2017), and restricted in several other countries — but it remains widespread in parts of Asia, Africa, and Latin America.

### The animal AMR landscape: hotspots and trajectories

Van Boeckel et al.'s 2019 *Science* paper provides the most geographically granular picture of where animal AMR is currently concentrated. Assembling 901 point-prevalence surveys covering four key food-borne bacterial pathogens — *Salmonella*, *Escherichia coli*, *Campylobacter*, and *Staphylococcus aureus* — in poultry, pigs, and cattle across low- and middle-income countries between 2000 and 2018, the study maps the emergence of high-resistance hotspots against a historical baseline.

The findings are stark. In 2000, most of the surveyed countries showed resistance rates below 40% to the antibiotics tested. By 2018, rates exceeding 50% resistance had emerged across large geographic hotspots: **northeastern China**, **northeastern India**, **southern Brazil**, **Iran**, and **Turkey**. These five regions share a common profile: rapidly intensifying animal production, largely unregulated access to veterinary antibiotics (frequently sold without prescription), burgeoning domestic meat markets, and limited national monitoring capacity. The trend lines are as important as the cross-sectional maps: in all five hotspot regions, resistance rates in food-animal pathogens increased substantially over the 18-year study period, not merely persisting but accelerating.

A consistent pattern across livestock species and countries is the high prevalence of resistance to tetracyclines, sulfonamides, and ampicillin — older, generic antibiotics that are cheap, widely available, and used extensively in animal production. But the more clinically alarming trend is the increasing prevalence of resistance to **third-generation cephalosporins** and **fluoroquinolones** in food animals — drug classes that are classified by the WHO as "critically important" for human medicine. In some countries, more than half of *E. coli* and *Salmonella* isolates from poultry show fluoroquinolone resistance; these are the very drugs used as first-line treatment for serious enteric and urinary tract infections in humans.

### Transmission pathways: how animal resistance reaches people

The pathways from animal antibiotic use to human resistant infection fall into three main categories, which are additive rather than alternative:

**1. Food chain transmission.** Resistant bacteria colonising the gut or skin of food animals survive slaughter and processing in sufficient quantities to contaminate retail meat. Inadequate cooking, cross-contamination from raw meat surfaces to other foods, and direct handling of raw products all provide routes of exposure. Campylobacter — the leading cause of acute bacterial gastroenteritis in high-income countries — is almost entirely acquired from poultry. Resistance to fluoroquinolones in *Campylobacter* was rare in clinical human isolates in Europe in the 1980s; after fluoroquinolones were licensed for veterinary use in poultry in the 1990s, resistance rates climbed to above 50% in many European countries within a decade, with molecular typing confirming poultry as the dominant source. Similarly, extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing *E. coli* — resistant to third-generation cephalosporins — are now routinely recovered from retail chicken in Europe, North America, and across Asia; phylogenetic studies in the Netherlands and the UK have identified specific ESBL *E. coli* clones shared between retail broiler chicken and clinical human urinary tract infection isolates, providing direct molecular evidence of food-chain transmission.

**2. Direct contact transmission.** Farmers, veterinarians, slaughterhouse workers, and others in close occupational contact with animals are at substantially higher risk of colonisation with livestock-associated resistant bacteria. The clearest example is livestock-associated methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus* (LA-MRSA, predominantly clonal complex 398), which emerged in intensive pig farming in the Netherlands in the early 2000s, spread widely through European pig herds, and is now regularly isolated from pig farmers, their household contacts, and hospitalised patients with pig exposure. LA-MRSA CC398 behaves differently from classical hospital-acquired MRSA in several respects — it is often more susceptible to non-beta-lactam antibiotics and less virulent in healthy individuals — but it contributes to the community reservoir of MRSA and can cause serious infections in immunocompromised hosts.

**3. Environmental transmission.** Animal manure is applied to agricultural land at enormous scale as a fertiliser, and with it come antibiotic residues, resistant bacteria, and resistance genes. Studies have consistently identified a gradient of resistance gene abundance in soils associated with intensive animal production: manure application increases the concentration of integrons (mobile genetic elements that collect resistance genes) and specific resistance gene families including *sul1*, *sul2* (sulfonamide resistance), and *tetM*, *tetW* (tetracycline resistance) in crop soils, relative to unfertilised controls. Surface water runoff carries these elements into rivers and groundwater, where resistance genes can be taken up by environmental bacteria that have no clinical association with humans but serve as a reservoir that can share genes with human pathogens. Wastewater treatment plants are a particularly important node: they concentrate antibiotic residues from pharmaceutical manufacturing effluents and hospital sewage, provide conditions that promote horizontal gene transfer in microbial communities, and release effluent into river systems. Studies using metagenomics to map the "resistome" — the entirety of resistance genes in an environmental sample — have documented resistance gene loads in rivers receiving wastewater effluent that parallel or exceed those found in clinical settings.

### MCR-1: a case study in how agricultural use creates last-resort threats

The discovery of MCR-1 in 2015-2016 is the single most dramatic illustration of the food-animal-to-human resistance pipeline, and it repays careful study because it involves a drug that had been held in clinical reserve for decades.

Colistin (polymyxin E) is an antibiotic first introduced in the 1950s that fell out of clinical use in high-income countries in the 1980s due to significant kidney toxicity. It was not abandoned globally, however: its cheapness and broad-spectrum activity against Gram-negative bacteria made it widely adopted in livestock production, particularly in China and Southeast Asia, where by the mid-2000s China alone was using an estimated 12,000 tonnes per year as a veterinary growth promoter. During this period, colistin was considered a drug of "last resort" in human medicine — pulled back out of obscurity as carbapenem-resistant pathogens became more common — precisely because resistance to it was thought to be chromosomal, rare, and not transmissible.

Liu et al.'s 2016 paper demolished that assumption. During routine surveillance of commensal bacteria from pig farms in Guangdong province, the authors identified a novel resistance gene, *mcr-1* (mobile colistin resistance 1), encoded on a conjugative plasmid — meaning it could spread between bacterial species via horizontal gene transfer. They found it in 21% of farm *E. coli* samples, in 14% of retail pork and chicken samples, and in 1.4% of hospitalised patients' clinical *E. coli* isolates in Guangdong hospitals. The gene was already present in a 2011 clinical isolate from a hospital archive. In short: fifteen years of high-volume colistin use in Chinese livestock had generated and disseminated a mobile colistin-resistance gene that had already entered human pathogens via the food chain.

The subsequent months were a demonstration of how rapidly an agricultural-origin resistance gene can globalise. Within a year of the initial publication, MCR-1 had been identified in clinical, veterinary, and environmental isolates from more than 30 countries on five continents. Retrospective screening of archived samples found the gene in European livestock isolates from 2011. It appeared in travel-associated bloodstream infections and urinary tract infections. It was found in environmental samples from rivers and estuaries. The global livestock trade, including live animal exports and trade in animal feed, provided the mechanism for distribution that human medicine alone could not have explained.

The policy response was swift in one country: China banned the use of colistin-positive feed additives in livestock in November 2016, with implementation from April 2017. Subsequent studies tracked the impact. A 2020 study in *The Lancet Infectious Diseases* analysing samples before and after the ban found that MCR-1 prevalence in *E. coli* from pig farms fell from 45.0% in 2016 to 19.4% in 2018 — a substantial decline in two years. A 2022 longitudinal study from Beijing hospitals found that the prevalence of MCR-1-positive *E. coli* in hospitalised patients, which had been increasing from 2009 to 2016, began declining after 2017, closely tracking the veterinary ban. This is among the clearest empirical demonstrations available that regulatory restriction of antibiotic use in animals can reduce resistance burden in humans within a few years.

### What works: evidence from policy interventions

The MCR-1/colistin case is not unique. Several natural experiments from countries that have implemented voluntary or regulatory restrictions on veterinary antibiotic use provide converging evidence that reduction is possible without destroying agricultural productivity:

**Denmark** pursued the most systematic national programme, beginning with a voluntary withdrawal of growth-promoting antibiotics in broiler chickens in 1998 and expanding to pigs in 1999, followed by the Yellow Card system (which limits use by any farm exceeding the median consumption for its category) and continued strengthening of the DANMAP surveillance programme. From 1992 to 2008, antibiotic use per kilogram of pig raised fell by more than 50%, while production (in weaning pig numbers) increased substantially. Vancomycin-resistant *Enterococcus faecium* (VRE) in Danish pigs, which had become extremely common during the 1990s avoparcin (a glycopeptide growth promoter) era, declined sharply after the avoparcin ban and has remained low.

**The Netherlands** legislated a 50% reduction in total veterinary antibiotic sales relative to a 2009 baseline, achieving that target by 2013, and subsequently extended it to a 70% reduction target. National surveillance data confirmed that the load of ESBL-producing *E. coli* in food animals declined in parallel with use restrictions, and that the contribution of poultry-source strains to human ESBL-*E. coli* urinary tract infections fell.

**The EU's full ban on all antibiotic growth promoters** came into effect on 1 January 2006. The cross-European data show a consistent decline in resistance to some antibiotic classes in food-animal pathogens in the years following, with the caveat that in some countries total therapeutic use (for prevention and treatment) increased in the short term as farmers sought to compensate for the health impact of denser housing without growth promoters — a phenomenon pointing to the need for accompanying animal husbandry and biosecurity improvements.

These cases do not prove that reduction is easy or costless. They demonstrate that it is achievable under the right regulatory and surveillance conditions. The critical common thread is that reduction in use was paired with monitoring: without surveillance capable of detecting whether resistance rates actually fell in response to policy, it would have been impossible to confirm effectiveness or detect perverse substitution effects.

### The environmental dimension and unresolved questions

Beyond the food chain and direct contact, the environment represents the least-controlled pathway for resistance dissemination. Pharmaceutical manufacturers — often in India and China, which produce the majority of global antibiotic active pharmaceutical ingredients — discharge concentrated antibiotic effluents into receiving waterways. Studies of rivers near pharmaceutical manufacturing clusters have found antibiotic concentrations sufficient to maintain selection pressure in aquatic microbial communities many kilometres downstream. This creates a continuous background of selection for resistance genes in environmental bacteria that is distinct from clinical or agricultural selection pressure — and largely absent from existing regulatory frameworks, most of which address human or veterinary use but not manufacturing effluent standards.

What remains genuinely contested in the One Health AMR literature is the *quantitative* attribution question: what fraction of the human AMR burden is attributable to animal/agricultural use, versus human medical use, versus environmental sources? A 2020 medRxiv preprint (not yet peer-reviewed) that modelled One Health drivers estimated that food-animal antibiotic use might account for approximately 30–50% of the AMR in common food-borne pathogens in humans, with wide uncertainty. The Allel et al. 2023 *Lancet Planetary Health* study of 76 countries found clear statistical associations between national animal antibiotic consumption and national human resistance rates for several critical pathogens — specifically, that countries with higher veterinary antibiotic use had significantly higher rates of carbapenem-resistant *Acinetobacter baumannii*, third-generation cephalosporin-resistant *E. coli*, and oxacillin-resistant *S. aureus* in human clinical isolates. But statistical association at the country level cannot definitively attribute individual resistance genes in individual patients.

This quantitative uncertainty does not, however, undermine the policy case for action. For pathogens like *Campylobacter* (almost entirely acquired from poultry) and *Salmonella* (substantially from food animals), the animal source is not contested. For carbapenem resistance specifically — the clinical frontline — the direct animal-to-human pathway is less clear and probably less important than the within-hospital transmission dynamics described on Day 4. The One Health framework's value is not that it assigns blame to one sector over another, but that it reveals the mechanisms by which selection pressure in any sector enriches the shared global reservoir of resistance genes available to clinical pathogens.

## Questions to think about

1. China's colistin ban in livestock in 2017 demonstrably reduced MCR-1 prevalence in pigs and in hospitalised patients within two years — a striking policy success. China was able to implement this quickly, in part, because a single regulatory decision covered the entire national livestock sector. Most low-income countries with high agricultural antibiotic use have weaker central regulatory capacity. What institutional, financial, or governance conditions are necessary to achieve similar reductions in settings without China's administrative capacity, and which international actors have leverage to support them?

2. European nations that have reduced veterinary antibiotic use dramatically (Denmark by >50%, the Netherlands by 70%) have maintained agricultural productivity — but critics note that these reductions were accompanied by investments in animal welfare (higher space-per-animal ratios), better biosecurity (all-in/all-out stocking systems), and diagnostic infrastructure. How much of the "antibiotic reduction success story" is actually a biosecurity and animal husbandry story? If a country reduces antibiotic use without improving the underlying conditions that drive disease in dense livestock populations, what would you expect to happen?

3. The environmental route — pharmaceutical effluent, manure on agricultural land, antibiotic residues in rivers — represents a resistance-selection pathway that is almost entirely absent from current national action plans on AMR. Given that the environmental resistome provides a background reservoir of resistance genes that clinical pathogens can acquire, should environmental contamination standards for antibiotics be treated as a public health emergency in the same way that contamination of drinking water with heavy metals is? What would such a regulatory framework look like, and who bears the cost?
