# Antibiotic resistance — Day 3/7: The Discovery Pipeline Crisis

## Context (2–3 paragraphs)

Day 1 established that antimicrobial resistance is a genuine emergency — more than 1.27 million people died directly from resistant bacterial infections in 2019 alone, and the GRAM Collaborators project that toll will rise to 1.91 million per year by 2050. Day 2 examined the molecular side of resistance: the four main strategies bacteria use to survive antibiotic exposure (enzymatic inactivation, efflux pumps, target site modification, and reduced permeability), and the role of horizontal gene transfer in spreading those strategies across species at remarkable speed. Together, Days 1 and 2 paint a picture of a biological problem that is scientifically well-understood. Today's reading examines why the institutional response has been so inadequate — specifically, why the drug discovery pipeline that once gave us nearly every antibiotic class in clinical use today has been running nearly dry for over fifty years.

The pipeline crisis has two interlocking dimensions. The scientific dimension came first: the "golden age" of antibiotic discovery between the 1940s and the mid-1960s was largely built on a single technique — screening soil-dwelling bacteria (primarily *Streptomyces* species) for compounds that could kill pathogens. By the late 1960s, that approach had hit a ceiling: new screens kept rediscovering the same molecules, a problem researchers call dereplication. The accessible chemical space of classical natural-product antibiotics had, to a first approximation, been explored. Moving beyond it would require substantially more sophisticated chemistry, computational tools, and biological understanding than existed at the time. The economic dimension then made the scientific challenge nearly irrelevant for commercial actors: even when breakthroughs occur, the business model for bringing them to market is so structurally unfavourable that most of the pharmaceutical industry has simply left the field.

Understanding the pipeline crisis is therefore not an exercise in blaming scientists for insufficient ingenuity or companies for insufficient public spirit. It is an exercise in recognizing a genuine market failure — a situation in which socially critical goods cannot be produced at sufficient scale by market mechanisms alone — and examining the policy tools being developed to address it. Today's sources include the most-cited economic review of the collapse, the WHO's most recent audit of what the pipeline actually contains, and a comprehensive peer-reviewed survey of the incentive mechanisms being tried.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

### The golden age — and why it ended

Between the 1940s and the mid-1960s, the pharmaceutical industry discovered the vast majority of the antibiotic classes still used today. The engine of that golden age was a remarkably simple technique: collect soil samples, culture the microorganisms in them, and screen the culture media for compounds that inhibit pathogen growth. The productivity of this approach was extraordinary. Streptomyces bacteria alone yielded streptomycin (1943), chloramphenicol (1947), tetracyclines (1948), erythromycin (1952), vancomycin (1953), and rifampicin (1957). Fungi gave us penicillin and the cephalosporin scaffold. Within roughly two decades, clinicians had access to drugs targeting bacterial cell walls, ribosomes, DNA replication, transcription, and membrane integrity.

The collapse of this productivity was not sudden but structural. By the late 1960s, pharmaceutical companies found that each new soil screen increasingly returned compounds they already knew — dereplication identified the same actinomycin, streptomycin, or tetracycline derivative appearing again and again. The accessible fraction of the natural-product chemical space had been thoroughly mined. To find genuinely new scaffolds would require either moving into poorly explored environments (deep-sea sediments, previously inaccessible organisms) or building synthetic compounds from scratch — requiring computational chemistry, target-structure knowledge, and medicinal chemistry capacity far beyond what was routinely available. Meanwhile, the existing antibiotic arsenal was large, the infections it addressed were mostly curable, and the economic rationale for continued investment was weakening. The last antibiotic class with truly broad Gram-negative activity approved in clinical use before the current era was the fluoroquinolones — and those entered clinical use in the 1980s, based on chemistry begun in the 1960s.

### Why antibiotics are a structurally poor investment

The market failure in antibiotic development does not arise because executives are unusually short-sighted or scientists unusually timid. It arises because the characteristics that make an antibiotic clinically important are almost exactly the characteristics that make it financially unattractive to develop.

Consider the economics of drug development compared to a chronic-disease drug such as a cholesterol-lowering agent or a diabetes medication. A patient on atorvastatin takes it daily for decades — generating decades of revenue per patient. A patient with a serious bacterial infection takes an antibiotic for five to fourteen days. The revenue per patient is therefore far lower, regardless of price. Now add stewardship: the entire framework of responsible antibiotic use — promoted by the WHO, national health ministries, and hospital infection-control programs — specifically aims to keep the most effective drugs in reserve, prescribed only when simpler alternatives fail. An antibiotic that works brilliantly against resistant organisms should ideally be used rarely and as a last resort. The company that developed it will therefore sell relatively few prescriptions, by deliberate design.

Add generic competition. Patents in pharmaceuticals last twenty years from filing, and antibiotic development typically consumes ten to fifteen of those years in preclinical and clinical trials. This leaves only five to ten years of patent-protected sales — at which point generics enter and prices collapse. Add clinical trial difficulty: recruiting patients with confirmed drug-resistant infections for randomised controlled trials is exceptionally hard, because those patients are rare, distributed across many hospitals, and often critically ill. A trial for a carbapenem-resistant pathogen might need to enrol patients from twenty countries over three years, at costs per patient far exceeding those of any common disease trial. Add the near-certainty that bacteria will eventually evolve resistance to the new drug anyway, further eroding the market over time.

The result, as Årdal and colleagues document, is a return on investment for new antibiotics that independent analyses have placed between 1% and 7% — compared with 30% or more for oncology drugs over the same period. Eighteen of the twenty largest pharmaceutical companies active in antibiotic R&D in 1980 had left the field by 2020. The market sent a clear signal: antibiotics are not a viable commercial proposition.

### Bankruptcy as a policy signal

The structural problem became viscerally apparent between 2019 and 2020, when a cluster of small biotech companies that had succeeded in developing novel approved antibiotics declared bankruptcy or were acquired for nominal sums.

The most striking case was Achaogen, which developed plazomicin — an aminoglycoside modified to evade the resistance enzymes that defeat older aminoglycosides, active against carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales. The drug received FDA approval in June 2018 on the strength of genuine clinical efficacy data. In April 2019, Achaogen filed for bankruptcy. The company had spent approximately $300 million developing plazomicin; in the quarter before bankruptcy, the drug generated $800,000 in sales. Physicians, quite reasonably, were holding it in reserve for cases where nothing else would work — and those cases, though catastrophic when they occur, are not common enough to sustain a company that needs to recoup hundreds of millions of dollars.

Melinta Therapeutics, which marketed four antibiotics including delafloxacin and meropenem-vaborbactam, filed for bankruptcy in December 2019 with nearly $1 billion in debt despite $41 million in sales over the first nine months of that year. Tetraphase Biosciences was sold in 2020 for a price representing a small fraction of its former valuation. These were not companies with bad products; their products worked. They were companies whose products worked in exactly the way that responsible stewardship demands — used sparingly, as a last resort — leaving them without a viable business model.

### What the WHO pipeline audit reveals

The WHO's 2023 pipeline review (with data through 31 December 2023) found 97 products in clinical development against WHO priority pathogens: 57 traditional small-molecule antibiotics and 40 non-traditional agents (including bacteriophages, monoclonal antibodies targeting bacterial toxins, and microbiome-modifying therapies). The count has risen from 80 in 2021, which the WHO itself characterised as encouraging. The qualitative assessment is considerably less optimistic.

Of the 57 traditional antibiotics, fewer than one in four represents a genuinely novel mechanism of action, a new chemical class, or a validated new molecular target. The majority are derivatives of existing scaffolds — improved beta-lactams, modified aminoglycosides, next-generation fluoroquinolones — against which resistance mechanisms already present in clinical pathogens can evolve relatively rapidly. The WHO's Bacterial Priority Pathogens List identifies carbapenem-resistant *Acinetobacter baumannii*, extended-spectrum beta-lactamase-producing *Escherichia coli* and *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, and carbapenem-resistant *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* as its four "critical priority" organisms — precisely the Gram-negative pathogens against which the pipeline is most inadequate. The clinical-stage attrition rate for antibiotics, historically, is approximately 80–90% from Phase 1 to approval. The non-traditional agents in the pipeline address this gap only partially: bacteriophage therapies, for example, are highly specific to particular bacterial strains and face their own regulatory and manufacturing challenges that make rapid scaling difficult.

The 2023 review also examined the preclinical pipeline and found 244 agents against priority pathogens and *Clostridioides difficile* — a healthier upstream reservoir than the clinical pipeline suggests. But the translation rate from preclinical to clinical has consistently been poor, and the WHO noted that innovation in the preclinical pipeline, while better than in the clinical stage, still does not match the breadth of unmet medical need.

### The push/pull framework and its current state

Economists and policy researchers divide potential solutions into two categories, a distinction first clarified in the antibiotic context in the mid-2000s and now central to the entire field.

**Push incentives** reduce the cost and risk of R&D before a product reaches the market. They include direct grants to researchers and companies (BARDA in the United States funds early- to mid-stage antibiotic development; CARB-X, a global public-private partnership launched in 2016, has committed over $550 million to early-stage projects); tax credits for qualified infectious-disease research; subsidised clinical trial infrastructure (the EU's ENABLE programme provided access to clinical trial networks for small companies); and regulatory fee waivers under the US GAIN Act. Push incentives are relatively well-established, administratively tractable, and politically palatable. Their limitation is structural: they make the upfront cost of development cheaper but do not change the fundamental problem that the market at the end of the pipeline cannot sustain viable companies.

**Pull incentives** change the expected reward for successful market entry, independent of sales volume. The most intellectually important design principle in pull incentives is *delinkage*: breaking the connection between the revenue a developer receives and the number of prescriptions written. Delinkage is the mechanism that allows the public-health goal (use this drug sparingly, as a last resort) and the commercial goal (recoup the investment in development) to be decoupled and pursued simultaneously.

The most advanced real-world implementation of a delinked pull incentive is England's subscription model, launched by NHS England and NICE in July 2022. Two antibiotics — ceftazidime-avibactam and cefiderocol — were the first to enter the pilot. Under the model, manufacturers receive approximately £10 million per year for ten years regardless of how much of the drug is prescribed, in exchange for guaranteed availability and quality. Early evaluations suggest the model is administratively workable and is broadly welcomed by both prescribers and stewardship specialists: a 2023 survey of infection consultants found that roughly 70% considered it a welcome development and believed it would stimulate further R&D.

In the United States, the PASTEUR Act (Pioneering Antimicrobial Subscriptions To End Upsurging Resistance), first introduced in 2020 and repeatedly reintroduced, would authorise $6 billion over ten years to subscription-style contracts for qualifying antibiotics, with individual payments ranging from $750 million to $3 billion per antibiotic depending on clinical value and innovation. As of 2025, the PASTEUR Act had not passed into law, though it continues to receive bipartisan support.

The most contentious policy debate is over the European Commission's proposal for a **Transferable Exclusivity Voucher (TEV)**: a mechanism that would grant a company one additional year of patent-protected sales on *any* of its products in exchange for developing a qualifying antibiotic. In February 2023, a Lancet commentary signed by 28 European AMR researchers, economists, and public-health experts — including Christine Årdal, Stephan Harbarth, Marc Mendelson, and Jim O'Neill (author of the UK's 2016 AMR Review) — argued forcefully against the TEV on the grounds that it does not delink revenue from antibiotic sales and risks inflating costs elsewhere in the pharmaceutical system without proportionately improving access to or innovation in antibiotics. This debate illustrates a broader tension: pull incentives that are cheap to implement (like the TEV) often fail to address the root cause, while those that genuinely fix the underlying problem (subscription contracts) require sustained public funding commitments that are politically harder to secure.

### The innovation gap in perspective

Day 1 noted that the WHO's 2023 pipeline review identified 97 agents in clinical development. That number is worth contextualising. The entire global antibiotic pipeline in clinical stages — representing billions of dollars of investment, hundreds of companies, and decades of work — contains roughly as many agents as a moderately large oncology company might have in its single-disease pipeline. And fewer than a quarter of those agents represent the kind of mechanistic novelty that can durably address resistance arising through existing mechanisms. The gap between what the pipeline contains and what would be needed to turn the AMR trajectory is not a matter of fine-tuning; it reflects a fundamental structural failure in how essential public-health goods are produced and financed.

## Questions to think about

1. The two most prominent pull-incentive models on the table — the UK/NHS subscription and the US PASTEUR Act — both involve governments paying a fixed sum for the right to use a drug, delinked from usage volume. What assumptions about how much the capability to treat resistant infections is *worth* to a society do these payment levels embed? What would it take to set those payments at the "correct" level — and who should decide?

2. Push incentives (CARB-X, BARDA) are established and producing candidates in early development. Pull incentives are newer, limited in geographic scope, and underfunded relative to what economists estimate is necessary. Given that a drug takes ten to fifteen years to go from preclinical to approval, if governments committed today to the full PASTEUR-level pull funding, what would the realistic timeline be before the pipeline effects became visible in clinical practice? What does this imply about the urgency of decisions made in the next five years?

3. The Achaogen/plazomicin case shows that a drug can receive FDA approval, be genuinely effective against resistant pathogens, be used responsibly (i.e., sparingly), and still bankrupt the company that made it. Does this suggest that pull incentives alone — even properly designed subscription contracts — are sufficient? Or does solving the AMR pipeline crisis also require rethinking how we conduct clinical trials for resistant infections, how regulators approve narrow-spectrum agents with smaller patient populations, and how we ensure global access rather than just high-income-country market entry?
