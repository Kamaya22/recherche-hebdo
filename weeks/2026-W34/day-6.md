# The biology of aging — Day 6/7: Controversies and open debates — is aging a disease, and can mouse data be trusted?

## Context

The first five days of this week treated aging biology as a body of established mechanism: hallmarks (Day 1), senescence (Day 2), genomic and epigenetic damage (Day 3), metabolic signaling (Day 4), and the human-trial evidence for intervening on it (Day 5). But running underneath nearly every one of those days has been a set of questions the field has not settled, and today surfaces three of the sharpest: should aging itself be classified and treated as a disease, does aging happen because of accumulating damage or because of something closer to a genetic program running past its useful point, and how much can findings from laboratory mice — the animal behind almost every mechanism and most interventions covered this week — actually be trusted to predict what happens in humans.

These are not academic side-questions. Day 1's closing question already asked whether it would make more sense to regulate "aging" as a single treatable target rather than as separate diseases — that is precisely the disease-classification debate, and it has direct regulatory teeth: the FDA does not currently recognize aging itself as an indication, which is part of why TAME (Day 5) has struggled for over a decade to get funded as a trial with "aging" rather than a named disease as its outcome. Day 3 flagged that mice have very different telomere biology from humans, a first hint that mouse-to-human translation is not automatic; today generalizes that caveat and asks how big a problem it really is across the whole field, from the NIA's Interventions Testing Program (Day 5) to the countless single-lab mouse studies that never make it that far.

Today's three readings tackle these in turn: a current science-journalism feature on the disease-classification fight and why it has resurfaced with new force; a 2026 peer-reviewed argument, grounded in evolutionary and comparative biology, for why aging does not fit standard disease criteria; and a clinical-pharmacology commentary on what is systematically wrong with how preclinical aging models are built, and what would need to change for their results to translate more reliably. Day 7 will pull today's open questions together with the rest of the week into a synthesis.

## Today's readings

**"Is ageing a disease? The debate that could reshape medicine"** (*Nature*, News Feature, November 2025) — https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-025-03525-3 — ~8 min. A current news feature in Nature — the field's leading peer-reviewed multidisciplinary journal — reporting on a survey of roughly 100 aging researchers that found deep disagreement not just on whether aging is a disease, but on what aging even is and when it starts. It is included because it maps the live state of the argument, including the practical stakes (funding, regulation, ethics) that make this more than a semantic dispute, and it is current science journalism from a peer-reviewed journal's own editorial team, not a general-interest outlet.

**"Aging is not a disease: an evolutionary and comparative biological reappraisal"** (Feltes, *Biogerontology*, 2026) — https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10522-026-10422-3 — ~7 min (abstract and the sections framing aging through declining selection gradients and cross-species heterogeneity). A peer-reviewed article in Biogerontology, a specialist gerontology journal (Springer Nature), arguing from evolutionary theory and comparative biology — rather than from clinical or regulatory reasoning — that aging does not meet standard criteria for a disease. It is useful precisely because it makes the "against" case with a specific, checkable scientific argument, giving readers something more substantive than the pro/con summary in the Nature feature to evaluate.

**"Optimizing preclinical models of ageing for translation to clinical trials"** (Mitchell, MacArthur & Kane, *British Journal of Clinical Pharmacology*, 2025) — https://bpspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/bcp.15902 — ~5 min. A peer-reviewed commentary by researchers at Princeton and the Institute for Systems Biology (Seattle), in a specialist clinical pharmacology journal, examining why preclinical aging models — historically young, inbred, healthy male mice — often fail to predict what happens in the older, genetically diverse, multimorbid humans an intervention is ultimately meant to help, and what changes would improve translation. It grounds today's third theme in a concrete, mechanism-level account of the mouse-to-human gap rather than treating "mouse studies don't always translate" as a vague truism.

## Guided summary

Start with why the disease question resurfaced now, rather than treating it as settled history. The Nature feature's reporting on a survey of about 100 aging researchers found that specialists cannot agree even on foundational questions — what aging is, whether it is a disease, and when it starts — despite broad consensus on the hallmarks framework this week has used throughout. That might seem surprising given how much shared mechanistic vocabulary the field has (Day 1's twelve hallmarks, Day 2's senescence, Day 3's epigenetic clocks), but the disagreement is not really about mechanism — it is about classification, and classification has consequences. Proponents of calling aging a disease argue it would unlock regulatory pathways and funding that currently only exist for named diseases (this is the TAME funding problem from Day 5 in miniature: metformin's actual target, if approved, would be "aging," which no regulator currently recognizes as treatable). Opponents warn of exactly the kind of harm that played out at the World Health Organization: in 2019, the ICD-11 diagnostic manual initially included a code effectively treating "old age" as a diagnosable condition, which triggered a large, organized backlash from geriatricians, gerontological societies, and older-persons' advocacy groups internationally, who argued the framing was ageist — reducing normal aging to a pathology risked justifying reduced care or treatment "futility" judgments for older patients precisely because they were old. That campaign led the WHO to walk the framing back, keeping aging-related conditions codeable without treating chronological old age itself as a diagnosis. The episode is a concrete illustration of a pattern also visible in Day 2's discussion of senescence and Day 5's discussion of drug trials: a scientifically defensible-sounding move (treating a biological process as an intervention target) can have institutional and ethical consequences its proponents did not fully anticipate.

The Feltes review makes the "not a disease" case on different grounds than the ethical argument above — it argues from evolutionary and comparative biology rather than from regulatory risk. Its core claim is that aging is best understood as an emergent consequence of declining selection gradients: natural selection's power to weed out harmful mutations weakens after an organism has already reproduced, because a mutation whose damage only shows up post-reproductively is invisible to selection acting on reproductive success. This is the same underlying logic behind the classical evolutionary theories of aging — mutation accumulation (harmful late-acting mutations drift unchecked because selection cannot "see" them) and antagonistic pleiotropy (a gene that helps early-life reproduction spreads through a population even if it causes late-life harm, because the reproductive benefit outweighs a cost incurred after most selection pressure has passed). Both models predict aging as a byproduct of how selection's strength changes over a lifespan, not as a deviation from some species-typical "healthy" state the way a disease is — which is precisely Feltes's point: disease implies a departure from normal function, but if decline is what selection was always going to permit once reproduction is largely complete, "normal" for an aging organism includes that decline. The review adds a second, comparative argument: aging trajectories vary enormously across taxa — some species show steep, early physiological decline, while others (certain long-lived fish, tortoises, and some clonal organisms) show very slow or barely detectable increases in mortality risk with age over long stretches of life — a diversity of patterns that sits awkwardly with treating "aging" as a single, uniform pathology with one diagnostic threshold, the way a disease category typically requires.

It is worth being precise about what this debate is and is not about, because "programmed vs. damage" is often stated more starkly in popular coverage than the science supports. The evolutionary theories above — mutation accumulation and antagonistic pleiotropy — are non-adaptive: they do not claim aging exists "for" any purpose, evolutionary or otherwise; it is simply what happens when selection stops caring. This is the mainstream position in evolutionary biology, and it is compatible with everything this week has covered: the hallmarks of aging (Day 1) can be understood as the accumulating molecular consequences of exactly this kind of unselected-against damage and drift, rather than as steps in a program. A minority, more contested strand of thinking — sometimes discussed under labels like "quasi-programmed" or "hyperfunction" theories — argues instead that some age-related pathology results not from damage per se but from developmental and growth-promoting gene activity (regulated by pathways like the mTOR signaling covered on Day 4) that simply never switches off once its useful developmental role is complete, producing pathology through continued, excessive function rather than through breakdown. That is a genuinely different mechanism from classical damage accumulation, and it remains an active, unresolved argument among specialists rather than settled consensus — worth knowing about specifically because it shows that "damage vs. program" is not a closed two-sided debate so much as a family of overlapping, partially compatible hypotheses about which mechanism dominates for which hallmark.

The third theme — how far mouse data can be trusted — connects directly back to nearly everything covered Tuesday through Friday. The Mitchell, MacArthur & Kane commentary makes a structural argument: for over a century, the default preclinical model has been young, healthy, inbred, and male, tested in a controlled lab environment that has little in common with the aged, genetically diverse, multimorbid, often frail humans an aging intervention is ultimately meant to help. That mismatch is not a minor technical detail — it can change results outright. Genetic background alone has been shown to determine whether an intervention like caloric restriction (Day 4's CALERIE trial) helps, does nothing, or actively harms cognitive aging in a given mouse, depending on which genetic variants that particular mouse carries; averaged across a genetically uniform inbred strain, such opposing effects can cancel out and produce a falsely "null" or falsely "positive" result that would not hold in a more diverse population. This is exactly the concern Day 3 raised in miniature about telomere biology differing between mice and humans, and it is why the NIA's Interventions Testing Program (Day 5), which tests across multiple genetically heterogeneous mouse populations at multiple sites specifically to avoid single-lab, single-strain false positives, is considered unusually rigorous rather than merely standard practice — most aging research is not run that way. The commentary's proposed fix is to shift toward more genetically diverse mouse populations (such as Diversity Outbred mice, which are bred specifically so that no two individuals are genetically identical, better mirroring human genetic variation) and toward outcome measures closer to what actually matters clinically — frailty, multi-system function, healthspan — rather than single biomarkers or lifespan alone.

Put together, these three threads point to a common underlying lesson for reading any aging-science headline, including the human-trial results covered on Day 5: a striking mouse result, an epigenetic-clock change, or a mechanistic finding is never automatically a finding about disease, about a shared human biological program, or about what will happen outside a narrow, historically atypical experimental population — each of those inferential steps is a separate claim that needs its own evidence, and today's readings show live, ongoing disagreement about all three among the specialists closest to the data.

## Questions to think about

- The WHO's 2019 "old age" ICD code triggered backlash specifically over the fear that framing aging as pathology could justify reduced care for older patients. Is that a risk inherent to classifying aging as a disease, or a risk that depends entirely on how such a classification is implemented and regulated?
- Feltes's evolutionary argument says aging is not a deviation from "normal" function because decline is what weakened selection was always going to permit. Does that evolutionary framing settle the practical question of whether aging should be medically treated, or are "is it natural" and "should we intervene on it" genuinely separate questions?
- If genetic diversity in mouse models can flip an intervention's effect from beneficial to harmful depending on which variants a given animal carries, how much should a single-strain mouse result (the kind behind much of what Days 2–5 described) shift your confidence about what the same intervention would do in a genetically diverse human population?
