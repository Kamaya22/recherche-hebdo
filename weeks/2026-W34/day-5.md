# The biology of aging — Day 5/7: Interventions and their evidence base — what human trials on rapamycin, metformin, and senolytics actually show

## Context

Day 4 ended on a deliberate pivot point: CALERIE, the one long-term randomized human trial of caloric restriction, showed that deliberately restricting food intake slows a validated pace-of-aging clock — real evidence, but also a demonstration of how hard the intervention itself is to sustain (25% caloric restriction for two years, in a trial that had to work hard to keep participants adherent). That difficulty is exactly why so much current research effort has gone into finding drugs that flip the same molecular switches — AMPK activation, mTOR inhibition — without requiring anyone to eat less. Today looks at whether those drugs actually deliver in humans.

Three intervention classes dominate this conversation: rapamycin (an mTOR inhibitor, pharmacologically mimicking part of what nutrient restriction does), metformin (a decades-old, cheap, generic diabetes drug with a long-debated "side effect" of possibly slowing aging more broadly), and senolytics (drugs that clear the senescent cells described on Day 2). All three have strong mechanistic rationales and, in the case of rapamycin, genuinely strong mouse-lifespan data. What they mostly still lack is the thing that would settle the question: large, long, randomized human trials with hard clinical endpoints — new diagnoses, hospitalizations, deaths — rather than biomarkers as a stand-in.

That gap is not a footnote; it is the central fact of this field right now, and it shapes everything else. Today works through what the best available human data — not mouse data — actually show for each of the three drug classes, including where a trial's own authors report a result that did not reach statistical significance. Reading trial results honestly, including the negative or ambiguous ones, is part of what separates evidence-based geroscience from marketing claims made about the same drugs.

## Today's readings

**"Influence of rapamycin on safety and healthspan metrics after one year: PEARL trial results"** (Moel, Harinath, Lee, Nyquist, Morgan, Isman & Zalzala, *Aging* [Impact Journals], April 2025) — https://www.aging-us.com/article/206235/text — ~8 min (abstract, trial design, and the results on lean mass, pain, and safety). A peer-reviewed, open-access report of a registered trial (NCT04488601): a 48-week, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled study of low-dose intermittent rapamycin (5 mg or 10 mg weekly, compounded) in 114 healthy adults aged 50–85. It is the largest published placebo-controlled human safety/healthspan trial of rapamycin to date, which is why it anchors today's reading on this drug.

**"Metformin and epigenetic age in non-diabetic older people with HIV in Madrid (METFORAGING): a double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled, pilot trial"** (Marcelo-Calvo, Esteban-Cantos, Jurado, Montejano, Rodríguez-Centeno, Gutiérrez-García et al., *eClinicalMedicine* [The Lancet Discovery Science], April 2026) — https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00121-5/fulltext — ~7 min (abstract, primary outcome, and safety results). A peer-reviewed, fully open-access randomized controlled trial from La Paz University Hospital and the CNIO (Spanish National Cancer Research Centre) testing 850 mg metformin twice daily for 96 weeks against multiple epigenetic clocks, including PhenoAge (introduced on Day 3) as the pre-specified primary outcome. It is included precisely because it is a real, recent RCT with a clearly reported null primary result — a useful corrective to how metformin's anti-aging reputation is often discussed.

**"Towards a personalized approach in senolytic trials"** (Khosla, Monroe & Farr, *Nature Aging*, 2025, vol. 5, pp. 1926–1929) — https://www.nature.com/articles/s43587-025-00964-5 — ~5 min. A peer-reviewed perspective by a Mayo Clinic bone/aging research group in a Nature Portfolio journal, written specifically to take stock of where senolytic clinical trials stand — as of the piece, only 9 published human senolytic trials exist, and just 2 include a control group — and to argue for a more targeted trial design going forward. It updates and complicates the senolytic-trial picture that Day 2's Chaib, Tchkonia & Kirkland review (2022) and the SenNet atlas left off with.

## Guided summary

Start with rapamycin, because it has by far the strongest non-human backing. The NIA's Interventions Testing Program (ITP) — a rigorous, multi-site, genetically heterogeneous mouse-testing pipeline explicitly designed to avoid the false positives that plague single-lab aging studies — found in 2009, and has repeatedly confirmed since, that rapamycin extends median mouse lifespan even when treatment starts late in life (20 months of age, roughly equivalent to human late-middle-age), with effects that are dose-dependent and, at lower doses, larger in females than males. Intermittent dosing (one month on, one month off) worked about as well as continuous dosing — an important detail, because it is exactly the intermittent, low-dose regimen the PEARL trial tested in humans. PEARL itself, however, was not designed or powered to detect disease or mortality outcomes; its 114 healthy older adults were followed for 48 weeks and assessed on safety labs, adverse events, and self-reported healthspan measures. The headline result is reassuring on safety — no significant excess of moderate-to-severe adverse events versus placebo, and none of the immune-suppression or impaired-wound-healing signals that shadow rapamycin's use as a transplant-rejection drug at much higher, continuous doses — plus a modest, sex-specific efficacy signal: women on the 10 mg weekly dose gained measurable lean tissue mass and reported less pain than placebo. That is real human evidence, but it is biomarker- and self-report-level evidence over one year, not evidence that rapamycin delays disease or extends healthy years — the mouse-to-human gap on hard outcomes remains wide open.

Metformin's story is almost the inverse: weaker non-human lifespan data, but a much longer clinical track record and a specific historical rationale for why anyone thought to test it for aging at all. Unlike rapamycin, metformin has not shown consistent lifespan extension across model organisms in meta-analysis — its case rests instead on decades of use in diabetes, where large observational studies repeatedly found that diabetic patients on metformin appeared to develop cancer, cardiovascular disease, and dementia at lower rates than expected, and in some comparisons even than non-diabetic controls, which is an unusual and provocative pattern for a drug treating a disease that itself accelerates aging-related decline. That observation is what launched TAME (Targeting Aging with Metformin), the trial designed by the American Federation for Aging Research (AFAR) to test metformin's effect on the joint onset of major age-related diseases in thousands of non-diabetic older adults — and TAME remains, as of mid-2026, unfunded and without a single enrolled participant, roughly $75 million short, because a cheap generic drug attracts little pharmaceutical-industry sponsorship. In that funding vacuum, smaller, differently-designed trials have had to carry the evidentiary weight. METFORAGING is one of the more rigorous of these: a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled pilot testing 850 mg metformin twice daily for 96 weeks in older, non-diabetic, virologically suppressed adults living with HIV (a population of interest because HIV infection is itself associated with accelerated epigenetic aging), with PhenoAge epigenetic age acceleration as the pre-registered primary outcome. The result, reported plainly by the trial's own authors: the adjusted between-group difference was -1.02 years (95% CI -5.30 to 3.26, p = 0.627) — not statistically significant. Every one of the epigenetic biomarkers measured trended in metformin's favor, and the drug was safe and well tolerated over nearly two years, but the trial's own conclusion is that this is an exploratory, hypothesis-generating result that "warrants a larger, adequately powered trial" — not a demonstration that metformin slows aging. That is the honest state of metformin evidence in 2026: plausible, safe, still unproven at the scale that would justify the drug's popular reputation.

Senolytics sit somewhere between the two, evidence-wise, but the Khosla, Monroe & Farr perspective makes a different kind of point than a single trial result: the problem right now may be trial design itself, not just insufficient data. As of their 2025 stock-take, only 9 controlled or uncontrolled human senolytic trials had been published in total, and just 2 of those included a control group — a strikingly thin evidence base for a mechanism (Day 2) with such strong mouse data behind it. Early trials of dasatinib plus quercetin (D+Q) in diabetic kidney disease and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, already covered on Day 2, showed the drugs could reduce senescent-cell markers in human tissue within days to weeks; more recent small pilot trials — including studies testing D+Q and fisetin (a plant-derived senolytic) in people with mild cognitive impairment or knee osteoarthritis — have reported similar short-term biological effects and no major safety signals, but nearly all remain small, uncontrolled, or short in duration. The authors' proposed fix connects directly back to the SASP biology from Day 2: senescent-cell burden varies enormously between individuals and tissues, so a trial that enrolls people regardless of how much senescent-cell burden they actually carry is statistically set up to dilute any real effect. Their argument for "personalized" senolytic trials — screening for senescence burden before enrollment, the way oncology trials now screen for a tumor's molecular markers before assigning a targeted therapy — is itself evidence of a field maturing past "does clearing senescent cells help" toward the harder, more useful question of who it helps and how much.

Stepping back, a pattern runs through all three drug classes that is worth naming explicitly: the size of the evidence gap tracks inversely with how commercially attractive the drug is. Rapamycin analogues and purpose-built senolytics have patent potential and industry interest, which is part of why trial infrastructure for them has grown quickly even without huge sample sizes. Metformin, already generic and pennies a dose, has the strongest epidemiological rationale of the three but the weakest path to the large trial that would confirm or refute it — TAME's twelve-year funding struggle is a case study in how a drug can be scientifically promising and commercially uninteresting at the same time. None of this is a reason to dismiss any of the three; it is a reason to notice that "how much trial evidence exists" is not a pure function of "how promising the biology is," and to read popular claims about all three drugs — often stated more confidently than the trials themselves state their own results — with that distinction in mind.

## Questions to think about

- METFORAGING's primary result was not statistically significant, yet every biomarker trended in the same direction and the authors call for a larger trial rather than treating the result as disconfirming. At what point does a pattern of small, favorable-but-non-significant trials become evidence in its own right, versus noise that a big enough trial would eventually wash out?
- TAME has stayed unfunded for over a decade specifically because metformin is off-patent and cheap — the same property that makes it appealing if it works. What would a funding model look like that could get expensive, decisive trials run on inexpensive, unpatentable interventions more generally, not just for aging?
- The Khosla, Monroe & Farr argument for "personalized" senolytic trials implies that some past trials may have shown a weak or null effect simply because they enrolled people who didn't have much senescent-cell burden to begin with. If that is right, how should a reader now interpret the negative or marginal results already published in this area — as evidence senolytics don't work, or as evidence the trials asked the wrong population?
