# The biology of aging — Day 4/7: Mitochondria, metabolism, and nutrient-sensing pathways

## Context

Day 1 introduced the hallmarks-of-aging framework and its three tiers. Day 2 and Day 3 worked through hallmarks that are relatively easy to picture as "damage": senescent cells piling up, telomeres fraying, DNA lesions and methylation drift accumulating. Today's hallmarks are different in character. Mitochondrial dysfunction and deregulated nutrient-sensing are not simple wear-and-tear — they are breakdowns in *signaling systems* that evolved specifically to adjust an organism's growth, repair, and energy use to how much food is available. Understanding why those systems exist in the first place, not just how they misfire with age, is the point of today's reading.

The nutrient-sensing pathways covered today — mTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin) and AMPK (AMP-activated protein kinase) chief among them — are ancient and evolutionarily conserved: essentially the same signaling logic operates in yeast, worms, flies, and mammals. That conservation is why a huge fraction of what is known about lifespan extension started in short-lived lab organisms: dietary restriction (eating less, without malnutrition) reliably extends lifespan across nearly every species it has been tested in, from yeast to primates, by acting through these same pathways. The evolutionary logic is a resource-allocation trade-off: when food is scarce, natural selection favors organisms that shift resources away from growth and reproduction and toward cellular maintenance and stress resistance — a "hunker down and survive" strategy that only pays off reproductively if the organism lives long enough to reproduce later, once conditions improve. mTOR and AMPK are the molecular switches that implement that trade-off.

Mitochondria sit downstream of this signaling network, which is why the two subjects share a day. They are not just ATP factories: they are also signaling hubs whose health is actively regulated by AMPK, and their dysfunction feeds directly into the damage accumulation described on Day 3 (via reactive oxygen species) and the senescence described on Day 2. Today looks at three things: how AMPK and mTOR sense nutrient availability and push cells in opposite directions, how mitochondrial function itself declines with age and why that matters beyond simple energy shortage, and what the best human evidence — not just mouse data — says about deliberately intervening on this system through caloric restriction. Day 5 will pick up directly from here, covering drugs (rapamycin, metformin) that pharmacologically mimic parts of what today's reading describes happening naturally under dietary restriction.

## Today's readings

**"Mitochondrial and metabolic dysfunction in ageing and age-related diseases"** (Amorim, Coppotelli, Rolo, Palmeira, Ross & Sinclair, *Nature Reviews Endocrinology*, 2022) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9059418/ — ~10 min (abstract, introduction, and the sections on mitochondrial biogenesis/mitophagy and the links to nutrient-sensing pathways; the disease-specific sections at the end can be skimmed). A peer-reviewed, open-access review in a top Nature Portfolio journal, co-authored by David Sinclair (Harvard Medical School, a leading figure in aging and sirtuin biology) — it is the standard reference connecting mitochondrial decline mechanistically to the broader metabolic hallmarks of aging.

**"AMPK: the energy sensor at the crossroads of aging and cancer"** (Penugurti, Manne, Bai, Kant & Lin, *Seminars in Cancer Biology*, 2024) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625618/ — ~8 min (read the introduction and the sections on AMPK's role in autophagy, mitochondrial biogenesis, and aging; the cancer-specific sections can be skimmed). A peer-reviewed, open-access review from a Duke University School of Medicine group; it lays out how AMPK acts as the cell's fuel-gauge, is activated when energy is scarce, and both promotes mitochondrial quality control and directly suppresses mTOR — the mechanistic hinge for today's reading.

**"Effect of long-term caloric restriction on DNA methylation measures of biological aging in healthy adults from the CALERIE trial"** (Waziry et al., *Nature Aging*, 2023) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10148951/ — ~6 min (abstract and results). A peer-reviewed, open-access analysis of CALERIE — the first long-term randomized controlled trial of caloric restriction in healthy, non-obese humans, funded by the NIH's National Institute on Aging — using the DunedinPACE epigenetic clock introduced on Day 3 as the outcome measure. It is the rare piece of *human* RCT evidence in a field otherwise dominated by mouse and invertebrate data, which is exactly why it anchors today's discussion.

## Guided summary

AMPK and mTOR are best understood as a matched pair of opposing switches, both responding to the same underlying question — is there enough fuel and building material right now? — but pointing cells in opposite directions. mTOR, specifically the mTORC1 complex, is activated by abundant nutrients (amino acids, glucose) and growth factors; when active, it promotes protein synthesis, cell growth, and proliferation, and it actively suppresses autophagy, the cellular recycling process introduced on Day 1. AMPK does the reverse: it is activated when cellular energy runs low, sensed through a rising AMP-to-ATP ratio, and once active it suppresses mTORC1 (partly by activating the TSC2 complex, partly through direct phosphorylation of mTOR's binding partner Raptor), switches on autophagy via the kinase ULK1, and promotes mitochondrial biogenesis by activating PGC-1α, the master regulator of new mitochondria production. In short: plenty of food pushes cells toward growth and away from cleanup; scarcity pushes cells toward maintenance and away from growth. The Penugurti et al. review is explicit that this is not a peripheral detail but the central mechanism by which dietary restriction extends lifespan across model organisms — and that AMPK activity itself declines with age, which independently weakens the cell's ability to mount this protective, maintenance-oriented response exactly when it is needed most.

This is also where the evolutionary logic flagged in the context becomes concrete. A cell (or organism) that senses scarcity and responds by boosting autophagy, DNA repair, and stress resistance while pausing growth and reproduction is making a bet that survival now is worth more, reproductively, than growth now — a bet that only makes evolutionary sense in environments where famine is real and recurring, which describes most of evolutionary history for most species. The same AMPK/mTOR circuitry that implements this trade-off in a single cell scales up to the whole-organism dietary-restriction effect that has been observed since the 1930s in rodents and later confirmed in yeast, worms, flies, and — more recently and much more modestly — primates and humans. The fact that essentially the same two proteins do this job from yeast to mammals is why mTOR and AMPK, rather than being a narrow biochemical curiosity, sit near the center of nearly every major aging-intervention research program, including the drugs Day 5 will cover.

Mitochondria are the downstream machinery this signaling network is partly built to protect. The Amorim et al. review frames mitochondrial dysfunction in aging as more than "less ATP": mitochondria also generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) as a byproduct of energy production, and while low levels of ROS act as useful signaling molecules, the review describes how, with age, three things typically go wrong together — mitochondrial DNA (which lacks the elaborate repair systems protecting nuclear DNA described on Day 3) accumulates mutations faster, mitochondrial biogenesis via the AMPK/PGC-1α axis declines, and mitophagy — the selective autophagic clearance of damaged mitochondria — becomes less efficient, so damaged, ROS-leaking mitochondria are not replaced or removed as effectively as they once were. The result is a self-reinforcing decline: fewer healthy mitochondria means less cellular energy for the demanding processes of repair and maintenance, while the damaged mitochondria that remain contribute oxidative stress that feeds into DNA damage (Day 3) and can help push stressed cells toward senescence (Day 2) — a concrete illustration of how the hallmarks of aging form a network rather than a list of independent problems.

The CALERIE trial is what elevates this from plausible mechanism to tested human evidence. Conducted at three US clinical centers and funded by the NIH, it randomly assigned 218 healthy, non-obese adults (aged 21–50) to either a prescribed 25% caloric restriction diet or an ad-libitum control diet for two years — the most rigorous human test to date of what sustained, moderate dietary restriction actually does, as opposed to what it does in mice. The Waziry et al. analysis measured biological aging in blood samples using DunedinPACE, the pace-of-aging epigenetic clock introduced on Day 3, and found that the calorie-restricted group's pace of aging slowed measurably relative to the control group over the two years, even though the trial was never designed or powered to observe actual differences in disease or mortality within its timeframe. The effect size was modest — this was healthy caloric restriction in normal-weight adults, not a dramatic intervention — but it is real, randomized, controlled, human data showing that a nutrient-sensing-pathway intervention can shift a validated marker of biological aging, which is precisely the kind of evidence the geroscience hypothesis (that targeting shared aging mechanisms can affect multiple disease risks at once, as Day 1 discussed) needs to make its case in humans rather than only in short-lived model organisms.

Two caveats are worth carrying into Day 5. First, CALERIE tested caloric restriction itself, not a drug — it is evidence that the AMPK/mTOR nutrient-sensing axis is a real, modifiable target in humans, but it does not by itself validate any specific pharmacological mimetic. Second, epigenetic clocks carry the same interpretive caution flagged on Day 3: a slowed DunedinPACE score is a strong, biologically plausible signal, but it is still a proxy for healthspan and lifespan, not a direct measurement of either — which is exactly why Day 5's discussion of rapamycin, metformin, and senolytics will need to weigh clock data alongside whatever harder clinical endpoints exist.

## Questions to think about

- The evolutionary logic behind dietary restriction is a trade-off: survival and maintenance now, at the expense of growth and reproduction now, as a bet that conditions will improve later. Does that framing change how you'd expect chronic (lifelong) versus intermittent caloric restriction to affect a modern human, who is not actually facing a famine that will end?
- AMPK activity declines with age, weakening exactly the maintenance response that seems most protective in old age. If a drug could restore youthful AMPK activity in an older person, would you expect it to fully recreate the benefits seen in young calorically-restricted animals, or might age-related damage elsewhere (Day 2's senescent cells, Day 3's accumulated mutations) limit how much a single restored pathway can achieve?
- CALERIE showed a real but modest slowing of biological aging from two years of moderate caloric restriction in healthy adults. Given that most people find sustained 25% caloric restriction very difficult to maintain, how would you weigh that evidence against a pharmacological approach that tries to activate the same pathways (AMPK) or inhibit the same pathway (mTOR) without requiring dietary change?
