# Quantum computing for the non-physicist — Day 1/7: What a Quantum Computer Actually Is (and What It Is Not)

## Context (2–3 paragraphs)

The word "quantum" has accumulated a layer of mysticism in popular culture that makes quantum computing harder to understand than it needs to be. Strip that away and the core idea is this: a classical computer stores and processes information as bits — each one definitively 0 or 1. A quantum computer stores information in qubits, which exploit the laws of quantum mechanics to exist in states that have no classical analogue. The payoff is not that quantum computers are universally faster; most tasks you perform on a laptop — writing a document, watching a video, running a spreadsheet — offer quantum computing no advantage whatsoever. The payoff is that for a specific and important set of problems, quantum computation can find answers in a number of steps that scales far more favorably than any known classical algorithm. Understanding which problems fall into that set, and why, is what this week is about.

It is equally important to understand where the field actually stands in mid-2026, because the gap between headline and reality is unusually large here. Quantum computers exist and can be rented over the cloud today. Google and IBM both operate machines with hundreds of physical qubits. In December 2024, Google's Willow processor achieved a landmark result: for the first time, a quantum chip demonstrated that adding more physical qubits actually reduced the logical error rate — crossing the theoretical threshold that error-correction theory demands for scalable, fault-tolerant operation. That was a genuine scientific milestone. And yet the same machine cannot factor a cryptographically relevant number, simulate a drug molecule of industrial interest, or solve an optimization problem that a classical computer cannot. We are in what researchers call the Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) era — past the stage of pure laboratory curiosity, not yet at the stage of practical quantum advantage in the real world.

This week builds the conceptual toolkit to follow that story intelligently. Today's reading establishes the foundations: what superposition, entanglement, and quantum interference are (and what they are not), the different physical technologies being used to build qubits, and a map of the field's current state. On Days 2 and 3 we go deeper into the physics of a single qubit and then into quantum circuits. Days 4 and 5 cover the algorithms and the hardware race. Day 6 examines the central unsolved engineering challenge — quantum error correction — and why the Willow result matters so much. Day 7 synthesizes the week into an honest assessment of timelines and applications.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

### The classical computer and its limits

A classical computer's power ultimately comes from transistors — switches that are either open (0) or closed (1). Everything a computer does, from rendering graphics to running machine-learning models, reduces to manipulating enormous arrays of these binary values according to fixed logical rules. The speed and memory capacity of classical computers have grown exponentially for sixty years (Moore's Law), but the fundamental operation — flip a bit — has not changed. For some problems, no increase in classical speed can help: the number of operations required grows so fast with problem size that even a computer using every atom in the observable universe as a transistor, running for the age of the universe, could not find the answer. Simulating the quantum behavior of a molecule with, say, 50 electrons is one such problem. Factoring a 2048-bit integer (the basis of RSA encryption) is another. These are not engineering failures; they are mathematical limitations baked into how classical computation scales.

### Superposition: the qubit's defining property

A qubit is a quantum system with two distinguishable states — call them |0⟩ and |1⟩. Unlike a classical bit, a qubit before measurement can be in a *superposition*: a genuine quantum-mechanical state that is, mathematically, a weighted combination of both. The weights are complex numbers called amplitudes; when you measure the qubit, you get 0 with probability equal to the squared magnitude of one amplitude and 1 with probability equal to the squared magnitude of the other. The superposition is not a statement of ignorance ("the bit is either 0 or 1, we just don't know which") — it is a statement about a physical state that has no classical equivalent. The difference becomes observable when qubits interact: interference between amplitudes produces effects that no probability distribution over classical bits can replicate.

With *n* qubits, the state space is a superposition over all 2ⁿ possible bit-strings simultaneously. This is why the "tries all possibilities at once" description is so tempting — and so misleading. The system does encode all 2ⁿ states at once. But if you simply measured all the qubits, you would get one random bit-string, drawn from the probability distribution defined by the amplitudes. To extract useful information, you must arrange the computation so that the correct answer emerges with high probability through interference — and designing that interference is the entire art of quantum algorithm design.

### Entanglement: the resource that enables correlation

Entanglement is a distinctly quantum form of correlation between qubits. Two qubits are entangled when their joint quantum state cannot be written as a product of individual qubit states. The simplest entangled state is the Bell state ½(|00⟩ + |11⟩): if you measure one qubit and get 0, the other will definitely be 0; if you get 1, the other will be 1. This holds regardless of the physical separation of the two qubits — a feature Einstein called "spooky action at a distance" and which has been experimentally verified countless times. Entanglement does not allow faster-than-light communication (measuring one qubit gives you a random result, not a controllable signal), but it is an extraordinarily powerful computational resource. It allows quantum circuits to create correlations across many qubits simultaneously — correlations that would require exponentially many classical bits to describe.

### Interference: the engine of quantum algorithms

Quantum interference is the mechanism that actually makes quantum computation work. Every quantum operation acts on amplitudes, not on definite bit-values. A quantum algorithm is designed so that the amplitudes associated with *wrong* answers undergo destructive interference (positive and negative amplitudes cancel), while the amplitudes associated with *correct* answers undergo constructive interference (amplitudes accumulate and reinforce). By the time the circuit is complete, measuring the qubits gives the correct answer with high probability. This is not a vague hope — it is a precise mathematical structure. Shor's factoring algorithm exploits the periodicity of modular arithmetic, using a quantum Fourier transform to make the period's signal constructively interfere; Grover's search algorithm uses repeated "amplitude amplification" to gradually shift probability mass onto the marked target. The key insight, stressed by the Quanta Magazine piece above, is that this carefully structured interference only works for problem structures where such cancellations can be engineered. There is no known way to apply it to arbitrary problems, and computer scientists believe (though have not proven) that many practically important problems — including most machine-learning training tasks — offer no quantum speedup.

### Physical implementations: five competing technologies

Qubits can be built from several very different physical systems, each with different trade-offs between qubit quality and scalability.

**Superconducting qubits** (used by Google, IBM, and Rigetti) are tiny electrical circuits, a few millimetres across, that exhibit quantum behaviour when cooled to around 15 millikelvin — colder than outer space by a factor of roughly 20. At that temperature, electrical current flows without resistance and the circuit behaves as a quantum two-level system. Gates are performed with microwave pulses in tens to hundreds of nanoseconds; the downside is that coherence times are short (typically tens to hundreds of microseconds) and the chips must live in dilution refrigerators the size of a small car.

**Trapped-ion qubits** (IonQ, Quantinuum) hold individual charged atoms — typically ytterbium or barium ions — suspended in electromagnetic traps. Laser pulses drive quantum gates with very high fidelity. Coherence times are far longer than superconducting qubits (seconds to minutes), but gate operations are also much slower (microseconds) and scaling to thousands of qubits while maintaining connectivity is an engineering challenge.

**Neutral-atom qubits** (QuEra, Pasqal) use neutral atoms held in arrays of optical tweezers — tightly focused laser beams. They can be reconfigured mid-computation, and the technology has recently demonstrated thousands of qubits in a single device with remarkable coherence. The entangling operations use Rydberg excitation (exciting atoms to high-energy states that interact strongly), and fidelity is improving rapidly.

**Photonic qubits** encode information in individual photons — polarization, path, or time-bin. Photons travel at the speed of light and do not require cryogenic cooling, making them attractive for quantum networking. The challenge is that photons barely interact with each other, making two-qubit gates difficult to implement deterministically; photonic quantum computing typically requires measurement-based or linear-optical approaches with significant overhead.

**Semiconductor spin qubits** use the spin state of individual electrons confined in semiconductor nanostructures (quantum dots). They are highly compatible with existing chip-manufacturing technology — a significant long-term advantage — but gate fidelities and qubit coherence still lag behind the leading platforms.

### Where we stand: the NISQ era and the path to fault tolerance

The term "Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum" (NISQ), coined by Caltech physicist John Preskill in 2018, describes the current era: machines with tens to hundreds of qubits, each subject to errors from decoherence and imperfect gates, not yet capable of error correction at scale. NISQ machines can perform impressive demonstrations — in 2024, IBM's Heron processor executed quantum circuits with up to 5,000 two-qubit gates on computational problems where exact classical simulation is infeasible — but demonstrating that these results carry practical scientific value requires careful argument.

The path forward runs through fault-tolerant quantum computing, which requires encoding one reliable "logical qubit" in many physical qubits using quantum error-correcting codes, and demonstrating that adding more physical qubits makes the logical error rate decrease rather than increase. Google's Willow chip, reported in *Nature* in December 2024, was the first experimental system to cross this fault-tolerance threshold for its error-correction code: a 101-qubit distance-7 surface code achieved 0.143% logical error per correction cycle, and — critically — each incremental increase in code size suppressed errors exponentially. This is exactly what theory requires for scalable fault tolerance. It does not yet mean fault-tolerant quantum computing is here; building the millions of physical qubits that useful algorithms would require is an enormous remaining challenge. But crossing the threshold is the first proof that the engineering path, in principle, is open.

## Questions to think about

1. The Quanta Magazine article argues that the popular description of quantum computing — "it tries all possibilities at once" — is misleading because it implies that simply having a superposition gives you the answer. Why does it not? What is the step that the popular description leaves out, and why does that step depend so heavily on the specific structure of the problem being solved?

2. There are at least five competing physical technologies for building qubits (superconducting, trapped ion, neutral atom, photonic, semiconductor). Each has different trade-offs between coherence time, gate speed, and scalability. Given that the history of computing involved a rapid convergence on one dominant technology (the silicon transistor), do you expect the same to happen in quantum computing? What factors might favour or disfavour convergence on a single platform?

3. Google's Willow chip crossed the fault-tolerance threshold for error correction in December 2024, yet fault-tolerant quantum computers remain years away. What is the gap between "crossing the threshold" and "having a fault-tolerant machine," and why is it still so large? What would need to be true for that gap to close faster than current roadmaps suggest?

## This week's theme

**Quantum computing for the non-physicist — what qubits actually are, where the technology stands, and realistic near-term applications** (2026-W31)

Quantum computers are not faster classical computers. They are a fundamentally different kind of computational device, built on quantum-mechanical phenomena — superposition, entanglement, and interference — that have no analogue in the transistor-based machines that have defined computing for sixty years. For two decades, quantum computing oscillated between breathless hype and practical obscurity. In 2024–25, that oscillation is resolving: key theoretical milestones have been crossed, the first demonstrations of "quantum utility" have appeared in narrow scientific domains, and the technology has moved unambiguously out of pure physics laboratories into engineering at scale. And yet the gap between the current state and the quantum computers that would break public-key encryption, simulate arbitrary drug molecules, or transform logistics optimization is still large — almost certainly a decade or more.

This week closes that gap for the general reader. No quantum mechanics background is assumed. We start from the physics of a single qubit, build up through gates, circuits, and algorithms, survey the competing hardware platforms, and examine the central engineering challenge of error correction. The goal is not to produce quantum computing engineers but to produce readers who can evaluate a press release about a new quantum chip with the same critical apparatus they apply to a clinical trial result in medicine: understanding what was actually measured, why it matters, and what it does not yet imply.
