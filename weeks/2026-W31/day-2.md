# Quantum computing for the non-physicist — Day 2/7: Inside a Qubit — Superposition, Measurement, and Decoherence

## Context (2–3 paragraphs)

Yesterday's reading gave us the wide-angle view: what quantum computing is, why it cannot be reduced to "trying all possibilities at once," and where the field stands in mid-2026. We established three phenomena — superposition, entanglement, and interference — as the pillars of quantum computation, and we saw that the current generation of hardware (NISQ devices) is genuinely impressive yet still far from the fault-tolerant machines needed to tackle problems of industrial relevance. Today we zoom in on a single qubit to make the first of those three pillars — superposition — concrete and geometric.

The central tool is the **Bloch sphere**, a visual model that converts the abstract mathematics of a quantum two-level system into a point on the surface of a ball. The model is not merely a mnemonic: it precisely captures how gates act (as rotations), how measurement collapses a state (as a projection onto one of two poles), and how decoherence degrades quantum information over time (as the ball's surface shrinking toward its centre). Understanding the Bloch sphere is the clearest path to understanding why decoherence is not just inconvenient but structurally catastrophic for quantum computation.

The second half of today's reading addresses decoherence itself — what physically causes it, how physicists characterize it with two timescales (T₁ and T₂), and why superconducting qubits must be kept at temperatures colder than almost anything else in the observable universe. These are not engineering quirks; they follow directly from the same quantum mechanics that makes the qubit useful in the first place. By the end of today you will understand not just *that* quantum computers must be extraordinarily cold, but *why*, in terms you can trace back to the energy of a microwave photon and the temperature of outer space.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

### The Bloch sphere: a geometry of quantum states

A single qubit has two basis states, written |0⟩ and |1⟩. In a quantum-mechanical system these are not two sides of a coin that you haven't looked at yet — they are two orthogonal basis vectors in a two-dimensional complex vector space. A general (pure) qubit state is written:

|ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2) |0⟩ + e^(iφ) sin(θ/2) |1⟩

where θ ranges from 0 to π and φ ranges from 0 to 2π. This parameterization maps every pure qubit state to exactly one point on the surface of a unit sphere — the Bloch sphere. The north pole (θ = 0) represents the ground state |0⟩; the south pole (θ = π) represents the excited state |1⟩. All points on the equator (θ = π/2) represent equal-amplitude superpositions that differ only in phase φ. The states |+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2 and |−⟩ = (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2 sit on opposite sides of the equator; so do |+i⟩ and |−i⟩.

This geometric picture carries three immediate payoffs. First, every quantum gate (single-qubit operation) corresponds to a *rotation* of the Bloch vector — a rotation by angle θ around some axis in the sphere. The Hadamard gate, which takes |0⟩ to |+⟩ and is used at the start of countless quantum circuits, is simply a 180° rotation around the axis halfway between the x-axis and z-axis. The X gate (a quantum NOT gate) is a 180° rotation around the x-axis, flipping north and south poles. Gate design is, from one perspective, a problem in choreography: how to rotate a Bloch vector from one point to another as quickly and accurately as possible.

Second, **measurement** in the computational basis corresponds to projecting the Bloch vector onto the z-axis. A qubit in state |ψ⟩ measured in this basis gives outcome |0⟩ with probability cos²(θ/2) and outcome |1⟩ with probability sin²(θ/2). After the measurement, regardless of the outcome, the Bloch vector snaps to whichever pole was measured — the superposition is destroyed. The qubit no longer occupies a point on the equator; it is at a pole. Measuring a qubit therefore fundamentally changes its state, in a way that has no classical analogue. A probability distribution over classical bits is just a statement of ignorance that measurement resolves without disturbing anything; measurement of a superposition *creates* a definite classical value that did not exist before.

Third, **decoherence** appears on the Bloch sphere as a progressive shrinkage of the Bloch vector toward the centre of the sphere. Whereas pure states live on the surface (vector of length 1), *mixed* states — states in which quantum coherences have been partially or fully destroyed — live *inside* the sphere. A fully decohered qubit has a Bloch vector of length zero (sitting at the centre), which corresponds to a classical probability distribution: 50-50 chance of being |0⟩ or |1⟩ if measured, with no phase information surviving. The shrinkage happens in two conceptually distinct ways, described by two timescales.

### T₁: energy relaxation

T₁ is the timescale over which the excited state |1⟩ decays to the ground state |0⟩. The Bloch vector contracts along the z-axis toward the north pole. In terms of physical mechanism: the qubit, being a quantum system at a definite frequency f, behaves like a tiny oscillating dipole that can exchange a photon with its electromagnetic environment. If the environment contains a mode resonant with f, the qubit can spontaneously emit a photon — the same process that causes an excited atom to glow. In a superconducting circuit, the "environment" includes the Josephson junction's dielectric interfaces, nearby microwave transmission lines used for control, and the residual electromagnetic modes of the chip's packaging.

The dominant known source of energy relaxation in today's transmon qubits is **dielectric loss from two-level systems (TLS)**: microscopic quantum defects, trapped at amorphous oxide layers on metal surfaces and at substrate interfaces, that resonantly absorb energy from the qubit. Think of them as unwanted tiny qubits embedded in the material surroundings; the qubit excitation can "hop" into one of them and then dissipate into vibrations. This is why the materials science of quantum chips matters so much — polishing surfaces, choosing the right oxide-free interfaces, and annealing to reduce defect densities are all active engineering fronts. A second important T₁ channel involves **quasiparticles** — single electrons produced when stray radiation (cosmic-ray muons, infrared photons leaking in from warm wiring) breaks Cooper pairs; these quasiparticles tunnel through the Josephson junction and relax the qubit.

In the best current transmon devices, T₁ spans roughly 100–400 microseconds for a chip of many qubits; in individual world-record devices (Princeton, 2025) values approaching 1.6 milliseconds have been demonstrated. Even 400 microseconds sounds plenty of time — but a two-qubit gate takes 30–200 nanoseconds, so a T₁ of 400 µs limits the circuit to a few thousand gate operations before the qubit has a good chance of having decayed. For a fault-tolerant algorithm, which requires millions of gates, T₁ must grow by another factor of several hundred at minimum.

### T₂: dephasing

T₂ characterises the decay of *phase coherence* between the |0⟩ and |1⟩ components of a superposition. On the Bloch sphere, this is a decay of the Bloch vector's length in the equatorial plane — the qubit remains in some mixture of |0⟩ and |1⟩ (its z-component is unchanged), but the phase relationship between them is randomized. Formally, 1/T₂ = 1/(2T₁) + 1/Tφ, where Tφ is the pure dephasing time. This means T₂ ≤ 2T₁ always: even if there were no dephasing at all (Tφ → ∞), energy relaxation alone would limit T₂.

What causes pure dephasing? Low-frequency fluctuations in the qubit's transition frequency. Anything that randomly nudges the frequency at which the qubit oscillates will cause the phase of the Bloch vector to drift irreproducibly. In superconducting qubits, the dominant source of this noise is **flux noise** — tiny fluctuations in the magnetic flux threading the SQUID loop used to make the qubit frequency tunable. These fluctuations are believed to arise from surface magnetic impurities (individual unpaired electron spins on the chip surface), and their spectrum is roughly 1/f — stronger at low frequencies, which is precisely where they overlap with the timescales of quantum circuits. Additionally, TLS defects (the same ones limiting T₁) also contribute to dephasing, because they randomly shift the qubit frequency as individual TLS defects flip.

In practice T₂ ≈ T₁ for the best transmon qubits, meaning that pure dephasing has been largely tamed and energy relaxation is now the binding constraint. This is an achievement of the transmon design: by making the qubit's charging energy much smaller than its Josephson energy, the transmon is made exponentially insensitive to **charge noise**, which was the dominant dephasing source in earlier charge-based qubit designs.

### Why quantum computers must be extraordinarily cold

The temperature requirement for superconducting qubits can be derived from a single comparison. A transmon qubit oscillates at a frequency f ≈ 4–8 GHz — in the microwave range. The quantum of energy at this frequency is:

E_qubit = hf ≈ (6.6 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s) × (5 × 10⁹ Hz) ≈ 3.3 × 10⁻²⁴ J

The thermal energy available in the environment at temperature T is:

E_thermal = k_B T ≈ (1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K) × T

Setting these equal gives the "critical temperature" above which thermal fluctuations are large enough to spontaneously flip the qubit at random: T* = hf/k_B ≈ 0.24 K (240 millikelvin) for a 5 GHz qubit. At any temperature substantially above this, the excited state |1⟩ is thermally populated — the qubit has lost all useful quantum state before you even try to run a circuit.

At room temperature (300 K), k_BT is about 1,250 times larger than the qubit energy gap. At liquid-nitrogen temperature (77 K), it is still 320 times larger. At liquid-helium temperature (4 K), it is 17 times larger — not good enough. At 1 K it is still roughly 4 times larger. This explains why superconducting quantum processors must sit inside **dilution refrigerators** — the only practical cooling technology that reaches below 20 millikelvin (0.02 K). At 15 mK, the thermal excitation probability is e^(-hf/k_BT) ≈ e^(-16) ≈ 10⁻⁷, meaning that on average only one qubit in ten million is thermally excited at any given time. The qubit is now in a definite, controllable ground state |0⟩.

A second reason for the extreme cold is **superconductivity itself**. The Josephson junction at the heart of every transmon qubit is a weak link between two superconductors; without superconductivity, the circuit behaves classically and no qubit exists. Aluminum — the most common material for Josephson junctions — becomes superconducting below about 1.2 K. But operating at exactly 1.2 K would leave the superconductivity fragile; dilution refrigerators' 15 mK provides a huge safety margin. A third reason is that high-energy radiation (cosmic rays, infrared photons leaking down control lines from room-temperature electronics) creates quasiparticles that destroy coherence. Reducing the temperature of the entire cold stage, and carefully filtering all electrical lines entering the cryostat, suppresses this source of quasiparticle poisoning.

The engineering challenge this imposes is immense: a state-of-the-art quantum processor hosting a few hundred to a thousand qubits must sit in a space about the size of a shoebox, at 15 mK, while being connected via hundreds of carefully filtered microwave coaxial cables to room-temperature control electronics at 300 K — a temperature ratio of 20,000. Maintaining this thermal isolation while driving high-fidelity gate pulses is one of the central engineering problems of the current hardware era.

### Decoherence as measurement by the environment

There is a deep conceptual unification between decoherence and measurement that is worth making explicit. In quantum mechanics, any interaction between a system and an external object that allows the external object to "learn" which state the system is in necessarily collapses the superposition — this is precisely what a measurement does. The environment (the chip substrate, nearby TLS defects, stray photons, flux noise) is constantly performing weak, imperfect measurements on the qubit. Each tiny interaction slightly collapses the superposition, causing the Bloch vector to drift toward the centre of the sphere. By the time T₂ has elapsed, the environment has accumulated enough information about the qubit's phase to have fully randomized it.

This framing clarifies why quantum error correction — the topic of Day 6 — is necessary and why it works. It does not reverse decoherence; it detects the *signature* of what the environment has learned, corrects the resulting error before it propagates, and restores the qubit to its intended state. The competition being run is between the speed at which the quantum circuit completes useful operations and the rate at which the environment extracts information that destroys them. ## Questions to think about

1. A qubit on the equator of the Bloch sphere is in a state like |+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2. If you measure this qubit in the computational basis (the z-axis), you get 0 or 1 with equal probability — it looks random. But the qubit's state is *not* a random classical 50-50 mixture; it contains phase information (the relative sign or phase between the |0⟩ and |1⟩ amplitudes). What is the experimental evidence that this phase information is real and not just a mathematical convenience? What measurement would distinguish |+⟩ from a true 50-50 classical mixture?

2. T₁ in the best current superconducting qubits is around 100–400 microseconds. A typical two-qubit gate takes 30–200 nanoseconds. How many gate operations does this allow, approximately? What does the ratio T₁/gate_time tell you about the scalability of error correction approaches, where logical qubit lifetimes must be extended by error-correction cycles running faster than decoherence?

3. The transmon qubit was specifically designed to suppress charge noise by making the qubit insensitive to offset charges on its Josephson junction. This design choice sacrificed something else: the transmon has more energy levels than just |0⟩ and |1⟩, and the spacing between them is not perfectly equal (it is "anharmonic"). Why is this anharmonicity crucial for the qubit to function, and what happens if the anharmonicity is too small?
