# Quantum computing for the non-physicist — Day 5/7: The Hardware Landscape — Superconducting Circuits, Trapped Ions, Neutral Atoms, and Photonics

## Context (2–3 paragraphs)

The first four days of this week built the conceptual scaffold: Day 1 introduced the three pillars — superposition, entanglement, and interference — and sketched the overall terrain between today's noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) machines and the fault-tolerant quantum computers of the future. Day 2 made the qubit physical: the Bloch sphere, the two timescales of decoherence (T₁ and T₂), and why a superconducting processor must be cooled to 15 millikelvin. Day 3 assembled qubits into circuits via quantum gates. Day 4 put those circuits to work on Shor's algorithm, Grover's search, and the variational algorithms designed for today's noisy hardware.

Today we ask the most concrete question of the week: how are qubits actually built? A quantum computer is not an abstraction — it is a physical object, and the choice of physical substrate determines everything: how long a qubit holds its quantum state, how precisely gates can be applied, how many qubits can be controlled simultaneously, and what engineering challenges stand between the present and a fault-tolerant machine. Four platforms have emerged as serious contenders: **superconducting circuits** (the approach pursued by IBM and Google), **trapped ions** (IonQ, Quantinuum), **neutral atoms** (QuEra, Pasqal), and **photonic systems** (PsiQuantum, Xanadu). Each encodes quantum information in a different physical system, exploits different quantum phenomena to perform gates, and faces a distinct set of engineering obstacles.

The central insight of today's reading is that all four platforms are navigating the same underlying tension: **qubit quality versus scalability**. Quality means long coherence times and high-fidelity gates — a qubit that does what you ask it to do without being disturbed by the environment. Scalability means building many such qubits and controlling them simultaneously. The platforms that lead in qubit quality (trapped ions) are not yet the leaders in qubit count; the platforms that lead in qubit count (superconducting circuits) are pushing hard on quality. Neutral atoms have entered the race with a striking property: reconfigurable connectivity that gives them the potential to combine quality and scale in a way neither superconducting nor trapped-ion systems can easily replicate. Understanding where each platform currently stands, and why, is the bridge from the theoretical machinery of Days 1–4 to Day 6's deep dive into quantum error correction.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

### The universal constraint: qubits must be isolated but also controlled

Before diving into each platform, it is worth stating the problem they all face in the same terms. Day 2 introduced decoherence — the loss of quantum information through interaction with the environment. The environment here means anything: stray electromagnetic fields, thermal photons, vibrations, neighboring impurities in the material, or even the control electronics used to apply gates. Every physical qubit interacts with its environment to some degree, and that interaction collapses superpositions and destroys entanglement on a timescale measured by T₁ (energy relaxation) and T₂ (phase decoherence).

The engineering demand is paradoxical: **a qubit must be simultaneously isolated and controlled**. It must be isolated enough from its environment that it holds quantum information for long enough to complete the intended computation. But it must also be coupled, controllably, to external signals so that gates can be applied — which means there is necessarily a channel through which the environment can disturb it. Every platform's engineering is ultimately about managing this paradox: shielding the qubit from everything except the signals you deliberately send.

Two numbers define a qubit's practical worth. **Gate fidelity** (usually quoted for two-qubit gates, the harder case) is the probability that a gate operation produces the intended output; 99.9% fidelity means one error per thousand operations. **The ratio of coherence time to gate time** tells you how many gates you can run before the qubit decoheres. A qubit with T₂ = 100 microseconds and gates that take 100 nanoseconds allows at most 1,000 gates before the quantum state degrades — roughly the circuit depth budget. Improving this ratio is one of the central engineering challenges in the field.

### Platform 1 — Superconducting qubits: speed and industrial scale

Superconducting qubits were introduced briefly in Day 2, where we saw that they must be cooled to ~15 millikelvin in a dilution refrigerator — colder than deep space — because at those temperatures, macroscopic metal circuits exhibit quantum behaviour. The key physical ingredient is the **Josephson junction**: a thin oxide barrier sandwiched between two superconducting metals (typically aluminium). Below the superconducting transition temperature, electrons form Cooper pairs that tunnel coherently through the barrier. The junction acts as a nonlinear inductor — its inductance depends on the current through it — which is the essential ingredient for creating an anharmonic oscillator whose energy levels are unevenly spaced.

This unevenness matters because a two-level quantum system (a qubit) requires that the energy gap between levels |0⟩ and |1⟩ be different from the gap between |1⟩ and |2⟩. If the gaps were equal (a simple harmonic oscillator), a microwave pulse resonant with the |0⟩→|1⟩ transition would also drive the |1⟩→|2⟩ transition, driving the qubit out of the computational space. The Josephson nonlinearity introduces an anharmonicity of roughly 200–300 MHz, enough to address the |0⟩–|1⟩ transition selectively with microwave pulses while leaving |1⟩–|2⟩ undriven.

The dominant superconducting qubit design today is the **transmon** — a Cooper pair box shunted by a large external capacitor. The large capacitance dramatically reduces the qubit's sensitivity to charge noise (random fluctuations of stray electric charges on the chip), which was the main source of decoherence in earlier designs, at the cost of a smaller anharmonicity. IBM's processor family and Google's Sycamore and Willow chips all use transmon-based architectures.

**Current performance (2024–2025):** IBM's Heron R2 processor (156 qubits, heavy-hexagonal lattice topology) achieves T₁ times of approximately 175 microseconds and T₂ times of roughly 110 microseconds, with single-qubit gate fidelities around 99.9% and median two-qubit gate error rates of ~3×10⁻³. Gate times are in the range of tens of nanoseconds for single-qubit operations and 100–300 nanoseconds for two-qubit gates. This gives a coherence-to-gate-time ratio of roughly 1,000–5,000 operations — enough for the circuits studied in Day 4's IBM quantum utility experiment.

**The key strengths of superconducting qubits are speed and industrial momentum.** Gate times in the nanosecond range mean that a syndrome cycle for quantum error correction (which requires many gates) can be completed in roughly one microsecond — far faster than any competing platform. IBM and Google have decade-long industrial supply chains for fabricating superconducting chips using standard semiconductor processes, and both operate cloud-accessible quantum computers used by thousands of researchers worldwide.

**The key weaknesses are cooling overhead and chip-level connectivity.** Every superconducting quantum processor requires a dilution refrigerator — a $1–2 million instrument the size of a large wardrobe that must run continuously, consuming significant electrical power. Scaling to millions of physical qubits (what fault-tolerant error correction will eventually require) means either engineering dramatically more compact cryogenic infrastructure or interconnecting multiple processors. Connectivity is also limited by the physical layout of the chip: in IBM's heavy-hexagonal topology, each qubit is connected to only two or three neighbors, which requires swap gates (and hence more error accumulation) to implement arbitrary two-qubit interactions.

### Platform 2 — Trapped ions: fidelity and all-to-all connectivity

Trapped-ion quantum computers take the opposite approach: rather than engineering a quantum system from scratch, they use nature's own perfect quantum systems — individual atoms. The atoms of choice are **ytterbium-171** (used by Quantinuum) and **barium-137** (used by IonQ and others): heavy, stable ions with convenient optical transitions. Qubits are encoded in the hyperfine levels of the ground state — two magnetic sublevels whose energy splitting is in the microwave frequency range and is extraordinarily insensitive to environmental perturbation.

The ions are trapped in a **Paul trap** — an electromagnetic trap created by oscillating electric fields that confine the ions in a linear chain suspended in ultra-high vacuum. Cooling is done by laser: first Doppler cooling brings the ions to millikelvin temperatures, then sideband cooling reduces the ions' collective vibrational motion to near the quantum ground state. Once cooled and trapped, the ion chain floats in space with no physical substrate touching it — which is why trapped-ion coherence times reach seconds and even minutes, compared to the microseconds typical of superconducting qubits.

**Two-qubit gates in trapped ions work through the shared vibrational modes of the ion chain.** When two ions are coupled via a laser (the Mølmer–Sørensen gate scheme), they both interact with the same collective vibrational mode of the trap, creating entanglement that is mediated through the phonons of the shared motion. Crucially, because all ions in a chain share vibrational modes, **any ion can interact with any other ion** — there is no nearest-neighbor constraint. This all-to-all connectivity is a significant architectural advantage: circuits that require many SWAP gates on a superconducting processor can be executed directly on a trapped-ion machine.

**Current performance (2024–2026):** Quantinuum's H2 processor — 56 ytterbium qubits arranged in an all-to-all-connected chain — achieved a Quantum Volume of 2²⁵ (roughly 33.5 million) by late 2025, representing the highest quality metric by that measure of any commercial quantum computer. Two-qubit gate fidelities reach 99.9% across all qubit pairs, with single-qubit fidelities exceeding 99.99%. IonQ's EQC prototypes have demonstrated 99.99% two-qubit fidelities on selected pairs. These fidelities are roughly an order of magnitude better than today's best superconducting two-qubit gates.

**The central weakness of trapped ions is speed.** Two-qubit gate times are in the range of 100 microseconds to 1 millisecond — roughly 1,000 to 10,000 times slower than superconducting gates. This means that a quantum error correction syndrome cycle that takes 1 microsecond on a superconducting machine takes milliseconds on a trapped-ion machine — and since fault-tolerant quantum computers will need to repeat those cycles millions of times, the total runtime penalty is severe for some applications. Scaling the qubit count is also challenging: a single linear ion chain becomes harder to control as it grows longer (vibrational mode crowding), and segmented trap architectures that shuttle ions between zones introduce additional complexity and heating.

### Platform 3 — Neutral atoms: reconfigurable connectivity and the logical qubit frontier

Neutral-atom quantum computing has undergone the most dramatic rise in status of any platform over 2023–2026, driven by a single architectural innovation: **optical tweezers**. A tightly focused laser beam can trap a single neutral atom (typically rubidium-87 or ytterbium-171) at its focus through the optical dipole force — the tendency of a polarisable atom to be attracted to intensity maxima. Arrays of such tweezers, created by spatial light modulators or acousto-optic deflectors, can trap hundreds of individual atoms in programmable two-dimensional patterns. Crucially, these tweezers can be moved: atoms can be physically transported from one location to another between gate operations, enabling a completely reconfigurable qubit connectivity graph.

Qubits are again encoded in hyperfine levels of the atomic ground state, with the same advantages as trapped ions: very long coherence times (seconds), identical qubits (all rubidium atoms are exactly alike), and no physical substrate touching the qubit. Two-qubit gates in neutral-atom systems rely on **Rydberg interactions**: one atom is temporarily excited by a laser to a very high principal quantum number (the Rydberg state), where its electron cloud becomes enormous. In this state, two neighboring atoms interact via a strong dipole-dipole force that prevents both atoms from being in the Rydberg state simultaneously — the **Rydberg blockade**. This blockade can be engineered to produce entangling gates.

**The key architectural innovation** is the "zoned" processor design, pioneered by the Harvard group led by Mikhail Lukin. In this design, atoms are held in a large **storage zone** during computation, then physically shuttled by optical tweezers to a dedicated **entanglement zone** when they need to interact, then returned. This allows arbitrary pairs of qubits to interact regardless of their initial positions — overcoming the locality constraints of both superconducting chips and ion chains. It also enables a crucial feature for error correction: the ability to move freshly-initialized ancilla atoms into a circuit mid-computation, bypassing the need to reset qubits in place (which is difficult without disturbing neighbors).

**The January 2026 Nature paper** — Bluvstein, Geim, Li, Evered, Bonilla Ataides, Baranes, Gu, Manovitz, and Lukin et al. — reports the most impressive logical qubit demonstration published to date on any platform. Operating 448 physical neutral-atom qubits (rubidium atoms), the team encoded **96 active logical qubits** using a [[16,6,4]] high-rate quantum error-correcting code: each logical qubit is built from 16 physical qubits, encodes 6 logical bits of information, and can correct errors of weight up to 2. They demonstrated below-threshold error suppression — the same regime Google reached with surface codes on superconducting hardware — and showed that universal computation on the logical qubits was feasible. The ratio of 96 logical qubits from 448 physical qubits represents an encoding efficiency that no other platform has matched: every other system demonstrating error-corrected logical qubits uses many more physical qubits per logical qubit.

**Weaknesses:** Neutral-atom gate times are typically in the microsecond range — faster than trapped ions in parallel operation, but slower than superconducting qubits. Atom loading is probabilistic: each tweezer may or may not capture an atom from the surrounding gas, requiring real-time reconfiguration to fill vacancies. The Rydberg interaction is sensitive to atom position, requiring precise control of the optical tweezers. And the field is younger than superconducting or trapped-ion computing, with fewer industrial players and less supply chain maturity.

### Platform 4 — Photonic quantum computing: a fundamentally different approach

Photonic quantum computers use **photons** — individual quanta of light — as qubits, with quantum information encoded in properties such as polarization (horizontal vs. vertical, representing |0⟩ and |1⟩), time-bin (early or late arrival), or spatial mode. The core appeal is that photons are naturally quantum mechanical objects with very long coherence times (they do not interact with thermal noise at room temperature), and they can be transmitted through optical fiber over long distances — making photonic qubits the natural substrate for quantum communication.

The fundamental challenge is that photons do not naturally interact with each other. Two-qubit gates require two photons to interact, but photons in vacuum and in most optical materials simply pass through each other without effect. Producing entanglement between photons requires either highly nonlinear optical materials, ancilla photon measurements with postselection (the KLM scheme), or squeezed-light approaches (Gaussian boson sampling). All of these approaches are inherently probabilistic — they work only some fraction of the time — which requires adaptive correction strategies and very high efficiencies.

Two approaches dominate current research. **Xanadu** (Toronto) develops photonic processors using squeezed states of light and Gaussian boson sampling; their Aurora system (2025) demonstrated 12 error-corrected GKP-encoded logical qubits across 35 integrated photonics chips connected by 13 km of optical fiber — the first modular, networked photonic quantum computer with real-time error correction decoding. **PsiQuantum** (Palo Alto) is pursuing fault-tolerant photonic quantum computation manufactured using standard 300 mm semiconductor foundries, aiming at a million-qubit machine whose photonic components can be fabricated like microchips — a scalability path unavailable to any atom-based approach.

The photonic approach is the most speculative of the four: no photonic processor has demonstrated quantum gates or algorithms at a scale competitive with leading superconducting or trapped-ion systems. But its unique advantages — room-temperature operation, compatibility with semiconductor manufacturing, and natural integration with quantum networking — make it a serious long-term contender, particularly if the threshold for fault-tolerant photonic computation proves achievable.

### The hardware landscape as a competition for the same objective

It is tempting to ask which platform "will win." The more useful framing is: each platform is on a different engineering trajectory toward the same destination — a fault-tolerant machine with hundreds of error-corrected logical qubits capable of running the algorithms from Day 4 at commercially relevant scales. The timelines along those trajectories depend on which engineering challenge is solved first.

**Superconducting circuits** are closest to the industrial scale required, have the fastest gates (critical for error correction cycle speed), and have the broadest toolchain. The December 2024 Willow result demonstrated that the essential mathematical condition for fault tolerance — exponential error suppression as more qubits are added — is achievable with this hardware. The obstacle is that achieving a full logical qubit under realistic error models will require thousands of physical qubits per logical qubit in a surface code, and scaling cryogenic control infrastructure to millions of qubits is a formidable engineering problem.

**Trapped ions** have the highest gate fidelities and longest coherence times — properties that reduce the overhead of error correction (fewer physical qubits needed per logical qubit for a given target error rate). The obstacle is clock speed: a microsecond syndrome cycle that runs ten million times takes ten seconds on a superconducting machine and roughly 10,000 seconds on a trapped-ion machine at current gate speeds.

**Neutral atoms** have demonstrated the most impressive logical-qubit results as of early 2026 and have the most flexible connectivity. The obstacle is gate speed and platform maturity. If engineering improvements in Rydberg gate fidelity and atom-loading efficiency continue at the pace seen between 2022 and 2026, neutral atoms are a strong candidate to lead the first demonstration of genuine quantum advantage in chemistry or materials simulation — the applications targeted in Day 7.

**Photonics** remain further from the leading edge of practical quantum computation but have a unique path to scalability that may matter enormously at the million-qubit scale fault tolerance requires.

The honest mid-2026 picture: superconducting and neutral-atom platforms are engaged in a genuine race toward fault-tolerant quantum computation, with trapped ions close behind in quality if not in speed. The hardware frontier is moving fast enough that the relative standings visible today may look very different in two years.

## Questions to think about

1. The quality-scalability tension runs through all four platforms, but it manifests differently in each: superconducting qubits are fast but need extreme cooling infrastructure to scale; trapped ions have excellent qubits but slow gates; neutral atoms are reconfigurable but gate fidelities lag behind trapped ions. If you had to bet on which engineering constraint gets solved first — cooling/control scaling for superconducting, gate speed for trapped ions, or Rydberg fidelity for neutral atoms — which would you pick, and what analogy from other technology development trajectories informs your intuition?

2. The neutral-atom [[16,6,4]] high-rate code demonstrated in the January 2026 Nature paper encodes 6 logical qubits in 16 physical qubits — a dramatically more efficient encoding than the surface code used in Google's Willow paper (which used roughly 100 physical qubits for a single logical qubit). Why might higher-rate codes matter enormously for the feasibility of fault-tolerant quantum computing at practically useful scales? What are the potential tradeoffs (if any) between code rate and other properties like noise threshold?

3. Photonic quantum computing operates at room temperature and is compatible with semiconductor manufacturing — two properties that would dramatically simplify the engineering path to large-scale quantum computers. Yet photonic systems are not currently competitive with superconducting or trapped-ion platforms in demonstrated circuit complexity. What does this gap tell you about the relationship between a platform's long-term scalability potential and its short-term competitive position? Can you think of analogous cases in the history of computing or energy technology?
