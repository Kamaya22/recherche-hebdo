# Quantum computing for the non-physicist — Day 7/7: Synthesis — What Is Settled, What Remains Open, and Where the Field Goes from Here

## Context (2–3 paragraphs)

This week built, layer by layer, a complete conceptual architecture for quantum computing. Day 1 gave us the landscape: superposition, entanglement, and interference as the three pillars, the NISQ era as our current location, and the distinction between noisy intermediate-scale machines and the fault-tolerant quantum computers of the future. Day 2 made the qubit physical — the Bloch sphere, decoherence, and why these machines must be the coldest objects in the human-built world. Day 3 showed how gates and circuits assemble quantum operations into algorithms. Day 4 examined the landmark algorithms: Shor's factoring algorithm and its cryptographic implications, Grover's search, and the variational methods designed to extract value from today's noisy hardware. Day 5 surveyed the hardware race — superconducting circuits, trapped ions, neutral atoms, and photonic systems — and the distinct engineering trade-offs each carries. Day 6 confronted the field's deepest challenge: quantum error correction, the threshold theorem, surface codes, and why Google's Willow chip crossing the fault-tolerance threshold in December 2024 was the most significant experimental milestone in years.

Today we synthesize. That means separating what the field has genuinely settled from what remains contested or unknown; taking an honest look at which applications are realistic in the near term and which remain speculative; understanding the cryptography threat and the response already underway; and constructing a sober timeline for when fault-tolerant quantum computing might actually arrive. The goal is not to replicate the week's individual readings but to stand back and ask: given everything we have learned, what is the most accurate picture of where quantum computing stands, and where it is going?

Day 7 is also the moment to name the field's genuine open problems — the questions that define the frontier — and to hand the reader tools for going deeper independently. By the end of this reading, you should be equipped not merely to understand news about quantum computing but to evaluate it: to recognise when a headline reflects a real advance, when it is hype, and when it is simply ambiguous because the question being asked does not yet have a clear answer.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

### The settled foundations: what this week established

The single most important thing to carry forward from this week is what quantum computing is *not*: it is not universally faster. A quantum computer does not outperform a classical one on arbitrary tasks — rendering a video, searching a database, training a neural network, running a spreadsheet, or solving a generic optimisation problem. Quantum speedup applies to a specific and narrow set of problems whose mathematical structure permits the careful engineering of quantum interference: amplitudes for wrong answers cancel destructively while amplitudes for correct answers reinforce. This structure exists for factoring large integers (Shor's algorithm), searching unstructured data with a quadratic speedup (Grover's algorithm), simulating quantum systems (the application that motivated Feynman's original 1982 proposal), and certain problems in linear algebra and quantum chemistry. Computer scientists believe — though have not proven — that it does not extend to NP-complete optimisation problems in general.

Within that narrower domain, three facts are now settled. First, quantum error correction works in principle: the threshold theorem guarantees that below a critical physical error rate, adding more physical qubits suppresses the logical error rate exponentially. Google's Willow experiment in December 2024 was the first to confirm this exponential suppression in a real physical system, a result described in Day 6 in detail. Second, we are in the NISQ era — machines with tens to hundreds of noisy, uncorrected qubits genuinely capable of non-classical computation but not yet of running long, reliable algorithms. Third, there is no consensus hardware winner: superconducting, trapped-ion, neutral-atom, and photonic platforms are all advancing rapidly with different strengths, and no single dominant technology has emerged.

### Realistic near-term applications: chemistry and materials simulation

Of all the promised applications of quantum computing, simulating quantum systems is the most scientifically grounded. The reason is fundamental: simulating the quantum mechanics of a molecule or material requires tracking the entangled state of all its electrons, a state that grows exponentially with system size on a classical computer. Even the world's most powerful supercomputers can simulate only small molecules exactly — larger ones require controlled approximations whose accuracy is hard to guarantee. A quantum computer, operating natively in the same quantum-mechanical framework, encodes this exponentially large state as part of its physical operation.

The practical caveat is scale. The problems of genuine industrial value — calculating the electronic structure of the iron-molybdenum cofactor at the active site of nitrogenase (the enzyme that fixes atmospheric nitrogen, a reaction humanity replaces industrially with enormous energy cost), simulating the dynamics of battery electrocatalysts, or modelling the binding of drug candidates with complex quantum chemistry — require hundreds to thousands of reliable logical qubits running algorithms for thousands to millions of logical gate steps. We do not have those yet. What variational quantum algorithms like VQE (the Variational Quantum Eigensolver) have demonstrated on NISQ devices is accurate simulation of very small molecules — a handful of atoms — alongside increasingly sophisticated demonstrations on slightly larger systems using error mitigation. Joint IBM-RIKEN work in 2024, combining a quantum processor with the Fugaku classical supercomputer, pushed those boundaries further; it is a proof of concept and a directional result, not yet an industrial tool.

The honest near-term picture is therefore incremental. Quantum simulation of chemistry will probably yield its first scientifically non-trivial, genuinely non-classical results in the early-to-mid 2030s, as error-corrected logical qubits become available at the hundred-qubit scale. The transition from "interesting demonstration" to "industrially impactful" will depend on which specific molecular problems prove amenable to the number of logical qubits available at each step of hardware development.

### The cryptography threat: urgent today, even if Q-Day is a decade away

Shor's algorithm, run on a fault-tolerant quantum computer with roughly a thousand to a million logical qubits (depending on algorithmic refinements and physical qubit quality), would break RSA and elliptic curve cryptography — the foundation of essentially all public-key cryptography currently protecting internet communications, banking systems, government secrets, and personal data. Estimates for when a machine of that scale could exist range widely, but most expert analyses place the earliest credible Q-Day in the mid-2030s, with many placing it later.

The pressing danger is not that future machine. It is the "harvest now, decrypt later" threat: encrypted data intercepted today can be stored and decrypted once a cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists. Nation-state intelligence services are documented to be pursuing exactly this strategy. Any data that is valuable today and will remain sensitive in 2035 or beyond is already at risk — the encryption protecting it must be replaced before that window closes, not after. For classified diplomatic cables, long-lived medical records, industrial intellectual property, or cryptographic keys embedded in long-lived infrastructure, the effective threat is not a decade away. It is now.

NIST responded with a multi-year standardisation process. In August 2024, it published its first three post-quantum cryptographic standards as Federal Information Processing Standards (FIPS):

- **ML-KEM** (FIPS 203), derived from CRYSTALS-Kyber — a key encapsulation mechanism based on the hardness of module-lattice problems.
- **ML-DSA** (FIPS 204), derived from CRYSTALS-Dilithium — a digital signature scheme with lattice-based security.
- **SLH-DSA** (FIPS 205), derived from SPHINCS+ — a stateless hash-based digital signature scheme, with security resting only on the difficulty of inverting cryptographic hash functions.

A fourth algorithm, **HQC** (based on error-correcting codes), was selected for standardisation in March 2025. None of these standards relies on integer factorisation or discrete logarithm problems, which Shor's algorithm attacks; they rely instead on mathematical problems for which no efficient quantum algorithm is known. NIST is urging organisations to begin migration immediately, with a planned phaseout of RSA and elliptic curve cryptography — in US government systems at least — by 2035. The practical challenge is that cryptographic infrastructure is woven into nearly every layer of modern computing: the migration will be expensive, slow, and disruptive, which makes starting early the only viable strategy.

### The honest timeline

Industry roadmaps are incentivised toward optimism; academic forecasts are sometimes incentivised toward conservatism; the truth is somewhere in between. Here is the most defensible synthesis of current expert consensus as of mid-2026:

**Now through ~2028 — Advanced NISQ.** Current machines continue to improve in qubit count, gate fidelity, and connectivity. The best superconducting processors have hundreds of physical qubits; trapped-ion systems have better qubit quality but fewer qubits at scale; neutral-atom arrays have reached thousands of physical qubits. Demonstrations of "quantum utility" — computations that are interesting and not trivially classically reproducible — continue, but demonstrations of practical quantum advantage for a problem that matters outside the quantum field remain elusive.

**~2028–2032 — Early fault tolerance.** The first systems encoding tens to low hundreds of logical qubits appear. Error rates on logical qubits reach levels that allow algorithms to run for thousands of logical gate operations. Quantinuum has stated a public roadmap targeting universal fault-tolerant operation by 2030; IBM's comparable milestone targets the early 2030s. At this scale, quantum simulation of small but scientifically interesting molecules becomes genuinely competitive with classical methods.

**~2032–2038 — Scaling toward utility.** Logical qubit counts grow into the hundreds to low thousands. The first unambiguous quantum advantage for a practically important problem may appear in this window — most likely in quantum simulation of chemistry or condensed-matter physics. Breaking RSA-2048 would require roughly 4,000 reliable logical qubits running Shor's algorithm, which lies at the optimistic end of this period's roadmaps and depends on algorithmic improvements not yet realised.

**Beyond 2038 — Long-horizon applications.** Full drug discovery pipelines, large-scale materials design, and the most ambitious financial or logistics optimisation applications would require logical qubit counts and computational depths that are not yet on any published roadmap.

### Five genuinely open questions

**1. Will NISQ-era machines demonstrate real quantum advantage before fault tolerance arrives?** The variational algorithms (VQE, QAOA) designed for noisy hardware have not yet demonstrated a convincing speedup for a practically important problem, and there are theoretical reasons — in particular, arguments about the limitations of shallow quantum circuits — to think the noise may be a fundamental obstacle rather than an engineering problem awaiting solution. Whether the NISQ era produces genuine practical value before fault tolerance supersedes it, or passes without doing so, remains the field's most contested empirical question.

**2. Which physical platform will dominate?** Computing history involves rapid convergence on one dominant technology; quantum computing may or may not follow the same arc. Superconducting qubits have the largest engineering and industrial ecosystem; trapped ions have the best qubit quality; neutral atoms have achieved remarkable scale and mid-circuit reconfigurability; photonics has inherent networking advantages for quantum communication. The question may not have a clean answer — different platforms may dominate different application niches.

**3. Are there undiscovered quantum algorithms for practically important problems?** The landmark quantum algorithms (Shor, Grover, HHL, quantum phase estimation) were all discovered in the 1990s and early 2000s. Despite enormous research effort since, no new quantum speedup for a broad practically important problem class has been discovered. It remains unclear whether the quantum algorithmic toolkit is nearly complete for general problems outside simulation, or whether major discoveries await — perhaps at the intersection of quantum information and machine learning, optimisation, or differential equations.

**4. Can error correction scale without millions of physical qubits?** Current estimates for running Shor's algorithm on RSA-2048 using surface codes imply millions of physical qubits — each logical qubit requiring several hundred to several thousand physical ones. Algorithmic improvements to Shor's algorithm, alternative error-correcting codes (such as the bivariate bicycle codes IBM demonstrated in 2024, discussed in Day 6), and better physical qubit fidelities could reduce this overhead substantially. Whether the physical qubit requirements for practically important fault-tolerant algorithms can be brought within the range of engineering feasibility on a reasonable timescale is arguably the most consequential open question in the field.

**5. What does quantum computing reveal about the boundary between classical and quantum computation?** The formal question is whether BQP — the class of decision problems solvable efficiently by a quantum computer — is genuinely larger than BPP, the classical probabilistic analogue. If BQP = BPP, quantum computers would offer no computational advantage for any decision problem (though they might still be useful for simulation). If NP ⊆ BQP, quantum computers could solve NP-complete problems efficiently, with revolutionary consequences. Both containments remain unproven after decades of theoretical work. The answers define which future problems quantum computers can and cannot solve, and whether quantum speedup is a fundamental feature of the universe's computational structure or an artefact of specific problem formulations.

### Pointers for going deeper

**Most accessible entry point**: Scott Aaronson, *Quantum Computing Since Democritus* (Cambridge University Press, 2013) — the most intellectually honest popular account of quantum computing, written by a leading theorist. It uses quantum computing as a lens to examine deep questions about computation, probability, and the nature of reality itself. The prose is demanding but rewards patience; it contains no hand-waving.

**The standard technical reference**: Michael Nielsen and Isaac Chuang, *Quantum Computation and Quantum Information* (Cambridge University Press, 10th anniversary edition, 2010) — the canonical textbook, covering the full subject from basic quantum mechanics through error correction, algorithms, and quantum information theory. Familiarity with linear algebra is assumed; prior quantum mechanics is not required.

**Free lecture notes**: John Preskill's course Ph219/CS219 at Caltech (<https://www.preskill.caltech.edu/ph219/ph219_2024.html>) is the field's best freely available technical introduction. The notes are self-contained, rigorous, and updated more frequently than any textbook; the 2024 version reflects the current state of the field including fault-tolerance milestones.

**Hands-on learning**: IBM Quantum Learning (<https://learning.quantum.ibm.com>) provides free interactive courses from introductory to advanced and allows you to run circuits on real quantum hardware via the cloud.

**Ongoing coverage**: *Quanta Magazine* (<https://www.quantamagazine.org>) remains the best source for expert-written, reliably accurate reporting on quantum computing advances at the research frontier. Its quantum computing section consistently bridges the gap between press releases and peer-reviewed results without oversimplifying either.
