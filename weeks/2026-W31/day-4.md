# Quantum computing for the non-physicist — Day 4/7: Landmark Algorithms — Shor, Grover, and the Variational Frontier

## Context (2–3 paragraphs)

The first three days gave us the conceptual machinery: superposition, entanglement, and interference as the three pillars of quantum computation (Day 1); the physics of a single qubit and the enemy that is decoherence (Day 2); and the circuit model — gates, universality, and how quantum circuits are built from a small toolkit of rotations and entangling operations (Day 3). Today we put that machinery to work on the question that motivates the entire field: what can a quantum computer actually compute better than any classical machine, and why?

The answer, as of mid-2026, is precise and narrow. Three families of algorithms define the frontier. First, **Shor's algorithm** (1994): it factors large integers in polynomial time, breaking the mathematical foundations of RSA public-key encryption — an exponential speedup with enormous real-world consequences. Second, **Grover's algorithm** (1996): it searches unsorted databases with a quadratic speedup — meaningful but more modest, and provably optimal for unstructured search. Third, **variational quantum algorithms** — the Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) and the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) — designed explicitly for the noisy, short-circuit hardware of today's NISQ era. Each family illustrates a different regime of quantum advantage, and together they define the honest frontier of what quantum computers can and cannot do.

All three families share the same engine from Day 1: interference. Shor's algorithm engineers interference through a quantum Fourier transform that amplifies a hidden periodic signal. Grover's builds an "amplitude amplification" loop that rotates probability mass toward the target answer. Variational algorithms use a classical optimisation loop to coax a short quantum circuit into an interference pattern that approximates a ground state or solves a combinatorial problem. Understanding how each one works transforms quantum computing from an impressive slogan into an understandable physical and mathematical story.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

### What "quantum speedup" actually means — and for which problems

Quantum computing discussions are contaminated by imprecise language. "Exponential speedup" and "quadratic speedup" describe fundamentally different things, and knowing which one applies to which problem determines everything.

**Exponential speedup** means the classical algorithm's runtime grows exponentially in the input size while the quantum algorithm's runtime grows polynomially. The gap is not a constant factor; it is a diverging gap that makes the classical approach infeasible at large inputs regardless of classical hardware improvements. Shor's algorithm provides exactly this: the best known classical algorithm for factoring an n-bit number runs in sub-exponential time — roughly exp(O(n^(1/3))) — while Shor's algorithm runs in O(n² log n log log n). For a 2048-bit RSA key, the classical best-known approach would require resources comparable to the energy output of stars over cosmic timescales; Shor's algorithm, on a sufficient fault-tolerant machine, would factor the same key in hours. The gap is categorically different in kind.

**Quadratic speedup** means the quantum algorithm's runtime is the square root of the classical runtime — O(√N) versus O(N). This is significant but not exponential. For N = 10^12, a quadratic speedup reduces a trillion operations to a million — a factor of a million. But a trillion operations might take a classical computer one second, while a million quantum operations (with the overhead of quantum control) might take minutes. Practical advantage depends on constants, not just asymptotics. More importantly, quadratic speedup does not turn an intractable problem tractable: for problems where the search space grows as 2ⁿ, Grover's algorithm reduces the search to 2^(n/2) operations — still exponential, still out of reach for large n.

**No known speedup** is the case for most of what computers are asked to do: machine learning training, image recognition, video rendering, database queries, most numerical simulation. This is not a gap in our knowledge; it is a structural consequence of what quantum interference can and cannot be engineered to do. The problems where quantum algorithms win are those with specific mathematical structure — periodicity, quantum-mechanical symmetry, or structured satisfiability — that enables the design of an interference pattern where wrong answers cancel and the right answer emerges. The popular image of a quantum computer testing all inputs simultaneously and instantly returning the answer is wrong: without an engineered interference pattern, you measure one random answer from a flat probability distribution.

### Shor's algorithm: factoring through hidden periodicity

The conceptual heart of Shor's algorithm is a mathematical equivalence: **factoring an integer N is equivalent to finding the period of a simple function**. The reduction is classical number theory; the period-finding is quantum.

Choose any integer a with no common factor with N (for instance, a = 7 and N = 15). Define f(x) = a^x mod N — the remainder when a^x is divided by N. The sequence of values f(0), f(1), f(2), f(3), … is guaranteed, by Euler's theorem from elementary number theory, to be eventually periodic. For a = 7 and N = 15: f(0) = 1, f(1) = 7, f(2) = 4 (since 49 mod 15 = 4), f(3) = 13, f(4) = 1 — the period is r = 4. Now apply the classical number-theory result: if r is even and a^(r/2) ≠ −1 mod N, then gcd(a^(r/2) − 1, N) and gcd(a^(r/2) + 1, N) are likely to be non-trivial factors of N. Here: 7² = 49; 49 − 1 = 48; gcd(48, 15) = 3. And indeed 15 = 3 × 5.

For a classical computer, finding the period r when N has hundreds of digits is hard: the period could be astronomically large, and checking f(0), f(1), f(2), … until the sequence repeats requires exponentially many evaluations. A quantum computer can find the period efficiently because the function f(x) = a^x mod N, computed in superposition over all x simultaneously, has a Fourier spectrum dominated by sharp peaks at multiples of 1/r — just as a periodic signal has sharp spectral lines. The quantum Fourier transform (QFT) amplifies those peaks into measurable probability concentrations.

The quantum circuit works in four steps. First, prepare an equal superposition of all possible values of x — via n Hadamard gates, creating (|0⟩ + |1⟩ + |2⟩ + … + |2ⁿ−1⟩)/√(2ⁿ). Second, apply the function f(x) = a^x mod N in superposition — using quantum modular exponentiation circuits, this step writes the value f(x) into a second register while leaving x in the first, creating a superposition of |x, f(x)⟩ pairs. Third, measure the second register: this collapses f to one specific value f(x₀), which simultaneously leaves the first register in a superposition of all x values that give the same remainder — and those values are exactly separated by the period r, so the first register now encodes a perfectly periodic quantum state. Fourth, apply the QFT to the first register: the periodicity is Fourier-amplified into sharp probability peaks at integer multiples of 2ⁿ/r. Measure; the outcome, combined with the classical continued-fraction algorithm, gives r.

The quantum speedup lives in step four: the QFT is implementable in O(n²) quantum gates, whereas the classical discrete Fourier transform of 2ⁿ values requires O(n × 2ⁿ) operations. The difference — polynomial versus exponential in n — is where Shor's factoring speedup originates.

**The cryptographic consequence** is direct and already driving global infrastructure change. RSA encryption encodes a public key as the product of two large primes; decryption requires knowing the factors. If Shor's algorithm can factor efficiently, it can reconstruct any RSA private key from the public key, compromising retroactively every message ever encrypted with that key. This vulnerability is not hypothetical — it is mathematically proven. The open question is when a fault-tolerant quantum computer large enough to run Shor's algorithm at cryptographically relevant key sizes will exist; estimates cluster between 2035 and 2045. NIST's August 2024 finalization of three new post-quantum cryptographic standards — FIPS 203 (ML-KEM, based on lattice problems resistant to Shor's algorithm), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA), and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA) — is the standards community's response: migrate now, before the threat arrives.

### Grover's algorithm: amplitude amplification as geometry

Grover's algorithm (1996) solves a simpler-sounding but broadly applicable problem: given a black-box function that marks exactly one item out of N as "the target," find the target using as few evaluations of the function as possible.

Classically, with no information about which item is marked, you must query items one by one — O(N) queries on average, O(N) queries in the worst case. Grover's algorithm finds the target with high probability using only O(√N) quantum queries, by repeatedly engineering a specific interference pattern that shifts probability amplitude toward the target item.

The algorithm begins by applying Hadamard gates to all n qubits, creating an equal superposition of all N = 2ⁿ basis states, each with amplitude 1/√N and probability 1/N. Then it repeats a "Grover iteration" approximately π√N/4 times — the right stopping point derived from the rotation geometry below. Each iteration consists of two steps:

**Oracle query**: A quantum oracle applies a phase flip to the marked item's amplitude — multiplying it by −1, leaving all others unchanged. This is the phase kickback mechanism introduced in Day 3's discussion of Deutsch's algorithm: the oracle is a unitary gate, and its action changes the sign of one amplitude without changing any measurement probabilities *yet*.

**Diffusion step**: A specific unitary, built from Hadamard gates and a conditional phase flip, reflects all amplitudes about their current mean. Because the marked item's amplitude is now negative (below the mean), reflection pushes it upward; all other amplitudes are slightly above the mean and are pushed slightly down. After one full iteration, the marked item's amplitude has increased at the expense of all others.

The geometry makes this precise: the state space can be parameterised by a single angle in a two-dimensional subspace (the "good" subspace containing the target, and the "bad" subspace containing everything else). The equal superposition starts at angle θ from the target state, where sin(θ) = 1/√N. Each Grover iteration rotates the state vector by 2θ toward the target. After approximately π/(4θ) ≈ π√N/4 iterations, the state vector is almost aligned with the target, and measurement yields the correct answer with probability close to 1.

Grover's quadratic speedup is **provably optimal**: no quantum algorithm can search an unstructured space of N items in fewer than Ω(√N) quantum queries, by a lower bound due to Bennett, Bernstein, Brassard, and Vazirani (1997). This optimality result is important for understanding quantum computing's limits. It implies that NP-complete problems — whose search spaces have no mathematical structure analogous to the periodicity that Shor exploits — can be searched quadratically faster by quantum computers, but not exponentially faster. Solving 3-SAT on n variables requires O(2^(n/2)) quantum operations by Grover, compared to O(2ⁿ) classically — a meaningful speedup, but still exponential, still intractable at large n. The widely-held belief that NP is not contained in BQP (the class of problems quantum computers can solve efficiently) rests partly on this result.

For **symmetric cryptography** (like AES), Grover's quadratic speedup has a concrete implication: a 128-bit key that provides security of O(2^128) operations against a classical brute-force attack provides only O(2^64) operations of security against a quantum adversary using Grover's algorithm. The standard recommendation — which NIST adopted — is to double symmetric key lengths (128 bits → 256 bits) to restore 128-bit security in a post-quantum world. This is a much smaller change than the wholesale replacement of asymmetric cryptography required by Shor's threat to RSA.

### Variational algorithms: NISQ-era pragmatism

Shor's and Grover's algorithms require **fault-tolerant** quantum hardware: circuits far deeper than any current machine can execute before decoherence corrupts the answer. In the NISQ era — machines with ~50–1000 qubits, each subject to error rates of 0.1–1% per gate, without error correction — these algorithms cannot run at meaningful scales.

This gap motivated a different design philosophy: rather than computing a definitive answer in a single deep circuit, **variational quantum algorithms** use a short parametrized quantum circuit as an approximation function, and optimize its parameters using a classical feedback loop. The quantum hardware handles the parts of the computation that are classically intractable — preparing and measuring quantum states in an exponentially large Hilbert space — while a classical computer handles the optimization of parameters.

**The Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE)** targets the ground-state energy problem: given the Hamiltonian H (the energy operator) of a molecule, find the quantum state of minimum energy. This is the central problem in quantum chemistry: knowing a molecule's ground state gives you its geometry, its bonds, and its reactivity. Classical algorithms (coupled cluster, density functional theory) approximate the ground state, but they scale poorly with the number of strongly correlated electrons. VQE works as follows: a parametrized quantum circuit (the "ansatz") prepares a trial state |ψ(θ)⟩; the quantum hardware estimates the energy expectation value ⟨ψ(θ)|H|ψ(θ)⟩ by repeated measurement and averaging; a classical optimiser (gradient descent, COBYLA, or similar) updates θ to reduce the estimated energy; the loop repeats until convergence. The variational principle of quantum mechanics guarantees that the true ground-state energy is always a lower bound on ⟨ψ(θ)|H|ψ(θ)⟩ for any state, so the optimization is in principle well-posed.

**The Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)** targets combinatorial optimization: given n binary variables and an objective function to maximise (for example, the number of satisfied clauses in a Boolean formula, or the total weight of a graph cut), find an assignment that approximately maximises the objective. QAOA prepares an initial equal superposition of all 2ⁿ binary assignments, then alternates between two layers: a "problem unitary" that encodes the objective function as phase rotations (marking high-scoring assignments with larger phases), and a "mixing unitary" that explores the space of assignments. The depth parameter p controls how many alternating layers are applied — deeper circuits approximate the optimal solution better but require more gates and accumulate more noise. A classical optimiser tunes the angle parameters.

The review by Cerezo et al. in *Nature Reviews Physics* is candid about the central challenge facing both algorithms: the **barren plateau** phenomenon. As the number of qubits grows and circuit depth increases, the gradient of the cost function with respect to the circuit parameters decreases exponentially — the optimization landscape becomes exponentially flat, making gradient-based training infeasible at scale. Multiple strategies for mitigating barren plateaus have been proposed (problem-specific ansatz design, layer-by-layer training, local cost functions), but whether any approach enables VQE or QAOA to outperform the best classical algorithms at practically useful problem sizes remains an open research question as of 2025-26.

### The quantum utility debate: locating the frontier precisely

IBM's 2023 *Nature* paper on "quantum utility" illustrates how precisely — and how actively — the frontier between classically tractable and classically intractable is being defined. The IBM experiment simulated the kicked Ising model — a simplified quantum magnet — on a 127-qubit Eagle processor, using circuits with up to 2,880 two-qubit gates. At this depth, exact classical simulation (tracking the full quantum state vector across 2^127 dimensions) is impossible with any existing hardware. IBM applied sophisticated error mitigation techniques to extract physically meaningful expectation values and argued that the results exceeded what the best available approximate classical methods (matrix product states, tensor network methods) could achieve.

The scientific community's response was swift. A team at Caltech published in *Science Advances* (2024) a classical algorithm based on sparse Pauli dynamics that reproduced the same expectation values on a laptop in minutes, to higher accuracy than the quantum experiment. The conclusion: for this specific problem, the quantum computer's advantage was not that the problem was classically intractable, but that the right classical algorithm had not yet been tried. The competition was not "quantum versus classical brute force" but "quantum hardware versus the best available classical algorithm for this particular problem" — and the classical frontier advanced to match.

This outcome is not a setback for quantum computing; it is a precise cartographic exercise. It tells us where the frontier currently lies and forces a more careful question: what problem class *cannot* be classically simulated, regardless of how clever the algorithm? The answer most researchers converge on: strongly correlated quantum systems with no efficient classical description — certain materials near quantum phase transitions, certain reaction intermediates in enzymatic catalysis, certain molecular systems with more than 50 strongly entangled electrons. These are genuinely quantum-mechanical in structure, not just large classically-described systems. Demonstrating quantum utility there requires both better quantum hardware and problems where the classical frontier is provably bounded — a program that the field is actively building toward.

## Questions to think about

1. Shor's algorithm achieves an exponential speedup by exploiting the hidden periodic structure of f(x) = a^x mod N. Grover's algorithm achieves only a quadratic speedup for unstructured search, and this is provably optimal. Together, these two results suggest a principle: the larger the quantum speedup, the more mathematical structure the problem must have that a quantum algorithm can exploit through interference. Do you think this principle generalises? What would it mean for quantum chemistry (VQE) or combinatorial optimisation (QAOA) — does the structure of molecular Hamiltonians or graph problems provide the kind of structure that could support exponential speedup, or are these problems fundamentally more like Grover than like Shor?

2. The NIST finalization of post-quantum cryptographic standards in August 2024 represents a multi-decade transition of global internet security infrastructure, driven by a threat from a technology that does not yet exist at scale. Is this the right kind of planning horizon for technology policy? Can you think of other situations where society has successfully (or unsuccessfully) prepared for a technological risk decades in advance? What does the quantum cryptography transition suggest about how institutions should handle long-fuse technological risks?

Does this pattern — quantum claim, classical rebuttal — suggest that quantum computers will never demonstrate practical utility, or does it suggest something more specific about the *types* of problems where genuine quantum advantage should be sought?
