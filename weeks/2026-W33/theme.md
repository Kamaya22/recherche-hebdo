# Week 2026-W33 — The deep ocean

## Why it matters

The deep ocean — everything below 200 metres of depth — covers approximately 65% of Earth's surface and contains roughly 95% of the planet's total habitable volume by three-dimensional space, yet it remains the least explored large environment on Earth. After nearly seventy years of systematic deep-sea exploration with remotely operated and crewed submersibles, humans have visually observed less than 0.001% of the deep seafloor — an area roughly the size of Rhode Island (Bell et al., *Science Advances*, 2025). The deep ocean is not merely vast and poorly mapped; it performs irreplaceable functions for the entire planet. It is Earth's largest long-term carbon sink, storing roughly fifty times more carbon than the atmosphere and absorbing approximately 30% of human carbon dioxide emissions and 90% of the excess heat generated by those emissions. The biological diversity it harbours is staggering: a 2023 survey of a single deep-sea mining zone in the Pacific identified 5,578 metazoan species, 88–92% of which were entirely new to science — and that zone covers only a fraction of the abyssal plain.

Understanding the deep ocean has accelerated sharply in recent decades thanks to advances in remote sensing, autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs), and environmental DNA (eDNA) sampling. But exploration coverage remains grotesquely uneven: nearly two-thirds of all visual observations of the deep seafloor have been made within 200 nautical miles of just three countries (the United States, Japan, and New Zealand), leaving the Indian Ocean, the Southern Ocean, and the vast abyssal plains of the Pacific almost completely unseen. Meanwhile, the deep ocean faces accelerating threats from deep-sea mining proposals, climate-driven warming and acidification at depth, and the creep of bottom-trawl fishing into deeper waters. We are in a race between discovery and destruction in an environment we have barely begun to understand.

## Tentative 7-day outline

- **Day 1 (Monday): Panorama** — What the deep ocean is, its major zones and habitats, the staggering scale of what remains unexplored, and why it matters for climate and biodiversity.
- **Day 2 (Tuesday): Life under pressure** — The physiological and molecular adaptations that allow organisms to survive crushing pressures, near-freezing temperatures, and total darkness; bioluminescence; the surprising biomass of the mesopelagic "twilight zone."
- **Day 3 (Wednesday): Hydrothermal vents and cold seeps** — The discovery of chemosynthesis-based ecosystems in 1977 that overturned assumptions about the requirements for life; the biology of these oases and what they tell us about the origins of life and the possibility of life on icy moons.
- **Day 4 (Thursday): The ocean's biological pump** — How the deep sea sequesters carbon on geological timescales, the biological and physical mechanisms involved, and how climate change is disrupting this service.
- **Day 5 (Friday): Deep-sea mining — a collision of interests** — The geology of polymetallic nodules, cobalt-rich crusts, and seafloor massive sulphide deposits; what mining would destroy; the state of international regulation and the ongoing scientific debate.
- **Day 6 (Saturday): Exploration technology and the data revolution** — How ROVs, AUVs, landers, eDNA, and machine learning are transforming what we can know; the Seabed 2030 mapping project; what a comprehensive census of deep-sea life might look like.
- **Day 7 (Sunday): Synthesis** — What is scientifically settled versus actively debated; five open questions that define the frontier; where to go deeper.
