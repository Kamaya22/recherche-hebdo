# The deep ocean — Day 7/7: Synthesis — What's Settled, What's Open, and Where the Governance Stands

## Context

Six days ago this week opened with a single, disorienting number: after nearly seventy years of deep-sea exploration, humans have visually observed less than 0.001% of the seafloor below 200 m (Bell et al., *Science Advances*, 2025). Everything since has built outward from that fact. Day 2 showed how life survives the crushing physics of the deep through membrane remodeling, TMAO accumulation, and chemosynthesis. Day 3 showed that wherever specialists sample systematically — the twilight zone's fish biomass, hadal sediment's microbes — the overwhelming majority of what they find is new to science. Day 4 quantified the deep ocean's climate role and showed that its carbon- and heat-absorbing circulation is itself measurably changing. Day 5 turned to direct human pressures — mining, plastic, deoxygenation — and Day 6 asked how we know any of this at all, given that the technologies that map the seafloor (nearly 29% covered) and the technologies that let us see it (0.001% covered) are answering two entirely different questions.

Today closes the week by doing three things the daily format could not: separating what this week's readings show is genuinely settled from what remains open, following the governance story to a milestone that landed only weeks ago, and pointing toward where a reader who wants to keep following this topic should look next. The three readings below were chosen for that purpose — not new sub-topics, but forward-looking anchors: a 2026 roadmap that directly answers Day 1's observation gap, an institutional synthesis of the field's biggest knowledge gaps, and the governance framework that came into legal force this January while this project's Antibiotic Resistance week (2026-W32) was still on the backlog.

## Today's readings (~20 min total)

**1. Johannes KN, Poulton SE, Kennedy BRC, Bell KLC. "The Global Deep Sea Exploration Goals: A Representative Approach to Visually Observing the Deep Seafloor."**
(*Science Advances*, 1 April 2026 — doi: 10.1126/sciadv.aea8731) — ~7 min (read the abstract and the section describing the 10,000-site sampling design) — This is the direct sequel to the paper this week opened with: the same research group behind the 2025 "how little we've seen" audit (Ocean Discovery League, with Scripps and Boston University collaborators) now proposes a concrete fix. Using a spatially balanced, probability-based sampling design, the authors identify 10,000 target locations worldwide that, if visited, would nearly double the number of unique deep-seafloor sites ever visually observed and — crucially — produce the first *representative* (rather than coastally-biased) global dataset. *Science Advances* is AAAS's peer-reviewed, fully open-access sister journal to *Science*, applying the same review standards.

**2. Sander S, Tamburini C, Gollner S, Guilloux B, Pape E, Hoving HJT, Leroux R, Rovere M, Semedo M, Danovaro R, Narayanaswamy B. "Deep Sea Research and Management Needs."**
(European Marine Board, Future Science Brief No. 12, Ostend, Belgium, 11 April 2025 — doi: 10.5281/zenodo.14928917) — ~8 min (read the executive summary and the section on knowledge gaps across biological, biogeochemical, physical and geological deep-sea science) — An expert-consensus report, led by Prof. Sylvia Sander (GEOMAR Helmholtz Centre for Ocean Research Kiel) with ten co-authors from marine research institutes across Europe, commissioned by the European Marine Board — a federation of roughly three dozen European marine research organisations and funding agencies. It is the closest thing this week has to an authoritative, field-wide stocktake of what remains unknown and what research investment is needed to close the gap, freely available via Zenodo.

**3. Congressional Research Service (Library of Congress). "The Biodiversity Beyond National Jurisdiction (BBNJ) Agreement (or High Seas Treaty)."**
(CRS In Focus IF12283, version 7, updated 20 January 2026 — congress.gov/crs-product/IF12283) — ~5 min — A short, nonpartisan briefing from the US Congress's own research service, summarizing the legal instrument that governs roughly half the planet's surface: the high seas beyond any nation's jurisdiction, which is where almost everything this week has discussed physically sits. The brief confirms the treaty's entry into force on 17 January 2026, its environmental-impact-assessment framework, and its provisions on marine protected areas and marine genetic resources. CRS reports are the standard, citable reference US policymakers use for treaty status and are freely public.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

### What this week established as settled

Some things are no longer scientifically contested, even if they are not widely known. The deep ocean — everything below roughly 200 m — constitutes about 95% of the planet's habitable volume and around 65% of its surface area (Day 1). It absorbs roughly 30% of humanity's excess CO₂ and 90% of the excess heat trapped by the greenhouse effect (Day 1, Day 4), through mechanisms — the solubility pump, the biological pump, thermohaline circulation — whose basic physics is well understood, even where their future magnitude is not. Life survives the deep's pressure through specific, now-identified molecular mechanisms: membrane lipid remodeling, TMAO accumulation with a biochemical ceiling that the deepest known fish sits almost exactly at, and, at vents and seeps, chemosynthesis rather than photosynthesis (Day 2). And direct, measured human impact is no longer hypothetical: Stewart et al.'s before/after study of an actual 2022 mining trial found macrofaunal density fell 37% and species richness 32% within vehicle tracks (Day 5) — this is not a model projection but an observation.

### What remains genuinely open — and why the gaps compound

Three open fronts run through the whole week. The first is observational: Bell et al.'s 2025 audit found that of the roughly 3,000 km² of seafloor humans have ever visually surveyed, about 65% sits within 200 nautical miles of just three countries (the US, Japan, and New Zealand) — so even the small sample we have is not a random one. Johannes et al.'s 2026 follow-up, today's first reading, is the field's answer: a specific, actionable target of 10,000 representative sites rather than an open-ended call for "more exploration." The second front is taxonomic: the European Marine Board brief states plainly that roughly 90% of deep-sea organisms remain undescribed — a figure consistent with the 88–92% undescribed-species finding from the Clarion-Clipperton Zone alone (Day 1, Rabone et al. 2023) and the 89% previously-undocumented microbial genomes from Mariana Trench sediment (Day 3). The third front is dynamical: Rintoul et al.'s review of Antarctic Bottom Water (Day 4) documents real, measured slowing of deep circulation, but how that slowing will interact with the biological pump, oxygen supply, and carbon uptake over coming decades remains model-dependent rather than directly observed at the relevant timescale. These three gaps compound rather than sit side by side — you cannot fully assess mining or fishing impact on species you cannot name, in an ocean whose circulation you can measure changing but not yet reliably project.

### Governance caught up with some of this — but not all of it

The most concrete development since this week's theme was chosen is legal, not scientific. On 17 January 2026, the Biodiversity Beyond National Jurisdiction Agreement — the "High Seas Treaty" — entered into force after reaching 60 ratifications the previous September, per today's CRS briefing. For the first time, there is a binding global framework requiring environmental impact assessments before activities in international waters, and a mechanism (still being built out through 2026's Preparatory Commission sessions) for designating marine protected areas beyond any single country's control. This directly answers part of the precautionary problem Day 1 raised about the Clarion-Clipperton Zone: an evidence-based legal duty to assess impact now exists where none did before. What it does not resolve is the specific regulatory gap Day 5 described in detail — the International Seabed Authority's Mining Code remains unfinished after more than twelve years of negotiation, and The Metals Company's parallel pursuit of a NOAA permit outside the ISA process, which led to litigation at the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea in June 2026, shows that a binding biodiversity framework and a settled extraction regime are not the same thing arriving at the same time. Governance, in short, moved this year — but unevenly, faster on conservation obligations than on resource-extraction rules.

### Big open questions for deep-ocean science, going into 2027

1. **Can representative sampling actually close the observation gap, or only redistribute it?** Johannes et al.'s 10,000-site design fixes the *geographic* bias Bell et al. identified, but achieving it depends on a "distributed network of expeditions over the next one to two decades" funded by the same small set of national agencies and foundations Day 6 flagged as controlling exploration priorities.
2. **What is the true rate of species description needed to catch up with discovery?** With ~90% of deep-sea organisms undescribed (EMB brief) and new expeditions routinely finding the same ratio wherever they look, is taxonomic description accelerating faster than the rate at which mining, fishing, and climate pressure reach undescribed habitats?
3. **How will a slowing deep circulation and a warming, more stratified surface ocean jointly reshape the biological pump?** Day 4's modelling (Pasquier et al.) found productivity falling but sequestration efficiency rising under warming — a genuinely counterintuitive, still model-based result that needs direct multi-decadal observational confirmation.
4. **Can BBNJ's environmental-impact framework meaningfully govern activities — like deep-sea mining — where the required baseline biodiversity data does not yet exist?** The treaty creates a legal duty to assess; it does not, by itself, create the missing species inventories Day 1 and today's EMB reading say are needed to assess against.
5. **Will mesopelagic fisheries (Day 3's biomass-rich twilight zone, already being explored commercially for fishmeal and omega-3 oil) be regulated before or after they begin at scale**, given that this biomass is tightly coupled to the carbon export the whole planet depends on?

### Where to go deeper

For continued, freely accessible tracking: Ocean Discovery League publishes updates on progress toward the Global Deep Sea Exploration Goals (oceandiscoveryleague.org); the European Marine Board's Future Science Brief No. 12 is a full 78-page primer with a much more detailed knowledge-gap breakdown than today's summary allows; the BBNJ Preparatory Commission's sessions (the third ran March–April 2026) are tracked publicly by the UN and by the SDG Knowledge Hub; and GEBCO's Seabed 2030 project publishes annual bathymetric-coverage updates each June around World Hydrography Day, which is how Day 6's "28.7% mapped" figure will next be updated.

## Questions to think about

1. This week repeatedly found the same pattern — roughly 90% of what is sampled in the deep sea turns out to be undescribed, whether species (Clarion-Clipperton Zone, Day 1), microbial genomes (Mariana Trench, Day 3), or unvisited seafloor (Day 1, today). If a field's most consistent finding is "we don't yet know most of what's there," what does that imply about how confidently any single week's readings — including this one — should be treated as representative of "the deep ocean" as a whole?

2. The High Seas Treaty entered into force faster than the ISA's Mining Code has been finalized in over a decade of negotiation, despite both concerning the same ocean space. What does the relative speed of "protect broadly" versus "regulate a specific extractive industry precisely" suggest about how international environmental governance tends to sequence itself — and is that sequencing a problem here, or appropriately cautious?
