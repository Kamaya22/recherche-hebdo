# The deep ocean — Day 1/7: Earth's Last Great Frontier — A Panorama

## Context (2–3 paragraphs)

The deep ocean is the largest and least-explored ecosystem on Earth. By volume, the ocean's dark interior — everything below 200 metres, where sunlight fades to nothing — constitutes roughly 95% of the planet's habitable space. And yet, after nearly seventy years of concerted effort with crewed submersibles, remotely operated vehicles (ROVs), and camera sleds, humans have visually observed less than 0.001% of the deep seafloor. That figure — published in *Science Advances* in May 2025 by researchers at Ocean Discovery League, the Scripps Institution of Oceanography, and Boston University — is not a rhetorical flourish. It comes from a systematic compilation of approximately 44,000 deep-sea dives spanning 1958 to 2023. The total area visually imaged amounts to roughly the size of Rhode Island. The rest — an expanse equivalent to more than 65% of Earth's surface — remains entirely unseen.

This is week's starting point. Today's reading is a panorama: what the deep ocean actually is (its zones, its major habitats, its defining physical conditions), what the existing science tells us about its biodiversity, and why this seemingly remote environment is deeply intertwined with the climate, the carbon cycle, and the livability of the surface world where we all live. The reading is deliberately broad — subsequent days will go deeper into specific topics: the physiology of deep-sea life (Day 2), hydrothermal vents and chemosynthesis (Day 3), the biological carbon pump (Day 4), deep-sea mining and the political economy of the abyss (Day 5), the technology of exploration (Day 6), and a synthesis on Day 7.

One clarification of terms before we begin: "the deep sea" and "the deep ocean" are used interchangeably here, both referring to the ocean below the photic zone — the depth at which sunlight can no longer fuel photosynthesis, typically around 200 metres. The region from 200 to 1,000 metres is the mesopelagic or "twilight zone," where dim filtered light persists but is insufficient for primary production. Below 1,000 metres begins the permanent midnight: the bathypelagic zone (1,000–4,000 m), the abyssopelagic zone (4,000–6,000 m), and the hadalpelagic zone in the ocean's trenches (6,000 m and deeper). Together these layers constitute the largest living space on the planet, and almost none of it is well understood.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

### What the deep ocean is — and how much of it there is

The ocean covers approximately 71% of Earth's surface, with an average depth of 3,682 metres and a maximum of nearly 11,000 metres at the Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench. Ninety percent of the ocean's volume lies below 200 metres — the depth at which sunlight becomes too faint to drive photosynthesis. This means that the overwhelmingly dominant state of ocean water, by volume, is dark, cold, and under extreme pressure. At 1,000 metres, the pressure is about 100 times what we experience at sea level; at 6,000 metres, it is 600 times. Temperature in most of the deep ocean hovers between 0°C and 4°C (the exception is immediately around hydrothermal vents, where water can exceed 400°C).

The deep ocean is typically divided into vertical zones based on depth and the penetration of light. The **mesopelagic zone** (200–1,000 m) — sometimes called the "twilight zone" — receives the last faint traces of blue light. Despite receiving almost no photosynthetically usable energy, it contains, by some estimates, more fish biomass than all the rest of the ocean combined: organisms here perform the daily vertical migration that is one of the largest animal movements on Earth, rising to surface waters at night to feed and descending to depth at dawn. The **bathypelagic zone** (1,000–4,000 m) and **abyssopelagic zone** (4,000–6,000 m) are in permanent darkness. The **hadalpelagic zone** (below 6,000 m) encompasses the ocean trenches — the subduction zones where one tectonic plate dives beneath another — and has a remarkable fauna of its own, including the snailfish *Pseudoliparis belyaevi*, which holds the current record for the deepest fish ever recorded, caught on video at 8,336 metres in the Izu-Ogasawara Trench.

The seafloor itself supports several distinct **benthic habitat types**. Abyssal plains — vast, nearly featureless expanses of fine sediment — cover more than half the ocean floor at depths between 3,000 and 6,000 metres. Seamounts (underwater mountains, mostly of volcanic origin) number in the hundreds of thousands globally and support disproportionately high biodiversity relative to their area, serving as hotspots of current, productivity, and larval retention. Deep-sea canyons channel organic matter from shallow coastal seas into the abyss. Hydrothermal vents and cold seeps — the subjects of Day 3 — are chemosynthesis-based ecosystems that obtain energy not from sunlight but from chemical reactions, and which have upended assumptions about the minimum requirements for life.

### How little we have seen

The 2025 Bell et al. paper in *Science Advances* performed what amounts to a systematic audit of human visual exploration of the deep seafloor. The finding — that fewer than 0.001% of the deep seafloor has been imaged in seventy years of effort — needs to be understood in context. The total area of the deep ocean floor is roughly 335 million square kilometres. The entire area visually observed by humans from 1958 to 2023 is approximately 3,000 km² — less than the surface area of a medium-sized city. Put another way: if the deep ocean were a standard-definition photograph, we have seen roughly one pixel.

The coverage is not merely sparse — it is profoundly biased. Of all documented visual observations, approximately 65% were made within 200 nautical miles of just three countries: the United States, Japan, and New Zealand. This means that vast portions of the ocean — the entire Indian Ocean, most of the Southern Ocean, large swathes of the Pacific and Atlantic away from wealthy nations' coastlines — have been visited almost not at all. The science that has been built from deep-sea observation is therefore not a random or representative sample of the global deep ocean; it is a heavily skewed sample that likely reflects the peculiarities of the habitats close to a few nations' coasts and technological ambitions. Whatever generalisations about "the deep sea" appear in textbooks are generalisations from a vanishingly small and geographically unrepresentative dataset.

This has consequences for biodiversity estimates. The species known from the deep sea — around 27,000 described as of recent counts — are certainly a small fraction of those that exist. Deep-sea exploration consistently discovers new species: expeditions to previously unvisited regions routinely find that the majority of organisms collected are new to science, as the 2023 Clarion-Clipperton Zone study illustrates dramatically. And the CCZ is not unusual in this respect — it is merely better studied than most abyssal regions because of the economic interest in its polymetallic nodules, which has driven scientific surveys as part of environmental impact assessments for potential mining licences.

### The Clarion-Clipperton Zone: a case study in abyssal biodiversity

The Clarion-Clipperton Zone (CCZ) is a six-million-square-kilometre stretch of abyssal plain in the central Pacific Ocean, between Hawaii and Mexico, lying at depths of 4,000–5,500 metres. It is of intense commercial interest because its seafloor is carpeted with polymetallic nodules — potato-sized concretions of manganese, nickel, copper, and cobalt that precipitate from seawater over millions of years and that have attracted interest as a potential source of metals for batteries and electronics.

The Rabone et al. (2023) study synthesised specimen and observation data from all published CCZ surveys to produce the first comprehensive species list for this region. The results are remarkable. Of the 5,578 metazoan species recorded, between 88% and 92% are entirely undescribed — known only from specimen collections, without a formal taxonomic name, diagnosis, or species description. The true diversity is likely higher still: the authors estimate that accounting for under-sampling and the statistical properties of species-accumulation curves, the CCZ may support between 6,000 and 8,000 metazoan species in total. These include polychaete worms (which alone number over 1,000 species in the region), sea cucumbers, brittle stars, and a diversity of small crustaceans and foraminifera — single-celled organisms that can grow to centimetres in size and dominate the meiofauna of abyssal sediments.

The finding matters beyond taxonomy. International bodies currently regulating deep-sea mineral extraction under the International Seabed Authority (ISA) are required to consider environmental impacts and ensure the protection of biodiversity before authorising industrial mining. But it is extremely difficult to protect what you have not described. If 90% of the species in a prospective mining area have no scientific name, no understanding of their life history, no ecological network data, and no formal conservation assessment, the precautionary basis for protective measures is both urgent and scientifically thin. The CCZ study has been widely cited as an argument for delaying mining licences until a more complete baseline of biodiversity is established — a position endorsed by a growing coalition of countries and scientific organisations but contested by others on economic grounds.

### The deep ocean and climate: a hidden engine

The connection between the deep ocean and the global climate is not metaphorical — it is mechanistic and quantitatively substantial. The world ocean as a whole has absorbed approximately 30% of all the CO₂ humans have emitted since the Industrial Revolution, and approximately 90% of the excess heat that those emissions have trapped in the Earth system. Without this buffering, the atmosphere would already be significantly warmer and higher in CO₂ than it is. The deep ocean is the ultimate repository of both the heat and the carbon that the surface ocean absorbs.

The mechanisms by which carbon moves from the atmosphere into the deep ocean and stays there are multiple and interacting. The **solubility pump** operates through the direct dissolution of CO₂ into cold surface waters — cold water holds more gas than warm water — and the subsequent sinking of that dense, CO₂-enriched water as it moves toward the poles and eventually into the deep via thermohaline circulation. The **biological pump** is the focus of Day 4 but warrants introduction here: phytoplankton in the sunlit surface ocean convert CO₂ into organic matter via photosynthesis, and a fraction of that organic matter (particles, zooplankton faecal pellets, dead organisms) sinks out of the surface layer before it can be respired back to CO₂. Once below the permanent thermocline — typically around 500 metres — this carbon is effectively removed from exchange with the atmosphere on timescales of decades to centuries. At abyssal depths, carbon can be sequestered for millennia. The 2023 Annual Review paper by Siegel et al. estimates that the global biological pump exports somewhere between 5 and 12 petagrams of carbon per year across the 100-metre depth horizon — a flux comparable in magnitude to total human emissions from fossil fuels, though most of it is subsequently respired in the mesopelagic zone rather than reaching the deep sea floor.

The deep ocean is also implicated in climate on longer geological timescales. The thermohaline circulation — the global system of deep currents driven by density differences in temperature and salinity — distributes heat, nutrients, and carbon around the planet on timescales of hundreds to thousands of years. Its disruption by climate change (through the freshening of the North Atlantic as the Greenland ice sheet melts) is one of the tipping points that climate scientists most closely monitor. A significant slowdown or collapse of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) would alter rainfall patterns, temperatures, and carbon uptake across much of Europe and the Americas.

## Questions to think about

1. The Bell et al. (2025) paper shows that 65% of all deep-sea visual observations have been made within 200 nautical miles of just three countries. What does this geographic concentration tell us about how scientific knowledge is produced and whose oceans get studied? If the deep ocean is international space (the "Area" beyond national jurisdiction), what are the governance implications of this concentration of exploration capacity?

2. The Clarion-Clipperton Zone study found that 88–92% of species from a region targeted for commercial mining are unnamed and effectively unstudied. What standards of scientific knowledge should be required before industrial activity is permitted in an ecosystem? Is it sufficient to survey and assess *known* species, or does the scale of unknown biodiversity create a fundamentally different precautionary problem?

3. The deep ocean absorbs roughly 30% of anthropogenic CO₂ and 90% of excess heat — yet this buffering role is precisely what is now beginning to destabilise it, as warming reduces the efficiency of the biological pump and changes ocean chemistry through acidification. Does this suggest that protecting the deep ocean's carbon-sequestration function requires limiting the very emissions the deep ocean is absorbing? How should this be weighed in climate policy discussions that rarely mention the ocean?

## This week's theme

**The deep ocean — what we know about Earth's least-explored biome and why it matters for climate and biodiversity** (2026-W33)

The deep ocean — everything below the depth at which sunlight fades, roughly 200 metres — is the largest ecosystem on Earth and, by most measures, the least understood. It covers approximately 65% of the planet's surface and accounts for about 95% of its total habitable volume, yet humanity has visually observed less than 0.001% of the seafloor (*Science Advances*, 2025). What we have seen reveals an environment of startling biological diversity — the 2023 survey of a single economic zone in the Pacific found 5,578 animal species, nine in ten of them entirely new to science — and of enormous ecological and climatic importance: the deep ocean absorbs roughly 30% of human CO₂ emissions and 90% of the excess heat generated by those emissions, acting as a global buffer that has significantly slowed climate change at the surface.

The deep ocean's importance is matched by its vulnerability. Proposals for commercial mining of the manganese nodules, cobalt-rich crusts, and seafloor sulphide deposits that blanket the abyssal plain are advancing faster than the science of what those ecosystems contain. Climate change is simultaneously warming and acidifying the deep, disrupting the thermohaline circulation that moves heat and carbon around the globe, and reducing the efficiency of the biological pump that sequesters carbon at depth. And the global governance framework for the ocean beyond national jurisdiction — the "Area" — is only now being negotiated in earnest, with the 2023 High Seas Treaty representing the most significant international ocean agreement in decades. The science of the deep sea is thus not merely intellectually interesting; it is urgently relevant to a set of decisions being made right now, with consequences on timescales of centuries.
