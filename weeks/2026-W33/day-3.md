# The deep ocean — Day 3/7: Biodiversity — What Lives in the Dark

## Context

Day 1 established that the deep ocean is vast and barely observed; Day 2 showed how the animals that do live there survive the physics of crushing pressure, near-freezing cold, and total darkness — through membrane remodeling, TMAO accumulation, and, at hydrothermal vents and cold seeps, chemosynthesis in place of photosynthesis. Today's question is different: given those constraints, what actually lives down there, and how much of it is there?

The honest answer is that nobody knows the full extent, but the partial answer keeps surprising specialists. Two numbers anchor today's reading. First, the "twilight zone" — the mesopelagic layer between roughly 200 and 1,000 m, which receives too little light for photosynthesis but not yet none at all — appears to hold more fish biomass than the rest of the world's oceans combined, a discovery that forced a decade-long revision of how much animal life the ocean actually supports. Second, at the opposite extreme, sediment sampled from hadal trenches below 6,000 m — water pressures exceeding 600 atmospheres, no sunlight ever, and food scarcer than almost anywhere else on the planet — turns out to host thousands of microbial species unknown to science, hinting that "extreme" and "empty" are not synonyms in the deep ocean.

A second thread running through today's reading is light itself — not sunlight, but light that animals make. In most of the ocean's volume, any light that exists is light an organism switched on. Understanding how common bioluminescence is, and why it evolved so many times independently, is essential to understanding what deep-sea biodiversity actually looks like day to day: darkness is not the absence of visual signaling in the deep ocean, it is the medium in which a different, self-generated kind of signaling dominates.

## Today's readings (~21 min total)

**1. Claes JM, Haddock SHD, Coubris C, Mallefet J. "Systematic Distribution of Bioluminescence in Marine Animals: A Species-Level Inventory."**
(*Life*, Vol. 14, Issue 4, 2024 — doi: 10.3390/life14040432) — ~8 min

The most comprehensive catalogue to date of which marine animals can produce their own light. Combining an exhaustive literature review with unpublished observations, the authors built a species-level inventory covering luminescence status for over 9,000 species across 695+ confirmed genera, scoring each record for reliability. *Life* is a peer-reviewed, open-access journal published by MDPI; the senior author (Steven Haddock, Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute) is one of the field's leading bioluminescence researchers, and the paper is openly accessible at mdpi.com/2075-1729/14/4/432.

**2. Irigoien X, Klevjer TA, Røstad A, et al. "Large mesopelagic fishes biomass and trophic efficiency in the open ocean."**
(*Nature Communications*, Vol. 5, Article 3271, 2014 — doi: 10.1038/ncomms4271) — ~7 min

Using acoustic (echosounder) surveys collected during the Malaspina 2010 circumnavigation expedition, an international team led by AZTI (Spain) revised the global estimate of mesopelagic fish biomass roughly tenfold upward, and used trophic-efficiency modeling to show that this biomass is consistent with the amount of primary production sinking out of the surface ocean. *Nature Communications* is a peer-reviewed, open-access journal in the Nature Portfolio; this is a foundational, widely cited primary research article that reshaped estimates of total fish biomass on Earth.

**3. Xiao X, Xu H, Fang C, et al. "Microbial ecosystems and ecological driving forces in the deepest ocean sediments."**
(*Cell*, Vol. 188, Issue 5, 2025 — doi: 10.1016/j.cell.2024.12.036) — ~6 min

A cover-story paper from the Mariana Trench Environment and Ecology Research (MEER) project: 1,648 sediment samples collected across the Mariana Trench, Yap Trench, and Philippine Basin (6,000–10,900 m) during a 2021 expedition, yielding a 92-terabasepair metagenomic dataset and 7,564 species-level microbial genomes, 89% of which had never been documented before. *Cell* is one of the most selective peer-reviewed biology journals (Cell Press/Elsevier); the study is the largest systematic genomic survey of hadal sediment life to date.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

### How much do we actually know is down there?

Day 1's figures on the Clarion-Clipperton Zone already hinted at the pattern: wherever specialists sample abyssal or hadal habitat systematically, the overwhelming majority of what they find has never been described. Today's readings extend that pattern in two directions at once — upward in the water column, into the mesopelagic, and downward into hadal sediment. In the mesopelagic, the surprise was not novel species so much as sheer quantity: the Irigoien et al. (2014) acoustic survey found ten times more fish biomass than trawl-based estimates had suggested, pushing global mesopelagic fish biomass into the range of several billion tonnes — plausibly the largest reservoir of vertebrate biomass on the planet, dwarfing all other fish combined. In hadal sediment, by contrast, the surprise was taxonomic: the MEER project's 2025 *Cell* paper sequenced sediment from six major hadal features and recovered 7,564 microbial species-level genomes, of which about 89% had no match in any existing public database. Even at 10,000 m, under conditions of permanent dark, near-freezing cold, and pressure over 1,000 times that at sea level, microbial diversity is not merely present but largely unknown to science.

### The twilight zone's hidden mass, and the pump that feeds it

The mesopelagic zone's outsized biomass is directly tied to the phenomenon introduced on Day 1: the biological pump, the process by which photosynthesis-derived organic matter sinks from the sunlit surface toward the deep. Only a small fraction of what is produced at the surface ever reaches the seafloor — typically well under a few percent of net primary production survives the trip past 1,000 m, and less than 1% reaches the true abyssal floor, because bacteria and mid-water animals consume most of the sinking material long before it gets that deep. The mesopelagic zone is precisely where most of that consumption happens, which is one reason it hosts such enormous standing biomass: it is the first major layer of "catchers" intercepting the rain of organic material falling from above. Irigoien et al. showed that the biomass of mesopelagic fish is consistent with — and helps explain — the magnitude of that intercepted flux through trophic-efficiency modeling, tying a population-scale biological pattern to an ecosystem-scale energy budget. A 2014 modeling study in *Global Change Biology* used a related logic in reverse: projecting how climate-driven changes in surface carbon export will alter the food supply reaching the deep seafloor, the authors estimated a measurable further decline in global benthic biomass by the end of the century as warming reduces the flux of organic matter sinking from above. The same mechanism that explains today why the mesopelagic is so richly populated is the mechanism Day 4 will examine in far more quantitative detail, and Day 5 will revisit as a climate-driven threat.

Much of that mesopelagic biomass also participates in diel vertical migration: countless individual fish, squid, and zooplankton rise from the twilight zone toward the surface each night to feed under cover of darkness, then descend again before dawn — widely described as the largest synchronized animal migration on Earth by biomass. This daily commute is itself a biological pump mechanism, actively transporting carbon and nutrients downward through animal respiration and waste at depth, on top of the passive sinking of dead organic matter. It is a vivid illustration of how "biodiversity" and "biogeochemistry" are not separate stories in the deep ocean — the abundance of mesopelagic animals is both a consequence of surface productivity and an active agent that redistributes it.

### Light as the deep ocean's dominant visual language

If food is the deep ocean's primary constraint, light is its primary sensory currency — and animals overwhelmingly produce their own. The Claes et al. (2024) inventory in *Life* confirms what earlier quantitative work had already suggested: bioluminescence is not a curiosity restricted to a few famous taxa (anglerfish, certain jellyfish) but a widespread trait spanning at least 695 genera across many separate branches of the animal tree, having evolved independently dozens of times. An earlier, frequently cited quantitative study by Martini and Haddock (*Scientific Reports*, 2017), based on 17 years and more than 350,000 ROV-based observations off the California coast, found that roughly 76% of individual animals observed between the surface and 3,900 m were capable of producing light — a proportion that increased with depth. Bioluminescence mostly appears blue-green (480–510 nm), the wavelength range that travels farthest through seawater, though some deep-sea predators such as certain dragonfish have independently evolved red bioluminescence and matching red-shifted vision, effectively giving them a private "infrared" channel invisible to most prey and rivals.

The chemistry — an enzyme (luciferase) catalyzing the oxidation of a small substrate molecule (luciferin) — is broadly conserved in principle but has arisen through convergent evolution using different luciferin-luciferase pairs across unrelated lineages, a striking case of the same functional solution being reinvented repeatedly. Ecologically, bioluminescence in the deep ocean serves at least three distinct functions: counterillumination, in which animals such as certain squid and fish emit downward-facing light calibrated to match the faint residual glow from above, canceling out their silhouette to predators looking up from below; predation, exemplified by the anglerfish's luminous lure, or by species that emit sudden bright flashes to startle or disorient attackers; and communication or camouflage-by-distraction, including luminous "smoke screens" or decoy displays used to escape pursuit. That such a high proportion of deep-sea animals rely on self-generated light is itself indirect evidence of how little ambient light there is to work with — in most of the ocean's volume, if you want to see or be seen at all, you generally have to switch the light on yourself.

## Questions to think about

1. The mesopelagic zone hosts an estimated majority of the ocean's fish biomass, largely because it is the first layer to intercept sinking organic matter from the surface — yet it remains almost entirely outside current fisheries management and conservation frameworks. What ecological risks would large-scale commercial harvesting of mesopelagic fish (already being explored for fishmeal and omega-3 oil) pose, given how tightly this biomass is coupled to carbon export?

2. Bioluminescence has evolved independently dozens of times across unrelated marine lineages, using different biochemical machinery each time, yet converges on similar ecological functions (counterillumination, luring, startling). What does this level of convergent evolution suggest about how strong the selective pressure of "being in the dark" really is, compared to other environmental pressures life has faced on Earth?

3. The MEER project found that ~89% of the microbial genomes recovered from hadal sediment had never been documented — a proportion strikingly similar to the 88–92% of undescribed metazoan species found in the Clarion-Clipperton Zone (Day 1). If this pattern — the vast majority of what we sample in the deep ocean turning out to be new to science — holds generally, what does it imply about the reliability of any current global estimate of total ocean biodiversity?
