# The deep ocean — Day 4/7: The Deep Ocean and Climate — Carbon Pumps and the Overturning Circulation

## Context

Day 1 introduced, in outline, why the deep ocean matters for climate: it holds roughly 30% of humanity's excess CO₂ and about 90% of the excess heat trapped by the greenhouse effect, moved and stored there by the solubility pump, the biological pump, and the thermohaline circulation. Day 3 came at the same machinery from the biodiversity side, showing how the biological pump's sinking flux of organic matter feeds the mesopelagic zone's enormous fish biomass — and promised that today would return to the pump "in far more quantitative detail." Today is that day, with one addition: alongside the biological pump, we look closely at the physical circulation that carries carbon and heat into the abyss, and at growing evidence that this circulation is itself changing under warming.

The deep ocean's climate role is often described in static terms — a fixed reservoir, a passive sink. The three readings below make clear it is not static. The biological pump's efficiency depends on nutrient supply and circulation speed, both of which are shifting. Deep water formation around Antarctica, which ventilates and oxygenates the abyss, is measurably slowing. And the Atlantic's overturning circulation, the current that carries surface heat and carbon into the North Atlantic depths, is weakening in ways that project forward into how much CO₂ the ocean will absorb over the next century. None of this is peripheral to the climate story most people know from newspapers — it is a large, still-underappreciated part of it, unfolding below the depth that satellites or public attention normally reach.

Today's structure follows the three main deep-ocean climate mechanisms in turn: the biological pump (how sinking organic matter exports carbon, and how that export is projected to change as the ocean warms and slows), the physical overturning circulation that forms and moves deep water masses (with Antarctic Bottom Water as the clearest current example of measurable change), and the feedback loop connecting the two — a weakening circulation that reduces the ocean's capacity to keep absorbing the carbon humans emit.

## Today's readings (~24 min total)

**1. Pasquier B, Holzer M, Chamberlain MA. "The biological and preformed carbon pumps in perpetually slower and warmer oceans."**
(*Biogeosciences*, Vol. 21, 2024 — doi: 10.5194/bg-21-3373-2024) — ~9 min

Using steady-state ocean circulation transport-matrix models tuned to future warmer, slower-circulation scenarios (following RCP4.5/RCP8.5-style forcing out to roughly the 2090s), the authors — at UNSW Sydney — separate the total carbon exported to the deep ocean into a "biological" pump (carbon fixed by photosynthesis and then sinking as organic matter) and a "preformed" pump (dissolved inorganic carbon carried into the deep purely by physical circulation, without ever passing through an organism). They find that biological productivity actually declines under warming, because a slower, more stratified ocean supplies less nutrient from depth back to the surface — but the biological pump still sequesters more carbon overall, because the same slower circulation means that whatever carbon does sink stays isolated from the atmosphere for longer. *Biogeosciences* is a fully open-access, peer-reviewed journal of the European Geosciences Union (Copernicus Publications); this is a primary modelling study that directly quantifies the mechanism Day 1 flagged as this week's return topic.

**2. Rintoul SR, Stewart AL, Johnson GC, et al. "Antarctic Bottom Water in a changing climate."**
(*Nature Reviews Earth & Environment*, December 2025 — doi: 10.1038/s43017-025-00750-2) — ~9 min

A major synthesis review, led by CSIRO/University of Tasmania oceanographer Stephen Rintoul with a team spanning Australian, US, and Japanese institutions, of what is known about Antarctic Bottom Water (AABW) — the coldest, densest water mass on Earth, formed by sea-ice and shelf processes around Antarctica and then sinking to fill the deepest layers of the global ocean. The review reports that ocean heat content below 4,000 m has increased at a rate of roughly 12.9 (± 1.8) terawatts since the mid-1980s, that AABW volume has thinned by more than 50 decibars per decade near its source regions, and that the deep overturning circulation carrying AABW northward is slowing, driven by freshening of Antarctic shelf waters from glacial meltwater and reduced sea-ice formation. *Nature Reviews Earth & Environment* is a peer-reviewed, invited-review journal from the Nature portfolio (subscription-access; a free author copy of the accepted manuscript is available via the NERC Open Research Archive at nora.nerc.ac.uk/id/eprint/540751/). This is exactly the kind of expert synthesis the source-quality rules prioritize, and it is the deep ocean's clearest current example of a measurably changing overturning circulation.

**3. Schaumann F, Alastrué de Asenjo E. "Weakening AMOC reduces ocean carbon uptake and increases the social cost of carbon."**
(*Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, Vol. 122, Issue 9, February 2025 — doi: 10.1073/pnas.2419543122) — ~6 min

A modelling study from the Max Planck Institute for Meteorology and the University of Hamburg that closes the loop between circulation and climate feedback. The authors ran ensembles of an Earth system model in which the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) — the northward-flowing, sinking limb of the Atlantic's part of the thermohaline circulation — weakens by varying amounts, and found an approximately linear relationship between AMOC strength and the ocean's capacity to take up atmospheric carbon: a weaker AMOC transports less surface carbon into the deep Atlantic, leaving more of it in the atmosphere. Feeding this carbon-cycle feedback into an integrated climate-economy model, the authors estimate additional global economic damages of several trillion US dollars and an increase in the "social cost of carbon" of roughly 1%. *PNAS* is a peer-reviewed, multidisciplinary journal of the US National Academy of Sciences, published fully open access since 2021 (also archived at PMC11892582). This paper is the clearest available demonstration that ocean circulation change is not just an oceanographic curiosity but something that propagates all the way to economic climate-policy metrics.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

### The biological pump, quantified — and how it responds to warming

Day 1 cited the 2023 *Annual Review of Marine Science* estimate that the global biological pump exports roughly 5–12 petagrams of carbon per year across the 100 m depth horizon — comparable in magnitude to total human fossil-fuel emissions, though the great majority of that exported carbon is respired back into dissolved form within the mesopelagic zone rather than reaching the seafloor. Only the fraction that survives the trip past the permanent thermocline (roughly 500 m) is meaningfully removed from atmospheric exchange for decades to centuries; carbon reaching the true abyssal floor can be sequestered for millennia. Pasquier, Holzer and Chamberlain's 2024 modelling extends this picture into the future. Their central, somewhat counterintuitive finding is a decoupling between productivity and sequestration: warming reduces the nutrient supply that upwelling and mixing normally deliver from depth back to the surface, so photosynthetic productivity actually falls even as temperatures rise (warmer water would otherwise favor faster biology). Yet the carbon that is exported ends up more effectively sequestered, not less, because a slower and more stratified ocean keeps deep water isolated from the surface for longer. The authors also highlight a second, less intuitive channel: the "preformed" pump, dissolved inorganic carbon carried into the deep by physical circulation alone, becomes relatively more important as biological export weakens — carbon still reaches the deep ocean, but increasingly via water-mass transport rather than sinking particles. The overall message is not "the biological pump breaks down under warming" so much as "the balance of mechanisms shifts," with implications for how much confidence we should place in extrapolating today's carbon-uptake numbers into a warmer future.

### Deep water formation: how the abyss gets filled, and why it is slowing

The biological and solubility pumps move carbon into the water column, but something has to carry that carbon-laden water into the deep ocean and keep it there — that is the job of deep water formation and the thermohaline (density-driven) overturning circulation Day 1 introduced only briefly. Two engines dominate globally: North Atlantic Deep Water (NADW), formed as warm, salty surface water flowing north via the Gulf Stream cools, becomes dense, and sinks in the Norwegian and Labrador Seas; and Antarctic Bottom Water (AABW), formed around the Antarctic continental shelf when sea-ice formation expels salt into the underlying water (a process called brine rejection), making it dense enough to cascade down the continental slope to the seafloor and spread northward into every major ocean basin. AABW is colder and denser than NADW and occupies the very bottom of the water column; together the two systems set the density structure — and therefore much of the heat and carbon distribution — of the entire deep ocean.

Rintoul et al.'s review makes clear that this system, long treated as a slow, geologically stable backdrop, is now measurably changing on human timescales. Two of their headline numbers are worth sitting with. First, ocean heat content below 4,000 m — a layer essentially inaccessible to surface weather but reachable only via deep water formation and mixing — has risen at 12.9 ± 1.8 terawatts since the mid-1980s, direct evidence that the abyss is absorbing and retaining heat from a changing climate, not merely reflecting the surface passively. Second, AABW itself has thinned by more than 50 decibars per decade closest to its Antarctic source regions, and the overturning circulation that exports it northward is slowing. The mechanism is a freshwater story: as glacial ice melts and adds fresh water to the Antarctic shelf, and as regional sea-ice formation patterns shift, the shelf water that would otherwise sink becomes less dense and less able to do so — the same freshening-driven weakening mechanism, applied to the Southern Hemisphere's deep water engine, that climate scientists have long worried about for the Atlantic's AMOC.

### Closing the loop: a weaker circulation, a weaker carbon sink, a real economic cost

Schaumann and Alastrué de Asenjo's 2025 PNAS paper is the piece that turns "the circulation is slowing" into "and here is what that costs." Their Earth system model runs show an approximately linear relationship: as AMOC strength declines, the volume of surface carbon transported into the deep Atlantic declines with it, leaving more anthropogenic CO₂ in the atmosphere than would otherwise be the case. This is a genuine climate feedback loop — warming weakens the circulation, the weaker circulation reduces ocean carbon uptake, and the resulting extra atmospheric CO₂ adds further warming — and it had been largely absent from the integrated climate-economy models used to calculate policy-relevant figures like the social cost of carbon. When the authors added the AMOC-carbon feedback into such a model, it raised estimated global economic damages by several trillion US dollars and increased the social cost of carbon by roughly 1%. That percentage sounds modest, but it applies to a baseline number already in the tens to low hundreds of dollars per ton of CO₂ used to set carbon prices and evaluate climate policy worldwide — meaning a genuine, previously under-counted physical mechanism translates directly into underestimated economic stakes. The same logic likely applies, though it has been studied less, to the Antarctic side of the system Rintoul et al. describe: a slower AABW formation reduces oxygen supply and carbon storage in the deep ocean by a separate but parallel pathway. A very recent, independent data point reinforces the broader picture that the deep ocean's heat and carbon uptake is an active, still-accelerating process rather than a settled one: an international analysis led by the Institute of Atmospheric Physics (Chinese Academy of Sciences), published in *Advances in Atmospheric Sciences* in January 2026, found that global ocean heat content in the upper 2,000 m set a new record in 2025 — the ninth consecutive annual record — rising by roughly 23 ± 8 zettajoules relative to 2024 alone.

### Why "slower circulation still sequesters more carbon" is not good news

It would be easy to read the Pasquier et al. finding — that a slower ocean sequesters biologically exported carbon more effectively — as a silver lining. It is not. That finding describes what happens to carbon that is already exported; it says nothing about the total volume of carbon the ocean can absorb from the atmosphere in the first place, which is exactly the quantity the AMOC-carbon feedback (Schaumann & Alastrué de Asenjo) and the AABW slowdown (Rintoul et al.) show is falling as circulation weakens. The picture that emerges from combining all three readings is therefore not contradictory but layered: individual parcels of carbon that do make it into the deep ocean may be held there somewhat longer under a slower circulation, but the total rate at which the ocean pulls carbon out of the atmosphere in the first place is declining — precisely because the physical circulation that does the pulling is weakening. Less carbon in, held slightly better, is still a net loss of buffering capacity for the atmosphere and the surface climate that depends on it.

## Questions to think about

1. The Schaumann & Alastrué de Asenjo (2025) paper shows that a physical ocean-circulation feedback, previously missing from most integrated climate-economy models, changes the social cost of carbon by about 1% — a small percentage applied to a number already used to set real carbon prices and policy. What other "hidden" physical feedbacks (in the deep ocean or elsewhere) might be similarly under-counted in the economic models that inform climate policy, simply because they are hard to observe or only recently quantified?

2. Antarctic Bottom Water formation and the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation are driven by broadly similar physics (density-driven sinking, disrupted by freshwater input) but are studied, funded, and discussed very differently — the AMOC has entered mainstream climate reporting as a "tipping point," while AABW's measurable slowdown remains comparatively obscure outside oceanography. Why might that asymmetry in public and policy attention exist, and what would change if AABW received AMOC-level scrutiny?

3. Pasquier et al. find that biological productivity is projected to fall even as the fraction of exported carbon that gets sequestered rises, because slower circulation isolates the deep ocean more effectively. Given that this "more isolated deep ocean" story could sound, in a superficial reading, like good news for carbon storage, how should scientists communicate a mechanistically real but net-negative finding like this without it being misread as a reason for less concern?
