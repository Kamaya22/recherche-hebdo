# The deep ocean — Day 6/7: Exploration Technology — Floats, Robots, and the Race to Map the Abyss

## Context

Day 1 opened this week with a striking number: after nearly seventy years of deep-sea exploration, humans have visually observed less than 0.001% of the seafloor below 200 m — an area roughly the size of Rhode Island. Day 5 showed why that gap matters right now: the International Seabed Authority and NOAA are being asked to authorize industrial mining across areas of the Clarion-Clipperton Zone that have barely been surveyed, using the same underlying class of pressure-tolerant robotic vehicle that scientists use to study the ecosystems the mining would disturb. Today asks the question underneath both of those stories: how do we actually know anything about a place we cannot see, breathe, or touch directly, and where does that knowledge come from?

The answer is not one technology but three complementary — and mismatched — ways of sensing the deep. The first is a passive, drifting network of robotic floats (Argo) that has quietly generated more ocean data than the previous century of shipboard oceanography combined, but that, in its core form, does not even reach most of the depths this week has been discussing. The second is active robotics — remotely operated vehicles (ROVs), autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs), and stationary landers — that can see, sample, and manipulate, but only in one place at a time, for a limited time, at a real cost. The third is remote, ship- and satellite-based mapping of the seafloor's shape itself, which is how we know the ocean's terrain without ever going there — and which is the technology behind the "percent of the seafloor mapped" figures that get cited in seabed-mining and cable-routing debates alike. Understanding what each can and cannot do is a prerequisite for understanding the limits of everything claimed in this week's earlier readings.

Today's three readings each anchor one of these three technologies: a two-decade retrospective on the Argo float program, a 2025 engineering review of AUV technology, and an institutional progress report on the Nippon Foundation-GEBCO Seabed 2030 mapping project — which also opens onto the funding and geopolitical dimension of ocean exploration, since who pays for exploration technology shapes what gets explored.

## Today's readings (~22 min total)

**1. Johnson GC, Hosoda S, Jayne SR, Oke PR, Riser SC, Roemmich D, Suga T, Thierry V, Wijffels SE, Xu J. "Argo—Two Decades: Global Oceanography, Revolutionized."**
(*Annual Review of Marine Science*, Vol. 14, pp. 379–403, 2022 — doi: 10.1146/annurev-marine-022521-102008) — ~8 min

A retrospective by scientists central to building and running the Argo program (NOAA/PMEL, JAMSTEC, Woods Hole, CSIRO, University of Washington, Ifremer) covering two decades of the international profiling-float array: nearly 4,000 free-drifting robots that measure temperature and salinity through the water column on repeated cycles, transmitting via satellite whenever they surface. *Annual Review of Marine Science* is a peer-reviewed, invited-review journal (Annual Reviews' "Subscribe to Open" model makes current and near-current volumes, including this one, freely readable); this is the standard reference for what the Argo array has actually delivered — and, read carefully, for where its core design still falls short of "deep."

**2. Ma D, Li Y, Ma T, Pascoal AM. "The state of the art in key technologies for autonomous underwater vehicles: a review."**
(*Engineering*, 2025 — doi: 10.1016/j.eng.2025.08.002) — ~8 min

A 2025 review by researchers spanning Chinese and Portuguese robotics labs, surveying the engineering building blocks of modern AUVs: vehicle design, power systems, planning and control, perception, navigation, underwater communications, and the growing role of onboard artificial intelligence in autonomous decision-making. *Engineering* is a peer-reviewed, fully open-access journal launched by the Chinese Academy of Engineering and published internationally by Elsevier; it gives a systems-level view of why autonomous robots — as opposed to tethered, human-piloted ones — are the technology increasingly expected to close the observation gap Day 1 described.

**3. "Seabed 2030 announces latest progress on World Hydrography Day."**
(Intergovernmental Oceanographic Commission of UNESCO / Nippon Foundation-GEBCO Seabed 2030 Project, June 2026 — ioc.unesco.org) — ~6 min

An institutional progress announcement from the IOC-UNESCO, co-steward (with the International Hydrographic Organization) of GEBCO, the body compiling the definitive bathymetric (depth) map of the global ocean floor through the Nippon Foundation-funded Seabed 2030 project. The IOC is a UN intergovernmental body and an acceptable primary institutional source under this project's sourcing rules; its releases are the standard, citable record of the project's year-over-year mapping coverage and funding structure, cross-confirmed by parallel releases from the IHO and from Seabed 2030 itself.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

### Argo: extraordinary global coverage — but not of the "deep" this week has been describing

The Argo array is one of the great success stories of ocean science: since the early 2000s, roughly 4,000 floats have executed a repeating 10-day cycle — drift at a "parking depth," dive deeper to profile, rise to the surface measuring temperature and salinity as they go, transmit the profile by satellite, sink again — generating over two million profiles that have underpinned more than 4,000 scientific publications and are now embedded in every operational ocean forecast and climate model, including the circulation science described on Day 4. But there is an important catch for a week about the deep ocean specifically: the core Argo array profiles only the upper 2,000 m. That is deep by everyday standards, but it stops well short of the abyssal zone (roughly 3,000–6,000 m, where most of the seafloor this week has discussed actually lies) and nowhere near the hadal trenches from Day 1. Extending Argo into true deep water — "Deep Argo," rated to full ocean depth — and into biogeochemistry — "BGC-Argo," adding oxygen, pH, and nutrient sensors — are newer, still-building sub-programs, not yet global in coverage. Argo is therefore the best illustration this week of a general rule: the *most* systematic, highest-volume ocean observing network humanity has ever built still does not, by itself, reach most of "the deep ocean" as this week has defined it.

### AUVs, ROVs and landers: the tools that actually reach the bottom, and their tradeoffs

Where Argo drifts, three families of robots go looking. Tethered ROVs — like the vehicle in Day 5's Stewart et al. mining-trial study — stay connected to a ship by cable, which supplies power and real-time control but limits range and requires a ship on station the whole time, at high cost. Untethered AUVs, the subject of today's second reading, trade that live control for independence: they can survey much larger areas on preprogrammed missions, but must rely on onboard batteries (limiting mission duration) and cannot transmit data live from depth, since radio does not propagate through seawater — acoustic modems and satellite uplink after surfacing are the workarounds, both far lower-bandwidth than a cable. Ma et al.'s review frames the current engineering frontier as putting more autonomy on board: better navigation without GPS (which does not penetrate water), smarter perception and path-planning, and increasingly, onboard AI that lets a vehicle decide in real time what is worth investigating rather than transmitting everything back for a human to review later — directly relevant to closing the Day-1 observation gap, since the bottleneck has historically been as much about *reviewing* data as *collecting* it. A third, simpler tool — the stationary lander, dropped to the seafloor, left to record or sample, and recovered later (or not) — trades mobility entirely for low cost and endurance, and is often the only economical way to get a long time series at a single hadal-trench site.

### Mapping the shape of the ocean is a different technology from seeing what's on it

Day 1's "0.001% visually observed" and today's "28.7% of the seafloor mapped" (IOC-UNESCO/Seabed 2030, June 2026, up from 27.3% in 2025 and 26.1% in 2024) sound contradictory but describe two different things. Visual observation — cameras on ROVs, AUVs, or landers — tells you what lives somewhere and what it looks like, but only where a vehicle has actually gone, which is why the covered fraction is so minuscule. Bathymetric mapping — mainly ship-mounted multibeam sonar, supplemented by satellite-altimetry-derived estimates for areas ships haven't surveyed — tells you only the *depth and shape* of the seafloor, at resolutions that (for ship-surveyed areas) can reach roughly 100 m grid cells, without seeing a single organism. It is a coarser, cheaper, more scalable signal, which is why coverage is orders of magnitude higher — and why "the ocean floor is now nearly 30% mapped" is compatible with "we have seen almost none of it." Seabed 2030 compiles both ship-collected and crowdsourced depth data from more than 185 organizations across 40-plus countries into the public GEBCO grid; even so, at the current pace of roughly five million square kilometers added per year, the project's original 2030 completion target is very unlikely to be met.

### Funding shapes what gets explored — and why that is a geopolitical question, not just a budgetary one

A recurring, easy-to-miss fact ties this reading together: a very large share of the technology underpinning both global bathymetry and expedition-scale exploration depends on a small number of funders. Seabed 2030's primary financial backer is a single private Japanese foundation, the Nippon Foundation, working through the IHO/IOC's GEBCO structure; national agencies (NOAA in the US, for its AUV and ROV expeditions) fund most of the direct exploration robotics. This matters beyond bookkeeping: whoever funds and controls mapping and exploration data has practical influence over which regions get surveyed first, which shapes everything from the extended-continental-shelf claims nations file under the Law of the Sea, to where undersea cables get routed, to — circling back to Day 5 — which parts of the seabed are well-characterized enough that a mining regulator like the ISA or NOAA can plausibly claim to know what is there before authorizing extraction. A scientific network built primarily to answer academic questions (Argo) and a mapping project built to serve navigation, industry, and national claims (Seabed 2030) are not neutral with respect to what data becomes available, or to whom, or first.

## Questions to think about

1. Argo's ~4,000-float array delivers systematic, repeated, global coverage of the upper 2,000 m, while AUV/ROV expeditions deliver rich but one-off, spatially tiny snapshots of the true deep. If you were allocating a fixed global exploration budget, how would you weigh expanding Deep Argo's full-ocean-depth coverage against funding more AUV/ROV expeditions — and does the answer depend on whether the goal is climate monitoring versus biodiversity discovery?

2. Bathymetric "percent mapped" (28.7%) and visual "percent observed" (0.001%) are both true, both meaningful, and easy to conflate in public and policy discussion. Where else in this week's readings — mining impact assessments, biodiversity estimates, carbon-flux models — might a similar mismatch between two legitimately different kinds of "coverage" be hiding in a headline statistic?

3. A single private foundation is the primary funder of the project mapping the entire ocean floor, and a handful of national agencies fund most direct deep-sea robotics. What obligations, if any, should follow from that concentration — around data openness, priority-setting, or governance — and who should be setting them?
