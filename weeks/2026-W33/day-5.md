# The deep ocean — Day 5/7: Threats — Mining, Plastic, and a Changing Chemistry

## Context

Days 1 through 4 built up a picture of the deep ocean as a vast, barely-observed habitat (Day 1), engineered by evolution to withstand crushing pressure and total darkness (Day 2), unexpectedly rich in biomass and biodiversity (Day 3), and functioning as one of the planet's most important climate regulators — storing roughly 30% of humanity's excess CO₂ and about 90% of the excess heat trapped by the greenhouse effect, moved there by the biological pump and the thermohaline circulation (Day 4). Today flips the lens: what is actually happening to this system, right now, as a direct result of human activity?

Three pressures dominate the current scientific and policy conversation, and they map closely onto the week's outline: industrial deep-sea mining for polymetallic nodules and, at hydrothermal vent fields, metal sulfides; plastic and chemical pollution that has been shown to reach even the deepest ocean trenches; and the direct biological consequences of the same climate-driven changes described on Day 4 — not just a slower carbon pump, but measurably less oxygen and more acidic water bathing long-lived deep-sea animals. These are not independent stories. A weakening deep circulation (Day 4) is also the mechanism that reduces how much oxygen reaches the deep ocean in the first place, so the "climate" story and the "threats" story are, mechanistically, the same story viewed from two angles.

The governance backdrop makes this more than an academic question. As of mid-2026, the International Seabed Authority (ISA) — the UN body charged with regulating mining in international waters — has spent over twelve years negotiating a "Mining Code" without finalizing it; more than thirty countries are now calling for a moratorium. Meanwhile the Metals Company (TMC), through its subsidiary Nauru Ocean Resources Inc. (NORI), has pursued exploitation permits through the US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) in parallel with the ISA process — a move that led TMC subsidiaries to sue the ISA at the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea in June 2026. Today's first reading examines the direct ecological aftermath of exactly this company's 2022 pilot mining trial.

## Today's readings (~22 min total)

**1. Stewart E, et al. "Impacts of an industrial deep-sea mining trial on macrofaunal biodiversity."**
(*Nature Ecology & Evolution*, Vol. 10, pp. 318–329, 2025/2026 — doi: 10.1038/s41559-025-02911-4) — ~8 min

In October 2022, NORI (a subsidiary of The Metals Company) and the contractor Allseas ran a large-scale trial of a nodule-collector vehicle across more than 80 km of abyssal seafloor in the NORI-D contract area of the Clarion-Clipperton Zone (eastern Pacific, ~4,280 m depth), recovering over 3,000 tonnes of polymetallic nodules. A team led by the Natural History Museum's Deep-Sea Research Lab, with the University of Southampton, University of Gothenburg, and the National Oceanography Centre, sampled sediment-dwelling macrofauna for two years before and two months after the trial — a rare before/after experimental design that separates the mining's direct effect from natural background variability. *Nature Ecology & Evolution* is a peer-reviewed Nature Portfolio journal; the paper is freely available (also mirrored via the NERC Open Research Archive and PMC), and it is the first quantitative, ground-truthed measurement of what an actual industrial-scale mining vehicle does to seafloor life, rather than a projection or small-scale simulation.

**2. Zhao S, Kvale KF, Zhu L, et al. "The distribution of subsurface microplastics in the ocean."**
(*Nature*, Vol. 641, pp. 51–61, 30 April 2025 — doi: 10.1038/s41586-025-08818-1) — ~8 min

An international team (including Northeastern University, NIOZ, and collaborators across the US, Germany, Chile, and Japan) compiled and standardized depth-profile microplastic measurements from 1,885 sampling stations collected between 2014 and 2024, spanning the surface to several thousand metres depth. They report particle concentrations ranging from roughly 10⁻⁴ to 10⁴ particles per cubic metre and document a "subsurface maximum" — layers well below the surface, in places exceeding 10,000 particles/m³ — including samples from 6,800 m in the Mariana Trench. *Nature* is a peer-reviewed flagship journal; this article is fully open access (CC BY) and is the largest synthesis to date of how far microplastic pollution actually extends into the water column, beyond the surface "garbage patches" that dominate public perception.

**3. Ross T, Du Preez C, Ianson D. "Rapid deep ocean deoxygenation and acidification threaten life on Northeast Pacific seamounts."**
(*Global Change Biology*, Vol. 26, pp. 6424–6444, 2020 — doi: 10.1111/gcb.15307) — ~6 min

Fisheries and Oceans Canada researchers combined multi-decade oceanographic time series with visual seafloor surveys of four Northeast Pacific seamounts hosting cold-water corals, sponges, echinoderms and fish. They find dissolved-oxygen and pH trends changing fast enough, relative to the multi-decade to centuries-long lifespans of these habitat-forming species, to threaten local extinctions if trends continue. *Global Change Biology* is a peer-reviewed Wiley journal (open access on PMC); this paper is the clearest direct evidence linking the ocean-circulation and carbon-cycle changes described on Day 4 to an observable biological threat, at a specific, monitored set of deep-sea sites.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

### Mining: from projection to measurement

Until 2022, almost everything written about deep-sea mining's ecological impact was either a small-scale plough test, a laboratory sediment-plume simulation, or a projection drawn from analogy with other seafloor disturbances. Stewart et al.'s study changes that: it measures what happened after an actual, unmodified commercial-prototype nodule collector drove across a real abyssal plain. The headline numbers are stark for a habitat this poorly studied elsewhere — macrofaunal density fell by 37% and species richness by 32% directly within the vehicle's tracks, with community composition also shifting significantly in areas reached only by the sediment plume the vehicle kicked up, beyond the tracks themselves. Two nuances matter for how to read this. First, community-level effects (dispersion, composition) were detected further from the tracks than raw density loss, meaning the plume's footprint is broader than the visible mechanical disturbance. Second, when diversity was measured with sample-size-independent methods, it was not significantly reduced — a reminder that "fewer individuals" and "less diversity" are not automatically the same signal, and that different metrics can tell different parts of the story. Because abyssal-plain fauna are slow-growing and adapted to an extremely stable environment, the two-month post-trial window this study captures says nothing yet about recovery time; that is precisely the open question the field now needs to answer, especially with commercial-scale extraction — orders of magnitude larger than an 80 km test track — now being actively pursued through NOAA outside the ISA framework.

### Plastic: pollution as a whole water-column phenomenon, not a surface problem

Public understanding of ocean plastic pollution is dominated by surface imagery — floating bottles, the "Great Pacific Garbage Patch." Zhao et al.'s synthesis of nearly 1,900 stations reframes the problem vertically: microplastic concentrations do not simply decline with depth. Instead the data show a "subsurface maximum," with some of the highest particle counts on record — over 16,000 particles per cubic metre at 2,000 m in the North Pacific, over 25,000 at 2,500 m in the Arctic — occurring well below the sunlit surface layer, and measurable microplastic loads reaching 6,800 m in the Mariana Trench. The likely mechanism is that microplastics behave like other suspended particles: they aggregate with marine snow, biofouling organisms, and organic matter, and sink or get transported along density gradients rather than staying at the surface where they entered the ocean. This matters directly for deep-sea food webs — Day 3's mesopelagic fish and Day 2's pressure-adapted invertebrates are, by this evidence, routinely exposed to and likely ingesting synthetic particles as a normal part of their environment, not as an occasional contamination event. It also means that estimates of "how much plastic is in the ocean" based on surface trawls likely undercount the true reservoir substantially, since most of the study's highest concentrations were found well below where surface-net sampling looks.

### A changing chemistry: oxygen and pH as slow, cumulative killers

Where mining and visible plastic are discrete, localized disturbances, Ross, Du Preez and Ianson document something more insidious: a basin-scale chemical shift that reaches organisms without any localized human activity at all. Their Northeast Pacific seamounts are hundreds of kilometres from any mining site or major pollution source, yet multi-decade time series show dissolved oxygen dropping and acidity rising fast enough, relative to the centuries-long lifespans of cold-water corals and sponges, that local extinction is a realistic outcome if trends continue. This is the seamount-scale, directly observed counterpart to the basin-scale, modelled AABW slowdown and biological-pump changes described on Day 4: the same weakening of deep-water ventilation and carbon-cycle dynamics that Rintoul et al. and Pasquier et al. quantified as physical and biogeochemical trends is, on these seamounts, already measurably squeezing real animal populations. Unlike mining or a plastic spill, there is no local remediation available for a seamount losing oxygen because global circulation has slowed — the fix, if there is one, is upstream, at the scale of global emissions.

### Why these three threats compound rather than simply add up

Read together, the three readings describe a deep ocean under pressure from mechanically distinct but spatially and biologically overlapping stressors. A seamount or abyssal plain already experiencing oxygen decline and acidification (Ross et al.) has less physiological headroom to withstand an additional disturbance like a mining plume (Stewart et al.) or a chronic load of ingested microplastic (Zhao et al.) — deep-sea organisms are, almost by definition, adapted to extreme stability, which makes them poorly equipped to buffer multiple simultaneous changes even when each one, alone, might be survivable. None of today's three studies modeled these interactions directly — that is itself a gap worth naming — but the geography does not require modeling to notice: the Clarion-Clipperton Zone targeted for nodule mining, the water column through which sinking microplastic passes, and the seamounts undergoing deoxygenation are not separate oceans. They are the same ocean, described from three different angles of attack.

## Questions to think about

1. Stewart et al.'s trial covered roughly 80 km of seafloor and 3,000 tonnes of nodules — a pilot scale. Commercial operations under discussion at the ISA and NOAA would operate continuously across areas thousands of times larger. What kind of evidence (beyond a two-month post-trial snapshot) would you consider sufficient before regulators authorize mining at that scale?

2. Zhao et al. show that most microplastic in the ocean may sit below the surface rather than on it — the layer public attention and most cleanup technology target. If the "garbage patch" narrative has shaped both public concern and policy investment, what would a more accurate, subsurface-aware narrative change about where resources and regulation get directed?

3. Ross, Du Preez and Ianson's seamounts are threatened by a global, diffuse driver (ocean chemistry change) with no local fix, unlike a mining site or a plastic source that can, in principle, be regulated or cleaned up locally. Does this difference mean conservation policy should treat "climate-driven" deep-sea threats fundamentally differently from "activity-driven" ones — and if so, how?
