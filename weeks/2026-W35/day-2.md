# Rewilding — Day 2/7: Trophic cascades and ecosystem engineering — how large animals reshape landscapes

## Context

Yesterday's reading laid out rewilding's conceptual scaffolding: trophic complexity, natural disturbance, and dispersal, restored together so that an ecosystem can regulate itself with less permanent human management. Today zooms into the first and most-studied of those three pillars — trophic complexity — and asks a very concrete question: mechanistically, *how* does putting a large herbivore or a large predator back into a landscape actually change that landscape?

Two related mechanisms do most of the work. The first is the **trophic cascade**: a change at one level of a food web (say, a predator's abundance or behavior) propagating down through the levels below it — predator affects herbivore, herbivore affects plants, plants affect soil, fire regimes, or even other animal communities that depend on that vegetation. The second is **ecosystem engineering**: species that physically reshape their environment through their own activity — a beaver damming a stream, an elephant knocking down trees, a herd of grazers compacting soil and cropping grass short — creating habitat structure that other species then depend on. Rewilding projects generally aim to restore both at once, because in practice they are entangled: a predator's cascading effect on a landscape is very often mediated by its effect on an ecosystem engineer's behavior, not just its numbers.

This is also, usefully, where the science is most mature. Trophic cascades and ecosystem engineering have been studied experimentally and observationally for decades, well before "rewilding" existed as a term, so today's readings sit on a much larger and more quantitative evidence base than the framework paper from Day 1. That maturity comes with a complication worth flagging now and returning to properly on Day 4: precisely *how strong* a given cascade is, and how much of it is attributable to the reintroduced species versus other changing conditions, is an active and sometimes sharp scientific argument — even for the field's most famous case study, wolves in Yellowstone.

## Today's readings

**"Impacts of large herbivores on terrestrial ecosystems"** (Pringle et al., *Current Biology*, 2023) — https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(23)00472-4 — ~12 min (read the introduction and the sections on top-down effects on vegetation and fire; the taxon-specific boxes can be skimmed). This is a peer-reviewed review co-authored by fourteen herbivore ecologists led by Robert Pringle (Princeton), synthesizing the global evidence for how large herbivores — not just predators — function as ecosystem engineers by restructuring vegetation, nutrient cycling, and fire regimes, and why their rapid worldwide decline is an underappreciated conservation problem.

**"Outsized effect of predation: Wolves alter wetland creation and recolonization by killing ecosystem engineers"** (Gable, Johnson-Bice, Homkes, Windels & Bump, *Science Advances*, 2020) — https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.abc5439 — ~8 min (focus on the results and discussion; the methods section on GPS-collar tracking can be skimmed). A peer-reviewed, fully open-access field study from the long-running Voyageurs Wolf Project in Minnesota, showing through years of GPS-tracking data that wolves reshape wetland creation not mainly by reducing beaver numbers, but by killing dispersing beavers before they can found new ponds — a precise, mechanistic example of how a predator's effect on an ecosystem engineer, not on vegetation directly, can cascade through a landscape.

## Guided summary

Start with the classic version of a trophic cascade, because it is the intuitive core that the rest of today's complexity builds on: a predator eats (or simply scares) an herbivore, so the herbivore eats less vegetation, so vegetation recovers, and that recovery can ripple further outward — more streamside shrubs stabilize riverbanks, more forest cover changes fire behavior, more seed and berry production benefits birds and insects. This is sometimes called "top-down control," in contrast to "bottom-up" effects where resource availability (soil nutrients, rainfall) constrains what higher trophic levels can do. Both operate simultaneously in real ecosystems, and one of the harder problems in rewilding science is figuring out how much of an observed change to credit to each.

Pringle et al.'s 2023 *Current Biology* review makes a point that is easy to miss if you think of trophic cascades purely as a predator story: large herbivores themselves are enormously important ecosystem engineers, independent of any predator's influence on their numbers. An elephant knocking down trees creates open savanna structure that entirely different species depend on; a herd of wild grazers compacting and cropping grassland suppresses the woody encroachment that would otherwise let wildfires burn hotter and more destructively; migratory herbivores redistribute nutrients across a landscape simply by eating in one place and defecating in another. Critically, the review stresses that these are not small, local effects — they operate at the scale of entire landscapes, and because large herbivores are shrinking in range and numbers worldwide faster than almost any other functional group of animals, their disappearance is quietly removing a landscape-shaping force many ecosystems have relied on for millions of years, well before humans hunted many of them to the brink.

The Gable et al. study on wolves and beavers is a good complement because it shows that predator-driven cascades and ecosystem-engineering effects are frequently the *same* mechanism, not two separate ones. Beavers are themselves a textbook ecosystem engineer — a single dam can convert a stream into a wetland that stores water, cycles nutrients, and creates habitat for dozens of other species. The Voyageurs Wolf Project's years of GPS-tracking data show that wolves affect this process not primarily by killing enough adult beavers to shrink the population, but by disproportionately killing young beavers as they disperse to find new territory and attempt to build their own dams — the single riskiest phase of a beaver's life, when it is moving overland, away from water, and briefly defenseless. A pond that a wolf-killed disperser had just started building had close to zero chance of being completed and occupied, versus a very high chance if the disperser survived. In other words: the wolf's cascading effect on wetland creation runs specifically through its effect on the *ecosystem engineer's* behavior and survival, not through some indirect chain via vegetation. This is part of why Yellowstone's beaver population grew from one colony to nine after wolves were reintroduced in 1995 — a data point often cited in popular accounts of the "wolves fixed Yellowstone" story.

That popular story is worth pausing on, because it illustrates a mechanism this week keeps needing: distinguishing a real, well-evidenced effect (wolves changing beaver dispersal and survival, as directly measured by the Voyageurs project) from a much more contested, higher-profile claim about the *same ecosystem* — namely, whether wolves reintroduced to Yellowstone triggered a dramatic recovery of streamside willow and aspen by suppressing elk browsing. A widely publicized 2025 analysis in *Global Ecology and Conservation* reported a roughly 1,500% increase in willow crown volume attributable to the restored carnivore guild; a comment published in the same journal later in 2025 argued the analysis relied on circular statistical reasoning (using willow height to both compute and predict willow volume) and that, once corrected, the willow recovery looks much smaller and far more variable from site to site. The original authors have defended their methods, and the dispute is unresolved as of this writing. The point for today is not who is right — that belongs to Day 4 — but that the *mechanisms* covered above (top-down control, ecosystem engineering) are well-established in general, even while their *magnitude* in any one specific, high-profile case can be genuinely uncertain and worth scrutinizing rather than taking as settled.

Two things unite everything above and connect back to Day 1's three pillars. First, both mechanisms are about restoring a *process*, not a species count: what matters is not simply "wolves are present" or "elephants are present" but what those animals are doing — how they move, disperse, forage, and die — and how that behavior propagates through the rest of the system. Second, both mechanisms interact with the "dispersal" pillar from Day 1 in a very literal way: the beaver study only works because young beavers disperse across the landscape (giving wolves the chance to intercept them), and large-herbivore ecosystem engineering only operates at meaningful scale if animals can move freely enough to graze, browse, and redistribute nutrients across large areas rather than being fenced into small reserves.

## Questions to think about

- Gable et al.'s beaver study found wolves affect wetland creation mainly through predation on *dispersing* young beavers, not through reducing the adult population. What would this imply for a rewilding project that reintroduces a predator into a landscape where the engineer species (beavers, in this case) cannot disperse freely — say, because of roads or fragmented habitat?
- Pringle et al. argue large herbivores are important ecosystem engineers even without a predator's influence on their numbers. Does that change how you'd prioritize a rewilding project with limited resources — is reintroducing a herbivore species ever a more urgent first step than reintroducing a predator?
- The Yellowstone willow debate shows two peer-reviewed papers reaching very different conclusions from overlapping data. What would you want to see — as a non-specialist reader — before deciding which claim to trust, short of re-running the statistics yourself?
