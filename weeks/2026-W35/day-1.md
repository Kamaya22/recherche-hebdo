# Rewilding — Day 1/7: What rewilding is, and why conservation science redefined "success"

## Context

For most of the twentieth century, conservation had a fairly simple mental model: protect a landscape, keep people and their disturbances out, and let it stay (or return to) whatever a "natural," historical baseline was assumed to look like. Rewilding, a term that entered scientific use in the late 1990s and has since produced hundreds of peer-reviewed papers and national-level policy programs, challenges that model directly. It argues that many landscapes are not simply under-protected but functionally broken — missing the large animals and natural disturbances that once regulated them — and that restoring those processes, rather than restoring a fixed species list or a fixed-looking habitat, is what actually rebuilds a self-sustaining ecosystem.

That reframing matters for three reasons this week will keep returning to. First, it is scientifically ambitious: it asks ecologists to identify which ecological *processes* (not just which species) are missing from a given landscape, and to predict how reintroducing them will ripple through a food web — a much harder question than "how many hectares are protected." Second, it is increasingly consequential for policy: rewilding principles now sit inside the EU's Nature Restoration Law, national strategies, and large NGO-led programs like Rewilding Europe. Third, it is genuinely contested — not just by people worried about wolves near livestock, but inside ecology itself, over how much we can know about historical baselines, how "natural" a rewilded landscape really is when it still depends on human-drawn boundaries, and whether the concept is coherent enough to guide policy at all.

Today is the panorama day: what rewilding actually means as a scientific concept, the framework researchers use to organize it, and why the field has grown so quickly. The rest of the week builds on this — Day 2 goes into the mechanics of how large animals reshape ecosystems (trophic cascades), Day 3 into landscape-scale case studies and what monitoring shows, Day 4 into the field's open controversies, Day 5 into how "success" is actually measured, and Day 6 into policy and societal implications.

## Guided summary

Rewilding, as a scientific concept, is younger than it sounds. The word first appeared in print around 1990 among conservation activists, but it was given a rigorous ecological grounding in 1998 by conservation biologists Michael Soulé and Reed Noss, who proposed a "cores, corridors, carnivores" model: large protected core areas, connected by corridors that let animals disperse, anchored by the return of large carnivores whose regulatory effect on prey populations ripples through the rest of the food web. That founding idea — that a few large, wide-ranging species can have an outsized structuring effect on an entire ecosystem — is what ecologists call a *keystone species* effect, and it remains the intuitive core of rewilding even as the science around it has grown far more precise.

The paper that did the most to formalize that science, Perino et al.'s 2019 *Science* review, distilled decades of case studies into three interacting pillars. **Trophic complexity** means restoring a full food web, especially large herbivores that physically reshape vegetation through grazing or dam-building, and predators that regulate herbivore numbers and behavior. **Stochastic disturbance** means allowing natural, somewhat unpredictable events — wildfire, flooding, large-herbivore trampling — to keep creating the shifting mosaic of habitats many species need, rather than managing a landscape toward a single stable-looking state. **Dispersal** means removing barriers (fences, roads, fragmented habitat) so that animals and, eventually, genes can move freely across a landscape, which is what allows the other two pillars to reinforce each other over time — a wildfire only regenerates a diverse mosaic if seeds and animals can actually recolonize the burned patch afterward. The framework's central claim is that these three processes interact: restoring only one (say, reintroducing a predator into a landscape still crisscrossed by fences) tends to under-deliver compared with restoring all three together.

This is also where rewilding explicitly departs from older conservation thinking. Classical restoration ecology often asked "what species assemblage existed here at some reference point in the past, and how do we recreate it?" — a compositional goal. Rewilding instead asks a functional question: "which ecological processes are missing, and what would it take to restore self-regulation?" That distinction matters practically, because it changes what "finished" looks like. A compositional approach is judged against a historical species list; a functional approach is judged against whether an ecosystem can sustain itself with less ongoing human management — which is also why rewilding sits more comfortably with the fact that some historical baselines (pre-agricultural Europe, Pleistocene megafauna assemblages) are impossible to fully recreate and, in a changing climate, may not even be the right target to aim for.

The 2025 *Annual Review* synthesis by Carver and colleagues gives a sense of how far the field has traveled since Soulé and Noss's founding paper: more than 450 academic papers on rewilding have now been published, and the concept has moved well beyond isolated reintroduction projects into national and supranational policy — most visibly the EU's Nature Restoration Law and coordinated multi-country programs like Rewilding Europe. The review also flags where debate is most active today: rewilding's exact relationship to mainstream ecological restoration, questions about "nonhuman autonomy" (how much a landscape should be actively managed versus left alone once a rewilding project starts), and rewilding's emerging role as a climate strategy, since restoring large herbivores and predators can measurably affect carbon cycling and ecosystem resilience to climate stress. These are threads this week will pick up directly: Day 4 goes into the sharpest disagreements, and Day 6 into the policy and societal stakes.

## Questions to think about

- If "success" in rewilding is defined by restored ecological *processes* rather than a specific list of species, how would you know a rewilding project had succeeded — and could two very different-looking landscapes both count as successes under that definition?
- The founding "cores, corridors, carnivores" model puts large carnivores at the center. What does it mean for rewilding science that the animals most central to the theory are also the ones most likely to bring humans and wildlife into direct conflict?
- Perino et al.'s three pillars (trophic complexity, disturbance, dispersal) are framed as interacting — restoring one poorly if the others are missing. Can you think of a real-world constraint (land use, borders, safety) that would make it hard to restore all three at once in a densely populated region?

## This week's theme

**Rewilding — the science and controversies behind returning large predators and keystone species to degraded ecosystems.** Rewilding has moved from a fringe idea championed by a handful of conservation biologists in the 1990s to a mainstream approach now written into national and EU policy, with hundreds of active projects across Europe and North America. It matters because it represents a genuine shift in how conservation defines success: instead of preserving or restoring a fixed historical snapshot of a landscape, rewilding tries to restore the ecological processes — predation, large-herbivore disturbance, free movement of species — that let ecosystems regulate themselves with less permanent human management. That shift is scientifically interesting (it forces ecologists to grapple with what a "natural" baseline even means on a planet reshaped by 10,000+ years of human activity) and practically consequential, since rewilding is increasingly proposed as a tool for both biodiversity recovery and climate mitigation. It is also genuinely contested: reintroducing large carnivores and herbivores raises hard questions about historical baselines, human safety and livelihoods, and how much "wildness" a crowded, human-dominated landscape can actually accommodate.
