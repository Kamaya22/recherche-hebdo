# Rewilding — Day 7/7: Synthesis — what the science actually supports, what remains open, and where to go next

## Context

This week moved from theory to mechanism to evidence to controversy to method to policy, and each day complicated the one before it in a specific, traceable way. Day 1 gave rewilding's founding claim: ecosystems can be pushed back toward self-regulation by restoring trophic complexity, natural disturbance, and dispersal, rather than by recreating a fixed historical species list. Day 2 showed that the mechanisms behind that claim — trophic cascades and ecosystem engineering — are genuinely well-evidenced at the process level, down to GPS-tracked wolves intercepting dispersing beavers. Day 3 then asked how often those mechanisms add up to measurable, landscape-scale success across real projects, and found a field with far more conceptual writing than outcome data: 22 studies against 450-plus papers. Day 4 showed what happens when that gap opens up into genuine dispute, from the decades-old Pleistocene-rewilding argument to a live, unresolved 2025 statistical fight over the size of Yellowstone's own flagship effect. Day 5 explained the methodological chain underneath that fight — contested baselines, hard-to-scale indicators, and the counterfactual designs needed to attribute change to rewilding rather than to something else. Day 6 showed the same unevenness one level up, in policy and governance: a binding EU legal mandate and new IUCN technical standards moving faster than the economic evidence for who should pay and benefit, and a live disagreement over whether "rewilding" is even the right frame for Indigenous-led land care.

Today does not add a new mechanism or a new dispute; it asks what all of that adds up to, and where a reader who has followed the whole week should look next. The two readings below were chosen because each looks forward rather than back: one proposes a concrete fix for the measurement problem Day 3 and Day 5 diagnosed, and the other asks a set of pointed forward-looking questions about rewilding's social dimension, using named case studies — Yellowstone, Oostvaardersplassen, Knepp, Alladale, Carpathia — that overlap directly with sites discussed earlier this week (Knepp on Day 3, Yellowstone on Days 2 and 4).

The guided summary below is organized around three questions: what is genuinely settled after this week's evidence, what remains open, and what a reader who wants to go deeper should read next.

## Today's readings

**"Five critical questions we should ask of rewilding projects—And that social science can help us answer"** (Cary, Jones, Thomas, Brieghel, Payo-Payo & Wartmann, *People and Nature*, 2025) — https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/pan3.70100 — ~10 min (read the introduction and the discussion of what the five case studies reveal; the discipline-by-discipline methodology sections can be skimmed). A peer-reviewed, fully open-access paper in the British Ecological Society's *People and Nature* — the same journal behind Day 4's stakeholder-conflict study — by researchers including historians, anthropologists and human geographers. It applies five distinct social-science disciplines to five well-known rewilding sites (Yellowstone, Oostvaardersplassen, Knepp, Alladale, Carpathia) to ask what a purely ecological account of "success" leaves out, making it a natural capstone to the human-dimension threads opened on Days 4 and 6.

**"Rewilding for resilience: A call to integrate quantitative and qualitative approaches for monitoring rewilding"** (Hawkins & Jones, *Cambridge Prisms: Extinction*, 2026) — https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/cambridge-prisms-extinction/article/rewilding-for-resilience-a-call-to-integrate-quantitative-and-qualitative-approaches-for-monitoring-rewilding/1038BC2EB006D1FC12729B4459DA6B0E — ~10 min. A peer-reviewed, open-access perspective in *Cambridge Prisms: Extinction* (Cambridge University Press), by ecologists at CQUniversity Australia and the Swedish University of Agricultural Sciences. Published just months before this reading, it argues directly with the "static targets" style of monitoring described on Day 5 (Segar et al.'s indicator scoring, Neugarten et al.'s counterfactual designs), proposing that quantitative metrics be paired with qualitative, place-based and participatory methods — a concrete forward step on exactly the measurement bottleneck this week kept returning to.

## Guided summary

**What is genuinely settled.** Two things held up across every day this week without serious challenge. First, the mechanisms: Day 2's evidence that large herbivores and predators reshape landscapes through trophic cascades and ecosystem engineering rests on decades of independent, mechanistic, often experimental research (herbivore-exclusion plots, GPS-tracked wolves and beavers) that predates "rewilding" as a term and does not depend on any single flagship case. Even Day 4's Yellowstone dispute was a disagreement about the *size* of one specific effect, not about whether predators can affect vegetation through herbivores at all — nobody in that 2025 exchange argued the underlying mechanism was fake. Second, the trajectory: wherever researchers have actually measured outcomes with reasonable rigor — Segar et al.'s seven Rewilding Europe sites (Day 5), the Knepp–Boothby comparison (Day 3) — the ecological indicators moved in the direction rewilding theory predicts. The evidence base is thin, as Day 3's scoping review showed, but it is not contradictory: nothing this week surfaced a well-designed study showing rewilding making an ecosystem measurably worse.

**What is still open, ecologically.** The open question is not "does rewilding work" but "how much, how fast, and how transferable is any single result." Day 3's scoping review found real outcome evidence concentrated in a handful of well-funded sites in the Netherlands and Scandinavia, with invertebrates, fungi and soil processes almost invisible in the evidence base despite being central to Day 1's "trophic complexity" pillar. Day 5 showed why: even a well-designed monitoring framework like Segar et al.'s does not scale into a routine, comparable, field-wide standard, and Neugarten et al.'s counterfactual (BACI) methods — the gold standard for attributing a change to rewilding rather than to a wetter year or a broader recovery trend — are rarely applied. The Yellowstone dispute (Days 2 and 4) is the sharpest illustration: the single most intensively monitored rewilding case study in the world still could not settle, as of 2025, how large its own headline effect really is, because of exactly the kind of methodological shortcut (circular statistics, inconsistent survey plots) Day 5's methods primer flags as a known failure mode. Layered under all of this is Day 5's "shifting baseline syndrome": even a perfectly executed study is being judged against a reference point that each generation of scientists and managers may already perceive as more degraded than it is, which is a bias in the question, not just in the data.

**What is still open, conceptually and socially.** Day 4 showed that "rewilding" itself is not a single, agreed-upon intervention — it spans everything from Pleistocene-proxy introductions (never implemented at scale, and still contested thirty years on) to passive land abandonment, and Nogués-Bravo et al.'s "novel ecosystems" critique argues that in a sufficiently altered landscape, an intervention may not be "restoring" anything recognizable at all. That definitional looseness has real consequences downstream: Day 6 showed the EU's Nature Restoration Law and the 2025 IUCN Guidelines for Rewilding building real legal and financial machinery on top of a concept whose economic literature (Faure et al.) is still uncertain about basic cost-benefit accounting, and whose relationship to Indigenous-led land care is the subject of a live, unresolved published disagreement between Derham et al. and Fletcher. Today's Cary et al. reading pushes on the same seam from a different angle: applying history, anthropology, political ecology, human geography and sociology to five well-known projects, it argues that even a rewilding project judged an ecological success can still leave open questions about whose vision of "nature" it encodes, who was displaced or excluded to create it, and what a society gains or loses beyond species counts — questions ecological monitoring alone was never built to answer, and which Day 4's Acebes et al. stakeholder study showed can determine whether a project survives contact with the people living in its landscape at all.

**Three to five big open questions**, distilled from the week: (1) Can the field build monitoring standards rigorous and cheap enough to close Day 3's 22-versus-450 evidence gap without every site needing its own resource-intensive Delphi panel or BACI design? (2) When a historical baseline is genuinely unrecoverable or contested — Pleistocene megafauna, pre-agricultural Europe, a "shifted" baseline no one alive remembers accurately — what should rewilding be measured against instead, and who gets to decide? (3) Can rewilding's economic costs and benefits (Day 6: tourism gains, agricultural losses) be redistributed fairly enough that human-wildlife conflict (Day 4) stops functioning as a proxy fight over rural livelihoods? (4) Is "rewilding" — a term built on a 1990s wilderness ideal — the right umbrella for Indigenous-led land care at all, or does forcing that work under the label risk exactly the causality error Fletcher describes? (5) As legal mandates like the EU's Nature Restoration Law scale rewilding-style restoration up fast, can the science and governance groundwork (measurement, economics, community leadership) catch up before major, hard-to-reverse decisions get made on thin evidence?

**Where to go deeper.** For the ecological mechanisms, Pringle et al. (Day 2) and the Perino et al. framework (Day 1) remain the best entry points. For the evidence-quality debate, Hart et al.'s scoping review (Day 3) and Nogués-Bravo et al.'s critique (Day 4) are the two most direct statements of the skeptical case, worth reading against each other. For methods, Neugarten et al.'s impact-evaluation primer (Day 5) is written explicitly for non-specialists and is the clearest short explanation of why the Yellowstone dispute was possible at all. For where the field is trying to fix itself, today's two readings — Cary et al. on the social dimension and Hawkins & Jones on monitoring reform — plus the 2025 IUCN Guidelines for Rewilding (Day 6) are the most current statements of where practice is heading next.

## Questions to think about

- Of this week's open questions, which one seems most tractable to resolve with better science, and which one is really a values disagreement (about baselines, fairness, or whose vision of "nature" counts) that no amount of additional data will settle?
- Hawkins & Jones argue monitoring should be not just a measurement tool but "a means of learning, reflection, and transformation." Does adding qualitative, participatory methods to rewilding's evidence base risk making outcomes harder to compare across sites — the very problem Day 3 and Day 5 identified — or does it fix a different problem that quantitative metrics alone cannot?
- Having read all seven days, would you say rewilding is best described as a settled scientific framework still being implemented unevenly, or as a genuinely unsettled concept whose disagreements (Day 4's definitional disputes, Day 6's Indigenous-leadership debate) are still live enough to change what the framework itself means?
