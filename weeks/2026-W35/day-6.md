# Rewilding — Day 6/7: Policy, economics, and the turn toward Indigenous- and community-led governance

## Context

The week has, so far, mostly stayed inside ecology and its evidence base: how trophic cascades work (Day 2), what monitoring shows at real sites (Day 3), where the concept is contested (Day 4), and how rigorously "success" can actually be measured (Day 5). Today shifts registers to ask a different question: who decides whether a rewilding project happens, who pays for it, and who is supposed to benefit? Day 4 already showed that human-wildlife conflict is often a proxy fight over rural livelihoods rather than a pure wildlife problem, and Day 1 noted in passing that rewilding has moved from a fringe idea into national and EU policy. Today develops both threads properly: the legal machinery that is now pulling rewilding into mainstream European conservation policy, what the (thin, but growing) economic literature actually says about who gains and loses financially, and a genuinely live debate over whether the founding vision of rewilding — restoring "wild," minimally managed land — is compatible with placing Indigenous and local communities in the lead.

That last point is not a minor addendum. It connects directly to Day 4's finding that rewilding's early framing emphasized "nonhuman autonomy" and hands-off management, and to the Ferraro & Whitehead relational-rewilding response discussed there, which proposed centering Indigenous communities as one way to reduce conflict. Today's readings show that this is not just a nice-sounding principle: it is contested within conservation science itself, with a direct published disagreement about whether "rewilding" and "Indigenous-led land care" are even compatible framings, and a set of real-world governance experiments — a cross-border Indigenous bison treaty in North America, an Aboriginal-led island restoration in Australia — that let us look at what "leadership" actually means in practice rather than in the abstract.

## Today's readings

**"Economics of rewilding"** (Faure, Levrel & Quétier, *Ambio*, 2024) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11300785/ — ~10 min (focus on the results on which economic sectors are affected, and the discussion of subsidies and funding gaps; the full methods section on database screening can be skimmed). A peer-reviewed, open-access systematic review in *Ambio* (published on behalf of the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences) by researchers at CIRED (Centre International de Recherche sur l'Environnement et le Développement, Paris). It is the first systematic attempt to map what the economic literature on rewilding actually says, rather than what advocates or critics assume it says — the natural economic counterpart to Day 3's finding that ecological outcome evidence for rewilding is thin.

**"Rewilding and Indigenous Community‑Led Land Care"** (Derham, Mathews & Johnson, *Conservation Letters*, 2025) — https://conbio.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/conl.13090 — ~9 min (read the two proposed principles and the two case studies; the theoretical discussion of "wilderness" as a concept can be skimmed). A peer-reviewed, fully open-access paper in *Conservation Letters* (Society for Conservation Biology, published with Wiley) by conservation researchers at the University of Tasmania and La Trobe University. It argues directly with rewilding's founding "hands-off wilderness" framing — the same framing Day 4 flagged as a source of friction — and proposes a version of rewilding built around Indigenous leadership rather than human absence.

## Guided summary

Start with the policy machinery, because it explains why this question has suddenly become practically urgent rather than purely academic. In June 2024 the European Union adopted its Nature Restoration Law (formally Regulation 2024/1991), the first EU-wide binding legislation requiring member states to restore, rather than merely protect, degraded ecosystems: at least 20% of the EU's degraded land and sea area by 2030, and all ecosystems in need of restoration by 2050, with each country required to submit a national restoration plan. The law itself does not mandate rewilding by name — it sets outcome targets and lets member states choose methods — but the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN), the intergovernmental body that sets much of global conservation's technical standards, published new international Guidelines for Rewilding in 2025 specifically to give practitioners and policymakers a common technical and ethical standard for implementing rewilding-style restoration under exactly this kind of binding mandate. That timing matters: a legal requirement to restore ecosystems at scale creates real funding and political pressure to know what actually works and who should lead it, which is precisely where this week's earlier findings about thin evidence (Day 3) and contested baselines (Day 5) stop being abstract academic caveats and start being the kind of thing a national government has to defend in a restoration plan.

That funding pressure is where Faure, Levrel and Quétier's review becomes useful, because it is the first systematic attempt to find out what the economic literature on rewilding actually shows, rather than assuming it. Screening 257 peer-reviewed studies that discussed rewilding's economic dimensions, they found the field to be lopsided in a very specific way: positive economic effects cluster heavily in one sector — tourism and recreation, mentioned in roughly a quarter of the studies they screened — with much smaller and less consistent positive mentions for hunting and wild-meat sale, and comparatively little for forestry or fisheries. Their most concrete example is bison restoration on four Native American reservations, where the operating costs of maintaining a herd are partly offset through culled-animal sales (on the order of $300,000–400,000 a year, assuming roughly 400 bison culled), hunting licenses ($2,000–7,500 a year), and trophy auctions (over $5,000 each) — a rare case where the literature actually quantifies a revenue stream rather than gesturing at "economic opportunity." But the review's more important finding is what is missing rather than what is present: over a third of the studies they screened reported the overall economic impact of a given rewilding case as simply uncertain or unknown, and about a fifth reported a net negative impact, usually concentrated in agriculture — echoing Day 4's finding that livestock farmers often bear rewilding's costs while tourism operators capture its benefits. The authors' central conclusion is not that rewilding is uneconomical, but that almost no study rigorously totals up costs and benefits across sectors and stakeholders at the same site, which means public subsidy decisions and national restoration plans are currently being made against a genuinely thin economic evidence base — a fiscal-policy version of Day 3's "more theory than evidence" problem.

If economics tells you rewilding's costs and benefits are unevenly distributed, the second reading asks the harder question of whose judgment should decide how a landscape is actually managed. Derham, Mathews and Johnson start from an uncomfortable observation: rewilding's founding 1990s vision explicitly celebrated "wilderness" — landscapes with minimal ongoing human presence or management — and that framing has made rewilding, as practiced and described in most of the literature, largely irrelevant or even hostile to Indigenous communities in colonized countries, whose relationship to land has never been one of absence. Their response is not to abandon rewilding but to reframe two of its core principles: shifting the goal from "wilderness" (land without people) to the "creative agency of wild beings" (restoring functional, self-willed ecological processes, which does not require human absence), and treating restoration as a collaborative endeavor between humans and wildlife rather than a project done to a landscape. They illustrate this with two governance case studies rather than abstract argument. The Buffalo Treaty, first signed in 2014 by thirteen Indigenous nations from eight reservations spanning the US–Canada border, is a cross-border, Indigenous-negotiated agreement to restore bison across roughly 6.3 million acres of their historical range — notable not because it is ecologically unusual (bison reintroduction has precedent) but because the decision-making authority sits with the signatory nations themselves rather than a government wildlife agency. The Lungtalanana Cultural Restoration Project, on an island off Tasmania, is Aboriginal-led by the Tasmanian Aboriginal Centre in partnership with WWF-Australia and University of Tasmania researchers, working to return locally extinct native mammal species (starting with a genetically distinct Bass Strait Island wombat population) as part of a program framed explicitly around healing Country and restoring cultural, not just ecological, function to the island.

That proposal is not uncontested, and it is worth reading against a direct published rebuttal in the same journal. Michael-Shawn Fletcher, an Indigenous (Wiradjuri) geographer and paleoecologist at the University of Melbourne, argues in a 2025 response that "rewilding" and "Indigenous-led land care" are not simply two framings that can be reconciled with better principles — they are, in his view, close to incompatible, because "rewilding" as a concept was built on the premise that ecosystems are healthiest with humans removed from them, which directly contradicts the deep body of evidence (some of which this newsletter has not yet covered in detail) that Aboriginal land management for tens of thousands of years actively created and maintained the "natural" ecosystems that later conservationists mistook for untouched wilderness. On his reading, adopting Indigenous leadership as a delivery mechanism for a still-wilderness-rooted concept risks getting the causality backwards: instead of recognizing Indigenous management as the reason many landscapes are ecologically rich, it treats Indigenous communities as a means to a rewilding end. The disagreement does not undercut the Buffalo Treaty or Lungtalanana as real, functioning examples of Indigenous-led restoration — both authors would likely support those specific projects — but it does mean the field has not settled whether "rewilding" is the right umbrella term for that kind of work at all, a conceptual dispute that echoes Day 4's finding that even the definition of "rewilding" itself remains genuinely contested.

Put together, today's three sources describe a field whose legal and funding basis is moving faster than its economic or governance groundwork. The EU's Nature Restoration Law and the new IUCN guidelines mean rewilding-style restoration now has real legal teeth and public money behind it in Europe; but Faure et al. show that the economic case for who should receive that money, and how costs and benefits should be balanced across farmers, tourism operators, and taxpayers, remains genuinely underdeveloped. And Derham et al. versus Fletcher show that even the more socially progressive turn within rewilding — toward Indigenous leadership — is not a settled fix but an active argument about whether the underlying concept can be salvaged at all. None of this means rewilding policy is being made irresponsibly; it means, as with Day 3's ecological evidence and Day 5's measurement standards, that the field is building the plane while flying it, at exactly the moment its EU policy mandate is scaling up fastest.

## Questions to think about

- Faure et al. found tourism captures most of rewilding's documented economic upside while agriculture bears most of the documented downside. If you were designing a national restoration plan under the EU's Nature Restoration Law, how would you structure compensation so the sectors bearing the cost are not simply the ones with the least political voice?
- Fletcher argues that framing Indigenous-led conservation as an application of "rewilding" gets the history backwards, since many landscapes were shaped by long-term Indigenous management rather than by human absence. Does that argument change how you read Day 1's original three-pillar definition of rewilding (trophic complexity, natural disturbance, dispersal), or is it a separate question about terminology rather than ecology?
- The Buffalo Treaty and Lungtalanana both put decision-making authority with Indigenous nations or communities, not a state wildlife agency. What would "Indigenous-led," as opposed to "Indigenous-consulted," concretely require of a European rewilding project operating under the EU's Nature Restoration Law, where the land in question is not Indigenous land in the same legal sense?
