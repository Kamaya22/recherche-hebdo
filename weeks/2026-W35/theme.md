# Rewilding — the science and controversies behind returning large predators and keystone species to degraded ecosystems

Rewilding has moved from a fringe idea championed by a handful of conservation biologists in the 1990s to a mainstream approach now written into national and EU policy, with hundreds of active projects across Europe and North America. It matters because it represents a genuine shift in how conservation defines success: instead of preserving or restoring a fixed historical snapshot of a landscape, rewilding tries to restore the ecological *processes* — predation, large-herbivore disturbance, free movement of species — that let ecosystems regulate themselves with less permanent human management. That shift is scientifically interesting (it forces ecologists to grapple with what a "natural" baseline even means on a planet reshaped by 10,000+ years of human activity) and practically consequential, since rewilding is increasingly proposed as a tool for both biodiversity recovery and climate mitigation. It is also genuinely contested: reintroducing large carnivores and herbivores raises hard questions about historical baselines, human safety and livelihoods, and how much "wildness" a crowded, human-dominated landscape can actually accommodate.
