# Rewilding — Day 3/7: What landscape-scale case studies and long-term monitoring actually show

## Context

Day 1 gave the conceptual scaffolding — trophic complexity, disturbance, dispersal — and Day 2 showed the mechanisms behind one of those pillars in fine detail: how a predator's effect on an ecosystem engineer, or a large herbivore's effect on vegetation structure, can cascade through a landscape. Both readings drew on well-controlled, mechanistic studies (GPS-tracked wolves and beavers, herbivore-exclusion experiments) that isolate one process at a time. Today asks a blunter, more practical question: when you zoom out from a single mechanism to an entire rewilding *project* — a fenced or unfenced landscape, run for years or decades, with dozens of interacting species and stakeholders — what does the accumulated evidence actually show? Does the field have the kind of long-term, comparable monitoring data needed to say rewilding "works," or is that claim still resting mostly on a handful of famous, telegenic examples?

This turns out to be one of the more sobering threads in rewilding science, and it sets up Day 4's controversies directly. The mechanisms covered on Day 2 are individually well-evidenced; what is much less settled is how consistently they add up to measurable, landscape-scale outcomes across the hundreds of projects now underway, and how much of what we think we know rests on a small number of intensively studied sites rather than a broad, representative evidence base.

Today's two readings tackle this from opposite ends. The first is a systematic scoping review that asks, across the whole of Europe, how much peer-reviewed evidence for rewilding outcomes actually exists and where it comes from. The second is a concrete, on-the-ground case study — the kind of study the scoping review is counting — comparing the UK's oldest, best-known rewilding site to a much younger one just starting the same process, to see whether the trajectory holds up empirically over time.

## Today's readings

**"A scoping review of the scientific evidence base for rewilding in Europe"** (Hart, Haigh & Ciuti, *Biological Conservation*, 2023) — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0006320723003440 — ~12 min (read the abstract and the discussion of geographic and taxonomic bias; the search-methodology section can be skimmed). A peer-reviewed scoping review in *Biological Conservation* (Elsevier), a leading conservation-science journal, by ecologists at University College Dublin. It systematically searched the peer-reviewed literature for studies that actually measured outcomes on self-identified European rewilding sites — as opposed to papers that merely discuss rewilding conceptually — making it the most direct available answer to "how much real evidence is there, and where does it come from?"

**"Using landscape biodiversity metrics to assess rewilding: A comparison of the Knepp Estate and an agricultural baseline at Boothby Wildland"** (Pates et al., *Ecological Solutions and Evidence*, 2025) — https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/2688-8319.70090 — ~10 min (focus on the results and discussion; the statistical methods for partitioning diversity can be skimmed). A peer-reviewed, fully open-access study (Creative Commons Attribution license) in the British Ecological Society's *Ecological Solutions and Evidence*, led by researchers including Michael J. Crawley and William D. Pearse (Imperial College London). It directly compares plant biodiversity at Knepp Estate — rewilded since 2001 and the field's most cited UK case study — against Boothby Wildland, a site only beginning rewilding, giving a rare space-for-time picture of what a rewilding trajectory looks like in measured data rather than anecdote.

## Guided summary

Start with the scoping review, because it answers the question this week keeps circling back to: how much of "the evidence for rewilding" is actually evidence, versus theory or advocacy? Hart, Haigh and Ciuti searched the peer-reviewed literature systematically and, after screening, found only 22 studies that had actually measured ecological or social outcomes on a self-identified European rewilding site — a strikingly small number given how much has been written about rewilding conceptually (recall from Day 1 that the *Annual Review* synthesis counted over 450 papers using the term). That gap between "papers about rewilding" and "papers that measure what rewilding actually did" is itself the review's central finding: the field, as of 2023, remained more theory-led than evidence-based, a criticism the authors say has repeatedly slowed policy uptake because decision-makers cannot always point to hard outcome data.

The review also flagged two structural biases worth carrying into the rest of the week. Geographically, the available evidence is heavily skewed toward the Netherlands and Scandinavian countries — a handful of well-funded, well-studied sites (the Netherlands' Oostvaardersplassen among them) account for a disproportionate share of what we know, while rewilding projects in southern and eastern Europe are comparatively understudied even though they may face very different ecological and social conditions. Taxonomically, most outcome studies focus on mammals and plants — the large, visible, easy-to-monitor organisms rewilding projects are built around — leaving invertebrates, fungi, and soil processes comparatively invisible in the evidence base despite their role in the "trophic complexity" pillar from Day 1. None of this means rewilding does not work; the review's overall conclusion is that the existing evidence, while thin and unevenly distributed, does support the theoretical proposition that rewilding can restore biodiversity and deliver ecosystem services. But it is not, in the authors' words, a "panacea," and claims made on its behalf should be read against how few landscapes that claim is actually built on.

A complementary paper, Zoderer, von Colbe and Loos's 2025 systematic characterization of 89 European rewilding projects in *Conservation Letters* (open access), adds useful texture here: even setting monitoring quality aside, "rewilding" turns out to cover a wide diversity of practice. Using the European Rewilding Network's project list, the authors clustered projects by their ecological and socioeconomic goals and found no single template — some projects prioritize large-carnivore reintroduction, others focus on passive land abandonment or herbivore-driven grazing, and the balance between ecological and social/economic objectives varies enormously by country and funding model. That diversity is a reasonable response to the geographic bias problem in Hart et al.'s review (rewilding is not one intervention applied uniformly), but it also makes cross-project comparison harder, which loops back to why standardized long-term monitoring — not just more projects — is the field's stated bottleneck.

The Pates et al. Knepp–Boothby study is a useful antidote to that "thin evidence" picture, because it is exactly the kind of study the scoping review found too little of: a direct, quantified, multi-year comparison rather than a single snapshot. Knepp Estate, in West Sussex, is more than two decades into rewilding and is probably the single most-cited European case study in both the scientific and popular literature. Boothby Wildland, by contrast, was still in its first years of rewilding when the study was conducted, having recently come out of conventional agriculture. Rather than simply comparing species counts at one point in time, the researchers measured both α-diversity (how many species are found at a given site) and β-diversity (how much species composition varies across the landscape — a signal of habitat heterogeneity, not just raw richness) across both sites over several years. In the study's first year, Knepp's plant richness (102 species) exceeded Boothby's (78), consistent with Knepp's two-decade head start. By the following year, however, total richness at Boothby had risen to 182 species, versus 162 at Knepp — and statistical ordination of the community data (non-metric multidimensional scaling, a technique for visualizing how similar or different plant communities are) showed Boothby's plant assemblage moving measurably closer, year on year, to Knepp's more established, spatially heterogeneous pattern, while Knepp's own community stayed comparatively stable.

Two things are worth drawing out of that trajectory. First, it is a genuinely rare kind of data point for this field: a real longitudinal signal, not a before/after snapshot, showing a younger rewilding site's biodiversity metrics converging toward an older one's over successive years — direct empirical support, at landscape scale, for the idea that rewilding trajectories are somewhat predictable and self-reinforcing rather than idiosyncratic. Second, it should be read with the scoping review's caution firmly attached: this is one comparison, of two UK sites, over a handful of years, for one taxonomic group (plants). It strengthens the case that rewilding can produce fast, measurable biodiversity gains even very early in a project's life — Boothby's overall richness overtook Knepp's total within roughly a year — but it is exactly the kind of intensively monitored, well-funded, methodologically rich study that Hart et al. found concentrated in a small number of countries and sites, not yet the norm.

## Questions to think about

- Hart et al. found only 22 studies that actually measured outcomes on European rewilding sites, against a much larger body of conceptual and advocacy writing. What kinds of institutional incentives might explain why measuring outcomes lags so far behind promoting the idea?
- The Pates et al. study found Boothby's plant richness overtook Knepp's within about a year, even though Knepp had a 20-year head start. Does a fast early rise in species richness necessarily mean a rewilding project is "succeeding" in the fuller sense Day 1 described — restored ecological processes, not just a higher species count?
- Given the geographic and taxonomic biases Hart et al. identified (Netherlands/Scandinavia, mammals/plants), what would you want to see monitored — and where — before trusting that rewilding's benefits generalize to very different landscapes, such as Mediterranean shrubland or Eastern European forest?
