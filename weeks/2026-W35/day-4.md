# Rewilding — Day 4/7: Controversies and open debates — Pleistocene rewilding, novel ecosystems, and human-wildlife conflict

## Context

The first three days built a mostly sympathetic case for rewilding: a coherent conceptual framework (Day 1's three pillars), well-evidenced mechanisms for how large animals reshape landscapes (Day 2's trophic cascades and ecosystem engineering), and encouraging, if still thin, outcome data from real projects (Day 3's Knepp–Boothby comparison). But Day 2 and Day 3 also planted two seeds of doubt that today's reading is built to follow up on. Day 2 flagged that even rewilding's most famous flagship case — wolves, elk and willows in Yellowstone — is the subject of an unresolved 2025 methodological dispute over how large the effect actually is, and explicitly deferred the question of "who is right" to today. Day 3 found that only 22 of the field's 450-plus papers had actually measured outcomes on a real rewilding site, concluding the field remained "more theory-led than evidence-based." Today asks what happens when that gap between theory and evidence, and between promise and proof, turns into open scientific and social conflict.

Three distinct kinds of controversy run through rewilding, and it is worth keeping them separate because they get argued past each other constantly in both the scientific and popular literature. The first is a conceptual dispute: what counts as "rewilding" at all, and does the term paper over disagreements that matter — most sharply in "Pleistocene rewilding," the proposal to introduce living relatives of extinct megafauna as ecological proxies. The second is an ecological-uncertainty dispute: how confidently can scientists predict what a reintroduced species will actually do to a landscape that has changed enormously since any historical baseline, and could an intervention create a "novel" or unwanted ecosystem rather than a restored one? The third is a social and economic dispute that has nothing to do with the ecology being right or wrong: even a scientifically well-evidenced reintroduction can generate real conflict with the people who now share a landscape with a large carnivore or herbivore.

Today's two readings take up the conceptual and ecological-uncertainty disputes together (a single paper directly built to catalogue rewilding's open scientific disagreements) and the human dimension separately (a stakeholder study of exactly the kind of conflict rewilding can generate on the ground). The guided summary below then works through all three threads — including finally answering Day 2's cliffhanger about Yellowstone.

## Today's readings

**"Rewilding is the new Pandora's box in conservation"** (Nogués-Bravo, Simberloff, Rahbek & Sanders, *Current Biology*, 2016) — https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(15)01575-4 — ~8 min. A peer-reviewed Dispatch in *Current Biology* (Cell Press) by four conservation biologists and biogeographers, including Daniel Simberloff, one of the most cited invasion-biology researchers, and Carsten Rahbek, director of Copenhagen's Center for Macroecology, Evolution and Climate. Written explicitly as a critical counterpoint after rewilding's momentum had already built (following Day 1's Perino et al. framework), it lays out, in one place, most of the field's open scientific objections: definitional inconsistency, weak evidence for assumed mechanisms, and the risk of unpredictable "novel ecosystem" outcomes — which is why it anchors today's reading.

**"Unveiling human–wildlife interactions in the context of livestock grazing abandonment and the return of large carnivores, ungulates and vultures: A stakeholder perspective"** (Acebes, Muñoz-Gálvez, Iglesias-González, García-Llorente & López-García, *People and Nature*, 2024) — https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/pan3.10769 — ~12 min (read the results and discussion; the interview-methodology section can be skimmed). A peer-reviewed, fully open-access study in *People and Nature*, the British Ecological Society's dedicated interdisciplinary journal, by ecologists and social scientists at the Autonomous University of Madrid and CSIC. Based on structured stakeholder interviews in two Spanish mountain regions undergoing passive rewilding (land abandonment, not active reintroduction), it is one of the few studies that measures the human side of a rewilding-adjacent process directly, rather than treating it as an afterthought to the ecology.

## Guided summary

Start with the sharpest and oldest of the conceptual disputes: Pleistocene rewilding. In 2005, Josh Donlan and eleven co-authors published a widely read commentary in *Nature*, "Re-wilding North America," proposing that since North America's mammoths, camels, and giant ground sloths were driven extinct roughly 13,000 years ago (very likely with human help), conservationists should introduce living relatives of those lost species — African and Asian elephants standing in for mammoths, wild horses and camels for their extinct American cousins, cheetahs for the extinct American cheetah — to restore the ecological roles those animals once played. The proposal was framed as ecologically, evolutionarily, and even economically justified, and it pushed rewilding's founding "large animals shape ecosystems" logic to its most provocative conclusion: if the goal is restoring lost ecological function, why let extinction dates limit which species can do the restoring? A 2006 response in *Biological Conservation* by David and Daniel Rubenstein, Paul Sherman, and Thomas Gavin pushed back hard, arguing that substituting a living species for an extinct one is not restoration but a new, untested experiment — the introduced proxies had never interacted with North American ecosystems, might behave as invasive species, and could put existing native wildlife at risk, all to recreate an ecosystem no ecologist has directly observed. Commentators at the time compared the proposal, only half-jokingly, to *Jurassic Park*. Two decades on, essentially no large-scale Pleistocene rewilding project of this kind has been implemented in North America, which is itself a data point: the idea remains scientifically influential as a thought experiment about what "restoration" even means, but has not translated into practice at the scale its proponents imagined.

That distinction — a proxy species recreating a *function* versus recreating a *historical ecosystem* — is exactly what Nogués-Bravo and colleagues generalize in their 2016 Current Biology critique, and it is where the "novel ecosystems" idea becomes central. Ecologists Richard Hobbs, Eric Higgs and James Harris had argued in an influential 2009 *Trends in Ecology & Evolution* paper that many of today's ecosystems are already so altered by species invasions, land-use change, and climate change that they have crossed a threshold into being genuinely "novel" — assemblages of species and processes with no historical analogue, which cannot simply be pushed back toward an earlier baseline no matter how much intervention is applied. Nogués-Bravo et al. apply that logic as a warning: introduce a large herbivore or predator into a landscape that has already changed enormously since any plausible historical baseline, they argue, and you cannot be confident you are "restoring" anything — you may instead be creating a new, unpredictable ecosystem with its own risks, sometimes called (with the same dark humor as the Jurassic Park comparison) a "Frankenstein ecosystem." Their broader point is definitional: because "rewilding" has been used to describe everything from Pleistocene proxy introductions to simply removing fences and letting cattle-grazed land recover on its own, the label can obscure how differently confident scientists should be about the likely outcome in each specific case — Day 1's three-pillar framework was a genuine attempt to bring rigor to that variation, but Nogués-Bravo et al.'s point is that even a rigorous framework does not resolve how much you can actually predict for a given species in a given, already-altered landscape.

This is also where Day 2's unfinished business gets resolved. Recall the two competing 2025 *Global Ecology and Conservation* papers on Yellowstone: one reporting a roughly 1,500% increase in willow crown volume attributed to wolves suppressing elk browsing, the other — a formal comment by researchers at Utah State University and Colorado State University — arguing the finding was an artifact of circular statistics (using willow height data to both build and test a height-to-volume model) and inconsistent plot locations between the two survey years that were compared. Read alongside today's papers, the dispute is a concrete illustration of exactly the uncertainty Nogués-Bravo et al. describe in the abstract: even in the single most intensively studied, most photographed rewilding case study in the world, with decades of monitoring data, well-credentialed scientists on both sides of a 2025 peer-reviewed exchange still cannot agree on the *magnitude* of the core claimed effect. That is not evidence that trophic cascades are fake — Day 2's Pringle et al. and Gable et al. readings showed genuinely solid, independent mechanistic evidence for both large-herbivore ecosystem engineering and predator effects on ecosystem engineers — but it is evidence that translating "the mechanism is real" into "the mechanism produced this specific, large, well-quantified outcome at this specific site" is much harder than popular accounts of Yellowstone usually suggest.

The Acebes et al. stakeholder study shifts registers entirely, from whether an ecological claim is true to what happens to people when it is. Their interviews, conducted across two mountain regions in Spain undergoing passive rewilding — extensive livestock grazing declining and land being abandoned, which in turn lets wild ungulates, carnivores, and scavenging birds like vultures recolonize — found that nearly every stakeholder interviewed had noticed the return of large wildlife, and close to half explicitly linked it to land abandonment rather than any deliberate conservation program. But the study's central finding is not simply "conflict exists": it is that wildlife recovery is experienced by many pastoralists as "the last straw" layered on top of pre-existing economic pressure on traditional grazing — the labor of extensive pastoralism going unrecognized and undercompensated relative to industrial farming, rural depopulation, and policy that does not account for the external costs (habitat maintenance, fire-risk reduction) that grazing provides. In other words, human-wildlife conflict around rewilding is frequently not purely a wildlife problem that better fencing or compensation payments alone can solve; it can be a proxy fight over which rural livelihoods a society is willing to sustain, with the returning wolf, bear, or vulture serving as the most visible symptom of a much longer economic squeeze. That reframes what "success" for a rewilding process needs to include, adding a fourth consideration to Day 1's three ecological pillars: whether the people sharing the landscape are structurally positioned to coexist with what returns, independent of whether the ecology itself is working as intended.

It would be too tidy to end today on pure conflict, so it is worth noting that some in the field are trying to respond to exactly these critiques rather than dismiss them. A 2025 paper in *Ambio* by Kristy Ferraro and Toryn Whitehead reframes rewilding as fundamentally a *relational* process rather than a purely ecological or biological one, proposing five practical principles — reconsidering cultural values and perceptions of "nature," centering local and Indigenous communities in project design, being cautious and reversible when reviving lost human-wildlife relationships, and strengthening the science-policy connection — as a direct answer to the kind of conflict the Acebes study documents. Whether principles like these actually reduce conflict on the ground is itself an empirical question the field has not yet answered at scale (echoing Day 3's finding that measured outcome data lags far behind conceptual writing), but their existence shows that "human dimension" critiques of rewilding are increasingly being treated as a research problem in their own right, not dismissed as a distraction from the ecology.

## Questions to think about

- The Rubenstein et al. critique of Pleistocene rewilding argued that a living proxy species (an African elephant standing in for an extinct mammoth) cannot really restore a lost ecological role, because it never evolved alongside the species and landscape it would be reintroduced into. Do you find that argument persuasive, or does restoring an approximate ecological *function* seem valuable even without an exact evolutionary match?
- The Yellowstone willow dispute shows two credentialed research teams reaching opposite conclusions from related data as recently as 2025. Given that even flagship, decades-monitored case studies can be this contested, how much weight should a policymaker put on rewilding's more typical, far-less-studied projects when deciding whether to fund one?
- Acebes et al. found that wildlife recovery is often experienced as "the last straw" on top of pre-existing economic pressure on pastoralism, rather than as a self-contained wildlife problem. If that is right, what would a solution actually have to include beyond compensation payments or better livestock protection?
