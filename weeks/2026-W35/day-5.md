# Rewilding — Day 5/7: How ecologists define baselines and measure rewilding's success

## Context

Two threads from earlier this week converge today. Day 3 found that, despite hundreds of papers using the word "rewilding," a systematic scoping review turned up only 22 studies that had actually measured ecological or social outcomes on a real rewilding site — the field, as of 2023, remained "more theory-led than evidence-based." Day 4 showed what happens when that thin evidence base gets stress-tested: even at Yellowstone, the single most intensively monitored rewilding case study in the world, two credentialed 2025 research teams reached opposite conclusions about the size of the core wolf-elk-willow effect, with the dispute partly hinging on a technical methods problem — using the same willow-height data to both build and test a statistical model, and comparing survey plots that were not consistently located between the two years being compared.

Both of those threads point to the same underlying question, which today addresses directly: when a rewilding project (or a skeptic of one) says it is "working" or "not working," what is that claim actually being measured against, and how rigorous is the method used to attribute a change to rewilding rather than to something else? This turns out to involve two distinct methodological problems that get conflated constantly. The first is a baseline problem: before you can say an ecosystem has improved, you need a reference point for what "improved" means, and deciding what that reference point should be is scientifically and psychologically harder than it sounds. The second is an attribution problem: even once you have measured a real change at a rewilding site, you still need a credible way to rule out the possibility that the change would have happened anyway — from land abandonment, climate trends, or simple chance — without the rewilding intervention.

Today's two readings tackle these from opposite ends of the practical pipeline: one is a rare example of a rewilding-specific measurement framework actually being applied across real sites, and the other is a 2025 methods primer written specifically to explain how conservation scientists try to prove that an intervention, rather than something else, caused an observed outcome. The guided summary below uses both, plus the baseline-psychology literature, to explain exactly why Day 4's Yellowstone dispute was possible in the first place — and what it would take to avoid it.

## Today's readings

**"Expert-based assessment of rewilding indicates progress at site-level, yet challenges for upscaling"** (Segar, Pereira, Filgueiras, Karamanlidis, Saavedra & Fernández, *Ecography*, 2022) — https://nsojournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ecog.05836 — ~10 min (focus on the methods summary and the results across sites; the technical description of the Delphi protocol can be skimmed). A peer-reviewed, fully open-access study in *Ecography* (Nordic Society Oikos, published with Wiley) by ecologists at the German Centre for Integrative Biodiversity Research (iDiv), Martin Luther University Halle-Wittenberg, and Rewilding Europe. It is one of the few published attempts to turn Day 1's three-pillar rewilding framework into an actual, repeatable measurement instrument and apply it to real landscapes over time — exactly the kind of outcome-measuring study Day 3's scoping review found too little of.

**"An introduction to impact evaluation for conservation"** (Neugarten, Rodewald, Eklund & O'Garra, *Conservation Science and Practice*, 2025) — https://conbio.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/csp2.70169 — ~10 min (read the sections on how impact evaluation differs from monitoring and on counterfactual methods; the funding-and-practicalities section can be skimmed). A peer-reviewed, fully open-access primer in the Society for Conservation Biology's *Conservation Science and Practice*, opening a special issue on closing the gap between impact-evaluation research and conservation practice. Written explicitly as a non-technical introduction for practitioners, it lays out — in plain terms — the causal-inference toolkit (control sites, counterfactuals, attribution) that Day 4's Yellowstone dispute shows what can go wrong without.

## Guided summary

Start with the measurement side, because it is the more concrete of today's two problems. Segar and colleagues took Day 1's three-pillar rewilding framework (trophic complexity, natural disturbance, dispersal) — until then mostly a conceptual scaffold — and operationalized it into 19 measurable indicators: ten tracking the intensity of ongoing human intervention in a landscape (fencing, supplementary feeding, culling) and nine tracking ecological health (species richness, natural disturbance regimes, connectivity). Because no single dataset covers all of this consistently across sites, they used a Delphi technique — an iterative, structured process for building consensus among a panel of experts through repeated, anonymized rounds of scoring and feedback — to have specialists familiar with each landscape rate it against the 19 indicators at two points in time. Applied to seven of Rewilding Europe's operational landscapes, the results were genuinely informative: five sites saw their overall rewilding score rise over the monitoring period, and in four of those five, a documented drop in human intervention was accompanied by a measured rise in ecological health — a reassuring sign that reducing management really does tend to move these two dimensions together, as the underlying theory predicts. The Central Apennines showed the largest gain, improving on 14 of the 19 indicators. But two sites, the Rhodope and Velebit Mountains, saw their scores fall, and the paper is candid that the method itself does not scale easily: expert scoring is inherently somewhat subjective, indicators are not standardized in the same way across every project, and running a fresh Delphi panel for every site is too resource-intensive to become a routine, field-wide monitoring standard. In other words, Segar et al. solved the "how do we turn a vague concept into a number" problem well enough to detect real change at seven sites, but that solution does not yet generalize into the kind of large-scale, comparable evidence base Day 3's review found missing.

Even a perfectly executed version of Segar et al.'s scoring, however, only tells you that a site's condition changed — not that rewilding caused the change. That distinction is exactly what Neugarten and colleagues' primer is built to explain, and it maps directly onto Day 4's Yellowstone dispute. They draw a sharp line between *monitoring* (tracking indicators over time at a site, which is what the Delphi-based rewilding score does) and *impact evaluation* (establishing, with a credible counterfactual, what would have happened without the intervention). The strongest counterfactual designs, they explain, compare an "impact" site against a "control" site that is environmentally similar but did not receive the intervention, measured both before and after — a before-after-control-impact, or BACI, design — because measuring only the impact site before and after cannot rule out that the same change (a wetter year, a broader recovery trend, a change in land use nearby) would have happened regardless. Read against that standard, the flaw that Day 4's comment paper identified in the 2025 Yellowstone willow study becomes much easier to see clearly: the original analysis used willow-height measurements to build its own predictive model and then used the same data to test that model (a circularity that inflates apparent fit), and it also compared survey plots between years that were not consistently the same physical locations — undermining exactly the kind of stable, comparable "before" and "after" measurement that a rigorous counterfactual design requires. The dispute was not really about whether wolves affect willows in principle (Day 2's mechanistic evidence for that is solid); it was about whether this particular before/after comparison was constructed rigorously enough to support the specific, large effect size being claimed.

Underneath both of these methodological layers sits a subtler, more psychological problem: even with a perfect indicator set and a perfect counterfactual design, you still have to decide what baseline condition counts as "healthy" or "wild" in the first place — and human judgment about that turns out to be systematically biased. Masashi Soga and Kevin Gaston's influential 2018 review in *Frontiers in Ecology and the Environment* names this "shifting baseline syndrome": each generation tends to treat the environmental conditions it personally remembers from childhood or early career as the normal, natural reference point, forgetting how much degradation had already occurred by that time. Two distinct mechanisms drive it — "generational amnesia," where knowledge of past conditions is simply never transmitted to younger practitioners and scientists, and "personal amnesia," where individuals gradually forget their own earlier observations. The practical consequence for rewilding is direct: if a scientist's or a manager's working definition of a "recovered" landscape is itself already a somewhat degraded baseline relative to, say, pre-industrial or pre-agricultural conditions, then a rewilding project can score well against that baseline while still falling well short of restoring the full historical scale of wildlife abundance or ecological process — success, in other words, can be relative to an already-shifted goalpost. A 2024 follow-up by the same authors in *BioScience*, synthesizing 73 case studies across environmental domains worldwide, found the pattern to be widespread rather than a one-off finding, with younger cohorts consistently reporting lower baselines than older ones across contexts as different as fisheries, climate, and biodiversity. Taken together with Day 4's "novel ecosystems" argument — that many landscapes have changed so much that no single historical state is even recoverable — the baseline problem means rewilding's evidence base is not just thin (Day 3) and occasionally under-attributed (this week's Yellowstone case); the target it is being measured against is itself a moving, contested choice, and that choice is rarely made explicit in either scientific papers or popular reporting.

Put the three layers together and Day 3 and Day 4's findings look less like separate problems and more like symptoms of the same methodological chain: you need a defensible baseline (contested, and subject to shifting-baseline bias), operationalized into indicators that can actually be measured consistently (solvable in principle, as Segar et al. show, but not yet scalable), compared against a credible counterfactual to establish that rewilding — not something else — produced the observed change (the tool Neugarten et al. describe, and the one Day 4's Yellowstone critique shows being skipped or done poorly). A rewilding claim that has not been explicit about all three is not necessarily wrong, but it is, at minimum, incomplete.

## Questions to think about

- Segar et al.'s Delphi-based scoring worked well for seven closely studied sites but does not scale easily to hundreds of projects. If you had to choose between a cheaper, less rigorous method that could be applied everywhere and an expensive, rigorous method that only a few flagship sites can afford, which would you prioritize for building rewilding's evidence base, and why?
- Shifting baseline syndrome implies that today's practitioners may be aiming for a target that is already a degraded version of what existed historically. Should rewilding projects try to reconstruct much older, harder-to-verify historical baselines (echoing Day 4's Pleistocene rewilding debate), or is a more recent, well-documented baseline a more honest and practical goal?
- Now that you have seen the BACI/counterfactual standard Neugarten et al. describe, look back at Day 3's Knepp–Boothby comparison: it tracked two sites over time but did not include a genuine unrewilded control. Does that change how much weight you would put on its finding that Boothby's plant diversity converged toward Knepp's within about a year?
