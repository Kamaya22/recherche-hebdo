# The skin microbiome — Day 3/7: Eczema — dysbiosis, *S. aureus* and the barrier-microbiome feedback loop

## Context

Two days ago, Day 1 established that skin hosts a structured, site-specific bacterial community, and flagged one concrete clinical fact as a preview: strains of "good" staph bacteria that kill *Staphylococcus aureus* are common on healthy skin but depleted on the skin of people with atopic dermatitis (eczema), and a phase 1 trial restoring one such strain (*Staphylococcus hominis* A9) to eczema patients was shown to be safe. Day 2 then filled in the mechanics of how any resident bacterium influences host skin at all — antimicrobial-peptide (AMP) induction, pattern-recognition-receptor (PRR) signaling, and a narrow early-life window in which the immune system learns to tolerate commensals rather than attack them.

Today puts those two threads together around a single disease. Atopic dermatitis affects up to one in five children in high-income countries, and of the three conditions this cycle covers (eczema, acne, wound healing), it is the one where the microbiome's causal role is most firmly established — not as a side effect of inflamed skin, but as an active participant in a loop that keeps making itself worse. Three things distinguish today's picture from Monday's: first, the dysbiosis in eczema is not just "fewer species" but a specific, repeatable shift toward *S. aureus* dominance that tracks with flare severity; second, the barrier and the microbiome drive each other in both directions, not one-way; and third, *S. aureus* does not just outcompete other bacteria passively — it runs its own social, toxin-producing program that depends on sensing its own population density. Day 4 will revisit some of these same bacterial-behavior ideas for acne, where, unlike here, the resident bacterium involved is not usually thought of as "bad" at all.

## Today's readings (~21 min)

**"Skin Microbiome in Atopic Dermatitis"** (Sofie M. Edslev, Tove Agner & Paal S. Andersen, *Acta Dermato-Venereologica*, 2020, vol. 100, article adv00164) — https://doi.org/10.2340/00015555-3514 — ~7 min. A peer-reviewed, fully open-access review (CC BY-NC) in the official journal of the Swedish dermatology society. It lays out the core epidemiological pattern this reading builds on: as eczema severity increases, bacterial diversity falls and *S. aureus* relative abundance rises, with the shift measurable even between lesional and non-lesional skin on the same patient.

**"The immunological and structural epidermal barrier dysfunction and skin microbiome in atopic dermatitis — an update"** (Tubanur Çetinarslan, Lisa Kümper & Regina Fölster-Holst, *Frontiers in Molecular Biosciences*, 2023, vol. 10, article 1159404) — https://doi.org/10.3389/fmolb.2023.1159404 — ~8 min (sections on filaggrin, barrier lipids and microbiome interplay). A peer-reviewed, open-access review focused specifically on today's central idea: that barrier defects (filaggrin loss, altered lipids, raised skin pH) favor *S. aureus* colonization, and that colonization in turn further damages the barrier — a two-way feedback loop rather than a one-directional cause.

**"Skin microbiota: pathogenic roles and implications in atopic dermatitis"** (Cong Huang, Fan Zhuo, Yang Guo et al., *Frontiers in Cellular and Infection Microbiology*, 2025, vol. 14, article 1518811) — https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2024.1518811 — ~6 min (sections on *S. aureus* virulence mechanisms and microbiota-based therapy). The most recent of the three, peer-reviewed and open access, with the most up-to-date treatment of therapeutic approaches — useful for cross-checking which interventions have moved from mouse models toward human trials.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with the pattern, because everything else today explains it. Across multiple studies summarized in the Edslev review, skin bacterial diversity on eczema patients falls as disease severity rises, and the species that fills the gap is overwhelmingly *S. aureus* — colonization rates during flares are far higher than on healthy skin or on the same patient's unaffected skin, and *S. aureus* abundance tracks disease severity closely enough that it has been proposed as a biomarker for flares. This is the same *S. aureus*-vs-protective-commensal balance Day 1 introduced; today's reading explains why the imbalance, once started, tends to entrench itself.

The entrenching mechanism is a genuine two-way feedback loop, and it is worth being precise about both directions. In the barrier-to-microbe direction: many eczema patients carry loss-of-function mutations in *filaggrin*, a protein essential for building the outermost, water-retaining layer of skin; filaggrin breakdown products are normally mildly acidic and help keep skin pH low, and low pH itself inhibits *S. aureus* growth while favoring protective commensals. Losing filaggrin therefore raises skin pH, directly creates a more hospitable chemical environment for *S. aureus*, and separately exposes more of the sticky surface proteins *S. aureus* uses to adhere to corneocytes (the dead, flattened cells of the outer skin layer). In the microbe-to-barrier direction: once established, *S. aureus* secretes proteases that degrade the very structural proteins (including filaggrin itself) and tight-junction components holding the barrier together, so colonization does not just exploit a weak barrier — it actively weakens it further. The Çetinarslan review's account of this loop is the clearest reason researchers now describe eczema as a disease where barrier dysfunction and dysbiosis cause each other, rather than debating which comes first.

*S. aureus* is not merely occupying space more successfully; it is actively signaling. A 2013 study in *Nature* found that *S. aureus* δ-toxin — a small peptide and the most abundant member of its phenol-soluble-modulin (PSM) family — directly triggers mast cells to degranulate, through a calcium-and-PI3-kinase pathway that does not require the IgE-antibody machinery classical allergic reactions use, though IgE amplifies the effect when present; mast-cell-deficient mice were protected from δ-toxin-driven skin disease, and restoring mast cells restored it. *S. aureus* strains isolated from eczema patients produce unusually large amounts of δ-toxin, giving a direct molecular link from "which bacterium is present" to "how much itching and inflammation results" — complementing Day 2's PRR-and-AMP picture with a route that bypasses pattern recognition altogether and acts straight on mast cells.

Toxin production itself is switched on by a bacterial communication system called Agr (accessory gene regulator), a quorum-sensing circuit: *S. aureus* only ramps up δ-toxin and related virulence factors once it senses, via small secreted peptides, that its own population density has crossed a threshold. A 2020 *Science Translational Medicine* study followed infants and found that *S. aureus* strains carrying a functional Agr system at six months of age were associated with later development of eczema, while strains that had picked up Agr-disabling mutations were not — and in mice, a working Agr system was necessary both for *S. aureus* to colonize skin efficiently and to trigger eczema-like inflammation. This connects directly to Day 2's early-life tolerance window, but as a distinct mechanism: Day 2 was about whether the host's immune system learns to tolerate commensals; today's finding is about which strain of *S. aureus* an infant happens to pick up, and whether that strain's own internal "volume control" for virulence is switched on.

Quorum sensing turns out to cut both ways, and this is where Day 1's protective bacteria reappear with new mechanistic depth. A 2019 *Science Translational Medicine* study (from the same lab that later identified *S. hominis* A9) found that several species of coagulase-negative staphylococci — the broad group of "good" staph species that includes *S. epidermidis* and *S. hominis* — secrete their own small peptides that jam *S. aureus*'s Agr receptor, measurably reducing its toxin output without necessarily killing it outright. In skin samples from eczema patients, the ratio of these protective peptides to *S. aureus* was lower than on healthy skin, suggesting that losing the "volume control override," not just losing raw antibacterial killing power, is part of how protective commensals normally keep *S. aureus* in check. This reframes the bacteriotherapy approach Day 1 described: restoring a strain like *S. hominis* A9 may work partly by directly inhibiting *S. aureus* and partly by quieting its quorum-sensing system so it stops producing toxins even where it still persists. As of this research, the phase 1 ShA9 trial's safety-focused follow-up — a phase 2 study (TIME-2), restricted to patients whose lesions test positive for *S. aureus* — was registered and recruiting, with results not yet published. A different early-stage strategy targets the bacterium more directly without using live bacteria at all: a 2025 open-label clinical study in *Frontiers in Immunology* reported that a topical gel containing an engineered bacteriophage-derived enzyme ("endolysin"), which lyses *S. aureus* cell walls on contact, reduced lesion scores, *S. aureus* load, and inflammatory markers in adults with mild-to-moderate eczema — a reminder that "treating the microbiome" covers a wider toolkit than adding back a good bacterium.

## Questions to think about

- The barrier-microbiome loop in eczema runs in both directions (weak barrier → more *S. aureus*; more *S. aureus* → weaker barrier). For a patient already in a flare, what would this imply about whether treating the barrier alone, or the bacteria alone, is likely to be enough — and how would you design a trial to tell which intervention point matters more?
- The 2020 infant study linked a specific bacterial trait (a functional Agr quorum-sensing system) in the colonizing *S. aureus* strain, not just colonization itself, to later eczema risk. If strain-level traits matter this much, what does that imply for the usefulness of "microbiome test kits" that report only which species are present?
- Three strategies appeared today for dealing with *S. aureus* in eczema: restoring a protective commensal (bacteriotherapy), jamming *S. aureus*'s own quorum-sensing signal, and directly lysing *S. aureus* with an engineered enzyme. What would make one of these preferable to the others as a long-term, repeatedly-applied treatment rather than a one-off intervention?
