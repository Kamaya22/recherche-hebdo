# The skin microbiome

Start: 2026-11-21

How resident bacteria shape eczema, acne, wound healing and immune tone.

## Why it matters

Skin is the body's largest organ and its outermost interface with the environment, and it is covered — far more densely than once assumed — by a stable community of bacteria, fungi, viruses and mites that most people never think about unless something goes wrong. Since DNA-sequencing studies replaced lab culturing around 2008-2009, researchers have found that healthy skin hosts hundreds of bacterial species occupying distinct ecological niches (dry, moist, oily) that differ more by body site than by person, and that this community is not a passive bystander: it actively trains and restrains the skin's immune system, competes with dangerous pathogens for space and nutrients, and helps maintain the physical barrier that keeps water in and pathogens out. When that balance shifts — a phenomenon researchers call dysbiosis — the consequences show up in some of the most common conditions in medicine: atopic dermatitis (eczema) affects up to 20% of children in high-income countries and is tightly linked to loss of microbial diversity and overgrowth of *Staphylococcus aureus*; acne, affecting the vast majority of adolescents at some point, involves not an infection but a shift in the behavior of a bacterium, *Cutibacterium acnes*, that is present on virtually everyone's skin; and impaired wound healing, a major burden in aging and diabetic populations, is shaped by which microbes colonize a wound and how they interact with the repair process. The topic is also a useful lens on modern biology more broadly: skin is comparatively easy to sample non-invasively and image, which has made it something of a model system for working out how any resident microbial community talks to a host immune system — lessons that increasingly feed back into gut-microbiome and cancer-immunotherapy research. Finally, the science has matured just enough to produce real, testable therapeutic candidates (bacteriotherapy sprays, engineered commensals, phage therapy), which makes this a live area where mechanistic understanding and consumer-facing hype (skincare marketing, home "microbiome test kits") are often several steps apart — a gap worth being able to evaluate critically.

## Tentative 7-day outline

- **Day 1 — Panorama**: what the skin microbiome is, how DNA sequencing revealed its true diversity, how it is organized across body sites, and why it matters for health.
- **Day 2 — Mechanisms**: how resident bacteria communicate with skin immune cells and the epidermal barrier — antimicrobial peptides, pattern-recognition receptors, and "immune tone."
- **Day 3 — Eczema**: dysbiosis in atopic dermatitis, the central role of *S. aureus*, the barrier–microbiome feedback loop, and early commensal-bacteria therapies.
- **Day 4 — Acne**: *Cutibacterium acnes* as a commensal rather than a pathogen, strain-level diversity, sebum and inflammation, and why "killing the bacteria" is the wrong frame.
- **Day 5 — Wound healing and aging**: how the microbiome shapes tissue repair, chronic and diabetic wounds, and how the community itself changes across the lifespan.
- **Day 6 — Methods and controversies**: metagenomics vs. culturing, the limits of mouse models, reproducibility concerns, and the gap between mechanistic evidence and skincare-industry claims.
- **Day 7 — Synthesis**: what is settled vs. genuinely open, 3-5 big open questions, and pointers for going deeper.

The outline is a guide, not a contract, and later days may shift based on what the research turns up.
