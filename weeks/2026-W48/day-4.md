# The skin microbiome — Day 4/7: Acne — why a resident bacterium, not an infection, drives it

## Context

Two days ago, Day 3 looked at eczema as a case of genuine dysbiosis: protective staph species depleted, *Staphylococcus aureus* overgrowing, and a two-way feedback loop between a weakened skin barrier and an increasingly dominant, toxin-producing bacterium. Today's disease looks superficially similar — a very common skin condition, a single bacterium usually named as the culprit — but the underlying biology turns out to be almost the opposite shape. The bacterium involved in acne, *Cutibacterium acnes* (formerly *Propionibacterium acnes*), is not overgrowing and is not rare on healthy skin: it is one of the most abundant residents of the oily, sebum-rich skin sites Day 1 described, present at broadly similar levels on people with and without acne. For decades acne was nonetheless treated clinically as if it were a bacterial infection to be cleared with antibiotics. That framing has been steadily dismantled over the last fifteen years, and today's reading explains what replaced it.

Three ideas carry today's reading, and all three echo mechanisms from earlier this week in a new context. First, what matters in acne is not how much *C. acnes* is present but which strains, and how they behave — a strain-level story that parallels Day 3's finding that an *S. aureus* strain's Agr quorum-sensing status, not just its presence, predicted eczema risk. Second, the trigger that tips a harmless resident into an inflammatory one is substantially environmental: a change in the skin's own sebum, driven by puberty's hormonal shift, rather than the bacterium acquiring new virulence genes from nowhere. Third, the resulting inflammation runs through much of the same innate-immune signaling Day 2 introduced for "immune tone" in general — except that here, a resident bacterium's normal metabolic activity, amplified by an altered local environment, pushes that tone from tolerance toward chronic low-grade inflammation. Day 5 will pick up a third skin condition, impaired wound healing, where the same cast of mechanisms (resident bacteria, host signaling, environmental context) combines yet again, in a third distinct way.

## Today's readings (~21 min)

**"The Microbiome and Acne: Perspectives for Treatment"** (Clio Dessinioti & Andreas Katsambas, *Dermatology and Therapy*, 2024, vol. 14, no. 1, pp. 31–44) — https://doi.org/10.1007/s13555-023-01079-8 — ~8 min. A peer-reviewed, open-access review in a Springer/Adis journal indexed in PubMed and DOAJ. It is the backbone reading for today: it lays out the evidence for strain-level (phylotype) differences between acne-associated and healthy-skin *C. acnes*, and surveys treatment approaches that specifically try to work with the microbiome — benzoyl peroxide, topical/oral probiotics, bacteriotherapy and phage therapy — rather than simply killing bacteria indiscriminately.

**"Exploring Acne Treatments: From Pathophysiological Mechanisms to Emerging Therapies"** (Hyun Jee Kim & Yeong Ho Kim, *International Journal of Molecular Sciences*, 2024, vol. 25, no. 10, article 5302) — https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms25105302 — ~7 min (sections on sebum, microbiome and inflammation). A peer-reviewed, fully open-access review (MDPI) that is more mechanistic on the sebum side: it distinguishes "hyperseborrhea" (too much sebum) from "dysseborrhea" (sebum with an altered composition) as two separate contributors to *C. acnes* proliferation, and covers emerging treatments including bacteriophages, probiotics and peptides.

**"A review of skin immune processes in acne"** (Zhongcai Jin, Yujun Song & Li He, *Frontiers in Immunology*, 2023, vol. 14, article 1324930) — https://doi.org/10.3389/fimmu.2023.1324930 — ~6 min (sections on microbial and lipid stimulators and innate immune response). A peer-reviewed, open-access review focused specifically on today's immune-signaling thread: how microbial and lipid signals together activate innate and then adaptive immune responses in acne-prone skin.

## Guided summary (~9 min)

Start with the fact that reframes the whole topic: *C. acnes* is present on essentially everyone's skin, acne-prone or not, and multiple studies have found no convincing link between total bacterial load and either the presence or the severity of acne. What differs is strain composition. Within the species, researchers distinguish phylotypes (named I, II, III, with subtypes like IA1, IA2, IB, IC), and acne-associated skin tends to show a loss of phylotype diversity alongside dominance by a small number of lineages — most consistently IA1 in Western cohorts, with one review reporting it in roughly 84% of acne patients versus a much lower share of healthy controls, while studies from Japan instead point to IA2 as the acne-associated lineage there, a geographic difference that is still not fully explained. The IA1 lineage has also been linked in laboratory work to stronger biofilm formation — bacteria embedded in a self-produced protective matrix — which may let it persist and signal to the immune system more effectively than other lineages. Not every study agrees on the details (one found no phylotype-severity link, and a 2024 review notes that adult-onset acne in women does not map onto a specific subtype the way adolescent acne does), which is itself a useful reminder that "acne" is not one uniform process at the strain level.

If strain alone does not fully explain acne, the environment the bacterium lives in does a great deal of the remaining work, and that environment is sebum. Puberty triggers a sharp, androgen-driven rise in sebum production (hyperseborrhea), which both feeds *C. acnes* growth — the bacterium metabolizes sebum lipids for energy — and changes the sebum's own chemistry (dysseborrhea). Two specific lipid changes recur across the literature: squalene, which normally makes up roughly 10–15% of sebum, oxidizes readily under UV, ozone and other exposures into squalene peroxide, a compound that is comedogenic in its own right, activates PPAR receptors, drives keratinocyte proliferation, and has been shown to induce a distinct population of pro-inflammatory (TREM2-expressing) macrophages found in acne lesions; separately, linoleic acid — an unsaturated fatty acid important for a healthy skin barrier — tends to be depleted in the skin surface lipids of acne patients, which is hypothesized to contribute to follicular hyperkeratosis, the excess shedding and plugging of follicle cells that helps form the comedo (clogged pore) in the first place. None of this requires *C. acnes* to behave abnormally — it only requires the chemical environment around it to shift.

Once a follicle is plugged and sebum-rich, *C. acnes* does not merely sit passively inside it. It metabolizes sebum triglycerides into free fatty acids and produces its own enzymes and surface molecules that are detected by the innate immune system — keratinocytes, monocytes and macrophages recognize these signals and respond by producing pro-inflammatory cytokines, a pattern the Jin, Song & He review describes as running through much the same kind of pattern-recognition machinery Day 2 covered for "immune tone" in general. The difference from Day 2's healthy-skin picture is one of degree and context rather than mechanism: a bacterium whose signals would ordinarily be tolerated is, in an oxidized, lipid-altered, biofilm-protected follicular environment, instead driving a sustained local inflammatory response — visible clinically as the progression from a comedo to an inflamed papule or pustule. This is also a cleaner illustration than Day 3's *S. aureus* case of a theme introduced then: a resident species does not need to become more abundant, or genuinely pathogenic, to drive disease — a shift in its surroundings can be enough to change what its ordinary presence means to the host.

That reframing has practical consequences for treatment, and it is where today's reading connects most directly to the broader theme of this cycle. Standard acne treatments — oral and topical antibiotics, benzoyl peroxide — work by killing bacteria broadly, which reduces *C. acnes* load but also disrupts the rest of the follicular and skin microbiome and, with antibiotics, contributes to the antimicrobial-resistance problem Day 3's phage-therapy discussion gestured at. The treatments reviewed today instead try to work with the microbiome rather than scorching it: probiotic strains intended to rebalance skin flora, and bacteriophages — viruses that infect bacteria — engineered or selected to target *C. acnes* specifically. A 2023 study in *Nature Communications* isolated eight novel *C. acnes*-targeting phages and showed, in a mouse model of *C. acnes*-induced acne-like lesions, that topical phage application reduced inflammatory markers (including the neutrophil-recruiting chemokine CXCL2) and neutrophil infiltration compared with untreated infected mice — the first demonstration of phage therapy applied topically rather than by injection for this bacterium. In humans, a phase 1 randomized, vehicle-controlled cosmetic trial of a topical phage gel (BX001, 75 participants with mild-to-moderate acne) published in 2022 in *Skin Health and Disease* found it safe and well tolerated and significantly reduced facial *C. acnes* burden relative to vehicle — but the same product's subsequent phase 2 trial, reported by the manufacturer, did not show a meaningful efficacy difference from vehicle on acne severity itself. That gap between "safely reduces the bacterium" and "measurably improves the disease" is worth holding onto: it is an early, concrete example of the kind of claim that can look compelling at an early trial stage and not hold up at a larger one — a theme Day 6 will return to when it turns to methods, reproducibility and the broader gap between mechanistic evidence and marketing claims.

## Questions to think about

- Acne and eczema both involve a skin bacterium implicated in disease, but in acne the bacterium is abundant and present on virtually everyone, while in eczema one species (*S. aureus*) genuinely overgrows. Given that difference, would you expect "kill more of the bacterium" to be a more or less promising strategy for acne than it is for eczema, and why?
- The IA1-in-the-West versus IA2-in-Japan pattern suggests that which *C. acnes* lineage is "acne-associated" may depend on the host population, not just the bacterium. What kinds of studies would help distinguish whether this reflects genuine host-strain co-evolution, versus differences in diet, climate, or study methodology between regions?
- The BX001 phage gel reduced *C. acnes* levels safely in its phase 1 trial but did not beat vehicle on efficacy in phase 2. What does that gap suggest about how much weight to put on a phase 1 "proof of mechanism" result when judging whether a microbiome-targeted treatment is likely to work?
