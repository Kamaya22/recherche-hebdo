# Exoplanet atmospheres and the search for biosignatures

## Why this theme matters

We have now confirmed more than 5,000 planets orbiting other stars, and for a growing subset of them we can do something that felt like science fiction a decade ago: read the chemical composition of their atmospheres from thousands of light-years away. The James Webb Space Telescope has turned this from a handful of noisy detections into a routine capability, and in the process it has moved the search for extraterrestrial life from philosophy into a concrete, falsifiable observational program. Understanding how this works — and how easily a promising signal can be over-interpreted — is essential to reading the announcements that will keep coming out of this field for the rest of the decade. This week also sits at the intersection of physics, chemistry, geology and biology, making it a good case study in how a genuinely multidisciplinary scientific question gets tackled. It is also a live methodological drama: the same week can bring a headline claim of a possible biosignature and a rebuttal paper questioning the statistics behind it, which makes it an excellent lens on how science actually adjudicates uncertain evidence.

## Tentative 7-day outline

1. **Panorama** — what exoplanet atmosphere science is, the core observational techniques (transit, transmission and emission spectroscopy), and why this became a mainstream research field only in the last ~15 years.
2. **Mechanisms** — how astronomers actually extract a spectrum from starlight: transits, light curves, molecular absorption bands, and the atmospheric retrieval models used to turn data into a chemical inventory.
3. **Key findings** — what Hubble and JWST have actually detected so far: water, CO2, SO2 (photochemistry), and the more contested detections on smaller, cooler, more Earth-like worlds.
4. **Controversies and open debates** — the K2-18b dimethyl sulfide saga, the problem of abiotic "false positive" biosignatures (e.g. oxygen), and why many researchers now reject the idea of a single "silver bullet" biosignature gas.
5. **Methods: how we know what we know** — statistical inference under heavy degeneracy, laboratory spectroscopy of candidate molecules, and the instruments (NIRSpec, MIRI, and next-generation missions like Ariel and the Habitable Worlds Observatory) that make or break what is detectable.
6. **Applications and societal implications** — what a credible life-detection claim would need to look like, how the field communicates uncertain results to the public, and how these questions shape multi-billion-dollar mission planning.
7. **Synthesis** — what is genuinely settled versus still open, the week's big open questions, and pointers for going further.

This outline is a guide, not a contract — later days will be adjusted based on what the readings turn up.
