# Exoplanet atmospheres and the search for biosignatures — Day 4/7: The K2-18 b controversy and why there is no "silver bullet" biosignature

## Context

The first three days of this week moved from technique (Day 1) to mechanics (Day 2) to results (Day 3), and Day 3 ended by naming the planet where all of that machinery collided head-on with the public imagination: K2-18 b, a temperate sub-Neptune whose atmosphere Nikku Madhusudhan's Cambridge-led team has argued may show signs of dimethyl sulfide (DMS) and dimethyl disulfide (DMDS) — on Earth, gases produced essentially only by marine life such as phytoplankton. First flagged as a faint hint in an earlier round of JWST data, the claim was pushed much further in April 2025 with new mid-infrared observations, and it became one of the most widely reported "possible sign of life" stories in the field's history. It has also become the clearest case study yet of Day 2's retrieval-degeneracy problem playing out in public, in real time, with the same underlying JWST dataset producing sharply different verdicts depending on who analyzed it and how.

Today's readings trace that arc: the claim itself, at the confidence level its authors actually reported (not the stronger version that circulated in headlines); two independent reanalyses, published within months, that reached a much more cautious conclusion; and a review that steps back from this one planet to explain a broader and older problem — that almost any candidate biosignature gas, including the textbook case of atmospheric oxygen, can in principle be produced by non-biological chemistry, which is exactly why the field has been moving away from hunting for any single "smoking gun" molecule.

## Today's readings (~20 min)

**"New Constraints on DMS and DMDS in the Atmosphere of K2-18 b from JWST MIRI"** (Madhusudhan, Constantinou, Holmberg, Sarkar, Piette & Moses, *The Astrophysical Journal Letters*, 2025) — https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/2041-8213/adc1c8 — ~7 min (abstract, introduction and discussion; the retrieval-table detail is not needed today). A peer-reviewed letter in ApJL, the rapid-communication counterpart to The Astrophysical Journal, published by IOP Publishing on behalf of the American Astronomical Society (AAS journals have been fully open access since January 2022, like `astronomical-journal` cited on Day 2). Using a new JWST MIRI mid-infrared transit spectrum, the authors report that the data are inconsistent with a flat, featureless spectrum at 3.4σ, and interpret the spectral shape as consistent with DMS and/or DMDS — while explicitly noting the two molecules cannot yet be distinguished from each other and that further data are needed. This is the primary claim the rest of today's reading responds to.

**"Insufficient evidence for DMS and DMDS in the atmosphere of K2-18 b"** (Luque, Piaulet-Ghorayeb, Radica, Xue, Zhang, Bean, Samra & Steinrueck, *Astronomy & Astrophysics*, 2025, vol. 700, A284) — https://www.aanda.org/articles/aa/full_html/2025/08/aa55580-25/aa55580-25.html — ~7 min (read the abstract, introduction and conclusions). A peer-reviewed paper in A&A, the same fully open-access European journal cited on Day 3. This independent team performed the first joint analysis of K2-18 b's full panchromatic JWST spectrum (near-infrared NIRISS and NIRSpec together with the mid-infrared MIRI data from the Madhusudhan et al. paper above) and found no statistically significant evidence for DMS or DMDS in the combined dataset. Concretely, they estimate that roughly 25 additional MIRI transit observations — far more than currently exist — would be needed before the data could even reject a flat, featureless spectrum at the conventional 3σ threshold, let alone confirm a specific molecule.

**"Exoplanet Biosignatures: Understanding Oxygen as a Biosignature in the Context of Its Environment"** (Meadows, Reinhard, Arney, Parenteau, Schwieterman, Domagal-Goldman et al., *Astrobiology*, 2018, vol. 18, 630–662) — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1089/ast.2017.1727 — ~6 min (read the abstract, introduction and summary table of false-positive mechanisms). A peer-reviewed review in *Astrobiology* (Mary Ann Liebert, Inc., now part of SAGE Publishing; this article is not confirmed open access and may sit behind the journal's hybrid paywall), commissioned as part of a NASA-organized community effort to formalize biosignature science. It works through the known ways a planet could accumulate large amounts of atmospheric O2 — historically considered astrobiology's strongest single biosignature gas — without any life at all, and argues that O2 (and, by extension, any candidate gas) can only be interpreted safely by reading it in the context of its whole planetary and stellar environment.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with what the original K2-18 b claim actually says, as distinct from how it was often reported. Madhusudhan and colleagues did not claim to have detected life, or even to have unambiguously detected DMS. What they reported is a specific, quantified statistical statement: their new MIRI spectrum departs from a flat line at 3.4σ, and among the candidate molecules that could produce that particular spectral shape, DMS and its close chemical relative DMDS are consistent with the data — while stating plainly that the two cannot yet be told apart, and that firmer conclusions require more observations. This distinction between "the data show structure that is statistically significant" and "that structure has been confirmed to be caused by this specific molecule" is exactly the kind of gap that Day 2's discussion of retrieval degeneracies predicted would matter, and it is where the rest of today's story unfolds.

Two independent teams tested that interpretation using different pipelines and reached the same qualified verdict: not enough evidence, yet. Luque and colleagues' A&A paper is the more quantitatively pointed of the two: by jointly modeling the near-infrared and mid-infrared JWST data together, rather than treating the MIRI spectrum in isolation as the original paper did, they found the case for DMS/DMDS collapses — and, importantly, they did not simply assert "not significant," they quantified how far the data are from being decisive, estimating that on the order of 25 more MIRI transits would be needed to even confidently rule out a flat spectrum at 3σ. A second, independent reanalysis by Stevenson, Lustig-Yaeger, May, Kopparapu, Fauchez, Haqq-Misra, Limbach, Schwieterman, Sotzen and Tsai, published in *The Astronomical Journal* in 2025 under the pointed title "K2-18b Does Not Meet the Standards of Evidence for Life," reached a similar conclusion from a different angle: reprocessing the public JWST data with independent reduction and retrieval methods, they argued the MIRI transit spectrum is highly susceptible to unresolved instrumental red noise, and that what looks like a molecular signal may be, at least in part, an artifact of how the raw detector data were cleaned up — echoing Day 2's warning that the extraction step is never fully neutral. Neither rebuttal claims to have proven DMS is absent; both argue the original statistical case was not yet strong enough to support the scale of the announcement it generated.

This is not simply a story of one team being wrong and another being right — it is a live illustration of how the field actually adjudicates uncertain evidence, which is precisely the methodological drama flagged in this week's opening framing. A single research group made a bold, transparently caveated statistical claim; multiple independent groups, using different pipelines and different combinations of the same public data, stress-tested it within months; and the working consensus that emerged is not "confirmed" or "refuted" but "not yet conclusive, and here is exactly how much more data would settle it." That last part — a specific, falsifiable target (roughly 25 more MIRI transits) rather than a vague call for "more research" — is arguably the healthiest outcome this kind of controversy can produce.

The Meadows et al. review supplies the deeper reason the field is so cautious about single-molecule claims in general, using the older and, on paper, much stronger case of oxygen. Oxygen was long considered biology's most reliable atmospheric fingerprint, because on Earth it is maintained only by continuous photosynthetic replenishment; left alone, atmospheric O2 reacts away within a few million years. But the review catalogs several abiotic pathways that can build up substantial O2 without life: a runaway greenhouse can boil away a planet's ocean, letting water vapor rise into the stratosphere where ultraviolet light splits it apart and the lightweight hydrogen escapes to space, leaving heavier oxygen behind; a planet with too little atmospheric nitrogen or other "non-condensable" background gas can suffer a related water-loss process even without a full runaway greenhouse; and certain carbon dioxide-rich atmospheres can photochemically build up O2 and ozone directly. Each mechanism leaves subtle environmental fingerprints of its own — for instance a desiccated, water-poor world, or particular ratios of co-occurring gases — which is exactly why the review's central argument is that no gas, including O2, can be safely read as a biosignature in isolation. It must be interpreted jointly with the planet's size, its star's type and activity history, and the surrounding atmospheric chemistry.

Put the two threads together and the shape of the field's current thinking becomes clear. K2-18 b shows the caution being exercised in real time, at the level of one contested spectral feature: a claim, quantified, published, and then tested — hard — by independent groups within the same year. The Meadows review shows why that caution is structural rather than a one-off overreaction: even the strongest historical candidate for a "smoking gun" biosignature gas turns out to have abiotic ways of appearing, so a responsible biosignature framework has to be built around convergent, whole-atmosphere context rather than any single molecule. That is also why the community's language has visibly shifted, in both today's readings and the PNAS perspective from Day 1, away from asking "did we detect the biosignature" and toward asking "does the whole weight of evidence — the planet, its star, its full chemical inventory — point toward biology being the best remaining explanation."

## Questions to think about

- The Luque et al. paper turned "not enough evidence" into a specific number: roughly 25 more MIRI transits. What do you think of stating scientific uncertainty this concretely, as a falsifiable target, compared with more familiar hedges like "further research is needed"?
- If a gas as historically trusted as oxygen has several known abiotic false-positive pathways, how should the public — and journalists — calibrate their confidence the next time a "possible biosignature" headline appears?
- Two capable, independent teams reached different conclusions from the same JWST dataset largely because of how they modeled instrumental noise and combined instruments. What does that suggest about where the field should invest next: more telescope time, better statistical methods, or something else?
