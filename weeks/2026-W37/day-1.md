# Exoplanet atmospheres and the search for biosignatures — Day 1/7: How astronomers read a planet's atmosphere from starlight

## Context

Thirty years ago we did not know for certain that any planet existed outside our solar system. Today more than 5,000 are confirmed, and for a meaningful and fast-growing subset of them, astronomers can do something far more ambitious than just detecting a planet: they can measure what its atmosphere is made of. This is done without ever sending a probe — by analysing starlight that has grazed or bounced off the planet on its way to our telescopes. The technique is called transit (or emission) spectroscopy, and it is the foundation for everything this week will build on, including the eventual search for gases in a distant atmosphere that might hint at biological activity.

Two things happened in the last decade to turn this from a niche pursuit into a mainstream field. First, ground- and space-based surveys (Kepler, TESS) found enough transiting planets, including many small, temperate ones, to give spectroscopists real targets. Second, the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), operating since 2022, combined the sensitivity and the wide, uninterrupted infrared wavelength coverage needed to pick out faint molecular fingerprints that were previously buried in noise. The result is that "what is this planet's atmosphere made of" has gone from a question we could barely ask to one we can now answer with real chemical detail for dozens of worlds — and, for a much smaller number, we are starting to ask the far harder question of whether anything in that chemistry looks like it needs life to explain it.

This first day lays the groundwork: what the actual observational technique is, what it can and cannot tell us, and why the field's center of gravity shifted so dramatically once JWST came online. Days 2 through 6 will dig into the mechanics of extracting a spectrum, the concrete findings so far, the controversies over how to interpret them, the statistical and instrumental methods behind the claims, and what a credible detection of life would actually require. Day 7 will pull it together.

A perspective piece by a team of leading exoplanet and astrobiology researchers (lead author Sara Seager, MIT) reflecting on JWST's first years of results and what they mean for the search for biosignature gases. PNAS is one of the most selective general-science journals in the world (peer-reviewed, and PNAS research and perspective articles are generally free to read); this piece is a good entry point because it is explicitly written as an overview for a broad scientific audience, and it is candid about how far the field still has to go before any single gas can serve as a "smoking gun" for life.

**"A benchmark JWST near-infrared spectrum for the exoplanet WASP-39 b"** (Rustamkulov et al., for the JWST Transiting Exoplanet Community Early Release Science Team, *Nature Astronomy*, 2024) — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41550-024-02292-x — ~7 min (read the abstract and results framing; the technical retrieval details are not needed today). This is a peer-reviewed paper in a top astronomy journal, produced by a large, coordinated community collaboration set up specifically to stress-test JWST's atmospheric characterization abilities on a well-studied "benchmark" planet. It is useful today not for WASP-39 b itself (a hot, Saturn-mass gas giant with no prospect of life) but as a concrete illustration of what a real, high-quality atmospheric spectrum looks like and what it can reveal: the team confirmed sodium, potassium, water, carbon dioxide and — notably — sulfur dioxide produced by atmospheric photochemistry, not just by the planet's original composition.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

The core trick behind exoplanet atmosphere science is deceptively simple. Most known exoplanets are found by the "transit method": we watch a star's brightness dip slightly, periodically, as a planet passes in front of it from our vantage point. During that transit, a sliver of starlight does not just graze past the planet — it passes through the thin ring of atmosphere around the planet's day-night terminator before reaching us. Different gases absorb light at very specific wavelengths (their spectral "fingerprints"), so if you compare the star's brightness across many wavelengths during transit versus outside of transit, the wavelengths where the planet's atmosphere blocks a little extra light stand out. Stack up that wavelength-dependent extra dimming and you get a transmission spectrum: a chemical inventory of the atmosphere, extracted purely from starlight. A complementary technique, emission spectroscopy, instead measures light coming from the planet's dayside directly (by watching the total system brightness dip when the planet passes behind the star, a "secondary eclipse") and captures the planet's own thermal glow and its reflected light.

Neither signal is easy to extract. The dimming caused by an atmosphere is minute — often just tens to hundreds of parts per million of the star's brightness — smaller than the star's own variability (starspots, flares) and instrumental noise. This is why the field's progress has tracked telescope capability so closely: the Hubble Space Telescope, working from the visible into the near-infrared, first proved the technique could detect water vapor and even helium escaping from exoplanet atmospheres, but its wavelength range was narrow and its precision limited. JWST changed the picture by covering a much wider, uninterrupted infrared range (roughly 0.6 to 28 micrometers depending on the instrument) with far higher sensitivity, which matters enormously because most of the molecules astronomers care about — water, carbon dioxide, methane, ozone — have their strongest, most distinctive absorption features in the infrared.

The WASP-39 b benchmark study illustrates what this actually buys you. WASP-39 b is a puffy, Saturn-mass planet orbiting close to its star — not remotely habitable, but useful precisely because its atmosphere is thick, hot and easy to observe, making it an ideal test case. Using four different JWST instrument modes together, the Early Release Science team not only confirmed expected gases like water and carbon dioxide, but also detected sulfur dioxide, a molecule that was not simply present in the planet's primordial atmosphere but was actively being manufactured, in real time, by photochemistry — sunlight-driven chemical reactions high in the atmosphere. That is an important conceptual lesson for the rest of this week: an atmosphere is not a static fingerprint you read once, it is an active chemical system, and correctly interpreting any given molecule's presence means understanding the physical and chemical processes that could produce or destroy it — a theme that becomes crucial once we start asking whether a molecule signals biology rather than photochemistry, geology or something else entirely.

That question — could a given gas be evidence of life — is precisely where the field is now cautiously venturing, and the Seager et al. PNAS perspective captures the field's mood at this moment reasonably well. Historically, the search for biosignatures leaned on the idea of "smoking gun" molecules, most famously atmospheric oxygen (O2), reasoning that it is hard to build up and maintain large amounts of a highly reactive gas without a persistent biological source replenishing it, as on Earth. JWST's early years have both sharpened and complicated this hope: sharpened it, because for the first time we have genuinely detected complex molecules in the atmospheres of small, temperate, sub-Neptune-size planets (not just hot giants like WASP-39 b); complicated it, because the community has increasingly documented "false positive" pathways — abiotic chemical or geological processes that can also produce gases once thought to be reliable biosignatures. The PNAS authors argue that no single gas will likely serve as an unambiguous "silver bullet," and that credible claims will instead need to rest on the whole atmospheric context: which gases co-occur, in what relative abundances, on what kind of planet, around what kind of star.

Putting the three readings together: NASA's overview supplies the vocabulary (transit, transmission, emission spectra), the WASP-39 b paper shows what a real, richly detailed JWST spectrum looks like and why chemistry in an atmosphere is dynamic rather than fixed, and the PNAS perspective explains why turning "we detected molecule X" into "we may have detected life" is a much harder, more contested step than the observational breakthrough itself. That gap — between what JWST can now measure and what we are entitled to conclude from it — is exactly the terrain this week is going to explore.

## Questions to think about

- If no single gas can serve as a definitive biosignature, what combination of evidence do you think *should* be enough to justify a serious, public claim of possible life on another planet — and who should get to make that call?
- The WASP-39 b atmosphere is actively reshaped by photochemistry. How might a planet's star (its brightness, its ultraviolet output, its stability) change what chemistry is even possible in that planet's atmosphere, independent of biology?
- Detecting an atmosphere at all requires a favorable transit geometry and a bright enough host star. What kinds of potentially habitable planets might we be systematically unable to characterize this way, no matter how good our telescopes get?

## This week's theme

**Exoplanet atmospheres and the search for biosignatures — how astronomers read distant worlds from spectral lines.**

We have now confirmed more than 5,000 planets orbiting other stars, and for a growing subset of them we can do something that felt like science fiction a decade ago: read the chemical composition of their atmospheres from thousands of light-years away. The James Webb Space Telescope has turned this from a handful of noisy detections into a routine capability, and in the process it has moved the search for extraterrestrial life from philosophy into a concrete, falsifiable observational program. Understanding how this works — and how easily a promising signal can be over-interpreted — is essential to reading the announcements that will keep coming out of this field for the rest of the decade. This week also sits at the intersection of physics, chemistry, geology and biology, making it a good case study in how a genuinely multidisciplinary scientific question gets tackled. It is also a live methodological drama: the same week can bring a headline claim of a possible biosignature and a rebuttal paper questioning the statistics behind it, which makes it an excellent lens on how science actually adjudicates uncertain evidence.
