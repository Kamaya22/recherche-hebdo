# Exoplanet atmospheres and the search for biosignatures — Day 5/7: How we know what we know — statistics, lab spectroscopy and next-generation instruments

## Context

The first four days of this week moved from technique to mechanics to results to controversy. Day 2 introduced two mechanical bottlenecks — stellar contamination and retrieval degeneracy — and Day 4 showed exactly how those bottlenecks played out in public during the K2-18 b dimethyl sulfide (DMS) saga: a 3.4σ statistical claim, contested within months by independent teams reanalyzing the same JWST data. Today steps back from that one planet to ask a more general question: what actually stands behind any of this week's claims, once you get past "we ran a retrieval and got a spectrum"? Three things do — the quality of the laboratory data astronomers compare their spectra against, the statistical and observational tools used to extract a signal in the first place, and the instruments (current and future) that determine what is even observable. All three are usually invisible in a headline, and all three are exactly where the field's real, ongoing work is happening.

There is also a second, quieter methodological layer this week has not yet touched: once a spectrum is in hand and statistically significant, how does the community decide whether it is *evidence of life*, as opposed to evidence of an interesting molecule? That is not a retrieval question, it is an epistemic one, and the field has recently tried to formalize an answer. Today's readings cover a ground-based observational technique this week has not yet discussed (high-resolution cross-correlation spectroscopy, and the laboratory data problem underneath it), a proposed framework for systematically ruling out false positives before calling something a biosignature, and the flagship space telescope now being designed specifically to search for life on Earth-like planets.

## Today's readings (~21 min)

**"Data challenges and prospects of high-resolution spectroscopy of exoplanets"** (Yurchenko, Tennyson & Brogi, *Nature Reviews Physics*, 2025, vol. 7, 645–659) — https://www.nature.com/articles/s42254-025-00839-z — ~9 min (read the introduction, the sections on laboratory data and on ground-based instrumentation; the technical retrieval-algorithm detail is not needed today). A peer-reviewed Technical Review in *Nature Reviews Physics* (Springer Nature; a subscription journal with no gold open-access option for this article type) by two of the field's leading molecular-spectroscopy theorists (Sergei Yurchenko and Jonathan Tennyson, UCL, co-leaders of the ExoMol line-list database) and a leading observational spectroscopist (Matteo Brogi, University of Turin/INAF). It covers high-resolution cross-correlation spectroscopy (HRCCS) — a ground-based technique, complementary to the space-based transit spectroscopy covered so far this week, that cross-correlates an observed spectrum against a full theoretical line list to detect a molecule's collective signature across thousands of lines at once — and is unusually explicit about how the reliability of any HRCCS detection is bottlenecked by the accuracy of the underlying laboratory or computed line lists.

**"Life on the edge: using planetary context to enhance biosignatures and avoid false positives"** (Arthur, Nicholson & Mayne, *Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society*, 2025, vol. 541, issue 4, 3664–3674) — https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/541/4/3664/8213525 — ~7 min (abstract, introduction and discussion; the modeling detail is not needed today). A peer-reviewed paper in *MNRAS*, the flagship journal of the Royal Astronomical Society, published by Oxford University Press, by researchers at the University of Exeter. Rather than proposing a new gas to search for, it proposes a systematic way to use a planet's context — its distance from its star, its size, its age, the properties of its star — to judge how likely a candidate biosignature is to be a false positive, directly building on the kind of abiotic-pathway reasoning Day 4's oxygen review raised for one gas into a general, planet-by-planet framework.

**"Habitable Worlds Observatory"** (NASA Science, Astrophysics Division) — https://science.nasa.gov/astrophysics/programs/habitable-worlds-observatory/ — ~5 min. The official NASA Science overview page for NASA's next flagship astrophysics mission, recommended by the 2020 US Astronomy and Astrophysics Decadal Survey and now in early design, planned as a roughly 6-metre space telescope purpose-built to directly image nearby Earth-like planets and search their atmospheres for biosignature gases using reflected starlight rather than transits. As an official NASA program page it is a recognized institutional source, used here for orientation on the mission's goals and timeline rather than for a specific research finding.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with the laboratory-data problem, because it runs underneath everything else this week. Every method covered so far — transmission spectroscopy (Day 1), atmospheric retrieval (Day 2), the molecule-by-molecule detections of Day 3, and the DMS/DMDS debate of Day 4 — ultimately depends on comparing an observed spectrum to a predicted one, and that prediction is built from a line list: a catalog of exactly which wavelengths a given molecule absorbs at, and how strongly, under given conditions of temperature and pressure. For well-studied molecules like water and carbon dioxide, these catalogs (assembled by projects like ExoMol and HITRAN) are extensive and well validated by decades of laboratory and theoretical work. For less common candidate biosignature gases, they often are not. DMS is the sharpest illustration: no dedicated line list exists for it, so retrievals lean on absorption cross-sections measured in the laboratory more than two decades ago, under a single fixed temperature and pressure that do not match the conditions in K2-18 b's atmosphere. Extrapolating those measurements to different physical conditions introduces exactly the kind of silent uncertainty that a statistical significance number, on its own, cannot capture — a spectrum can be a real, well-measured departure from a flat line and still be compared against an imperfect model of what any given molecule would actually look like there.

Yurchenko, Tennyson and Brogi's review folds that laboratory-data problem into a broader account of high-resolution cross-correlation spectroscopy (HRCCS), a technique this week has not yet covered. Where JWST's transit spectroscopy (Days 1–4) works by resolving an atmosphere's spectrum into tens or hundreds of broad wavelength bins, HRCCS uses ground-based, high-resolution spectrographs — instruments capable of separating thousands of individual, narrow spectral lines — and cross-correlates the full observed spectrum against a theoretical template built from a molecule's complete line list. Because a molecule imprints not one broad feature but a distinctive "comb" of thousands of narrow lines, this method can pull out a real signal even when any single line is far too weak to detect on its own, and it has the added benefit of directly measuring a planet's orbital velocity and, in favorable cases, its rotation and wind patterns — information transit spectroscopy cannot provide. But the review is candid that this power comes at a cost: because the whole technique depends on correlating against a template, the accuracy of that template's line list directly sets the ceiling on what can be detected and how confidently. An incomplete or slightly inaccurate line list does not just weaken a detection, it can actively suppress a real signal or manufacture a spurious one, which is why the review argues that new laboratory and quantum-chemistry work on line lists is now as rate-limiting for the field's progress as new telescope time.

If the first reading shows how uncertain any single molecule's identification can be, the second reading tackles what to do once a candidate signal survives that uncertainty and starts looking real. Arthur, Nicholson and Mayne's MNRAS paper generalizes the lesson Day 4's Meadows et al. review drew for oxygen — that a gas can only be trusted as a biosignature once its known abiotic formation pathways have been ruled out in context — into a systematic planning tool. Their central argument is that a planet's context (how close it orbits its star, its size, its age, its star's activity level and spectral type) changes the relative odds that a given gas came from biology versus geochemistry or photochemistry, and that this context should be used deliberately, before a claim is made, rather than invoked afterward as a rebuttal. They introduce the idea of a "peribiosignature": a candidate biosignature detected in a planetary context where an abiotic origin is comparatively easy to rule out (for instance, at the edges rather than the comfortable middle of a habitable zone, where fewer abiotic processes compete to produce the same gas), arguing that searching in such contexts, even if the planets themselves are less obviously "Earth-like," could produce more convincing evidence than a technically stronger detection made in a context riddled with plausible false positives. This is a genuinely different lever from anything covered earlier this week: it does not ask for a better spectrum or a better retrieval, it asks the community to choose where and how to look based on where a positive result would actually be interpretable.

The final reading is a reminder that none of this happens in a vacuum of telescope time. Every method discussed this week — JWST transit spectroscopy, ground-based HRCCS, and the statistical-context frameworks above — is bounded by what current instruments can actually observe: JWST cannot easily reach the smallest, most temperate rocky planets with enough precision to settle their atmospheres one way or another (as Day 3's TRAPPIST-1 and rocky-planet readings showed), and ground-based HRCCS needs bright, favorably placed targets. The Habitable Worlds Observatory is NASA's answer to that limitation for the specific case of life detection: rather than watching a planet transit its star, it is designed to directly image nearby Earth-like planets in reflected starlight — blocking out the much brighter host star with a coronagraph — and take their spectra directly, a fundamentally different observational geometry from anything covered so far this week. It is still in early design (the 2020 Decadal Survey recommendation, now moving through NASA's formal mission-development stages), which means today's laboratory-data and false-positive-framework problems are not abstract concerns for some future mission; they are exactly the open methodological questions a multi-billion-dollar flagship telescope's science case is currently being built to answer.

Put together, today's three readings widen this week's lens from "is this particular JWST spectrum statistically significant" to "what would it actually take, across laboratory physics, planning strategy and telescope design, for the field to earn confidence in a life-detection claim." That is a slower, less headline-friendly story than Day 4's K2-18 b drama, but it is the story underneath it.

## Questions to think about

- The DMS line-list gap means a real, statistically significant spectral feature can still be compared against an imperfect model of what DMS "should" look like. How should a reader weigh a molecule identification when the underlying laboratory data, not just the telescope data, is acknowledged to be incomplete?
- Arthur, Nicholson and Mayne argue that searching at the "edges" of the habitable zone, where fewer false positives compete, could yield more convincing evidence than a technically stronger detection in a more ambiguous context. Do you find that trade-off (a less classically "habitable" planet, but a cleaner interpretation) convincing?
- The Habitable Worlds Observatory is being designed years before it will fly, based partly on open methodological problems raised this week. What would you want the mission's science team to prioritize now — better laboratory spectroscopy, broader false-positive frameworks, or something else — to make its eventual results easier to trust than K2-18 b's?
