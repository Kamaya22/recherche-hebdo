# Exoplanet atmospheres and the search for biosignatures — Day 6/7: What would count as proof — communicating uncertain claims and planning the next telescopes

## Context

Day 4's K2-18 b story ended on a specific number: roughly 25 more MIRI transits before the data could even reject a flat spectrum at a conventional statistical threshold, let alone confirm a molecule, let alone confirm life. That is a technical answer to a question this week has not yet asked directly: what would actually have to happen — technically, procedurally, and communicatively — before the field, and the public, could treat a "possible biosignature" as something closer to settled? This is not a rhetorical question. It has occupied astrobiologists and space agencies for years, because the alternative to having an answer is what already happened with K2-18 b: a peer-reviewed, carefully hedged statistical claim (3.4σ, two candidate molecules that could not yet be distinguished, explicit calls for more data) turned, in much of the public conversation, into something close to "signs of alien life found."

Today's readings look at three linked responses to that problem. First, a proposal for a structured scale that lets scientists say precisely how much confidence a given piece of evidence deserves, rather than leaving "detection" to do all the rhetorical work. Second, an independent expert review of where that effort stands and what it still gets wrong, particularly around how a life-detection claim should be communicated as it develops. Third, a 2025 workshop report — convened in direct response to the confusion visible in cases like K2-18 b — that surveys what actually goes wrong when astrobiology findings reach the public, from press releases to social media, and what scientists, journalists and institutions could do differently. All three connect back to Day 5's discussion of the Habitable Worlds Observatory: an instrument now being designed specifically to look for life is also, unavoidably, an instrument whose eventual results will need a public-communication plan that does not yet fully exist.

## Today's readings (~21 min)

**"Call for a framework for reporting evidence for life beyond Earth"** (Green, Hoehler, Neveu, Domagal-Goldman, Scalice & Voytek, *Nature*, 2021, vol. 598, 575–579) — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03804-9 — ~7 min. A peer-reviewed comment piece in *Nature* by a team including NASA's then chief scientist (James Green) and senior NASA astrobiology researchers; a freely readable preprint of the same text is posted on arXiv (2107.10975) for anyone without journal access. It proposes the "Confidence of Life Detection" (CoLD) scale: a seven-rung ladder running from an initial, plausible-but-unconfirmed signal up to a claim that has survived repeated, independent attempts to rule out non-biological explanations. It is the founding document for most of what the field now argues about when it argues about how to announce a possible biosignature.

**"Independent Review of the Community Report from the Biosignature Standards of Evidence Workshop"** (Committee on Astrobiology and Planetary Sciences, National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2022) — https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/26621/ — ~7 min (read the summary and Chapter 3, "Communication of a Life Detection Result"; the rest is procedural detail). An institutional report: NASA asked the National Academies' Committee on Astrobiology and Planetary Sciences to independently evaluate a NASA-convened community workshop's proposed standards of evidence for life detection. The Academies' review is a recognized, rigorously vetted institutional source, and it is useful today precisely because it is candid about where the community's own proposed framework (including the CoLD scale) still falls short, especially in how a claim should be presented while evidence is still accumulating rather than only once it is "final."

**"Communicating Discoveries in the Search for Life in the Universe Workshop Report"** (Bimm, Voytek, Scharf, Billings, Suldovsky, Wolfe-Simon, Cable, Yeo, Xenos, Segura, Berea, Anbar, Kaufman, Meadows, Graham, Cartier, Sheikh, Nováková, Ockert & Determann, *Astrobiology*, 2025, vol. 25, 743–758) — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/15311074251387636 — ~7 min (read the introduction and the sections on communicating uncertainty and social media). A peer-reviewed, open-access workshop report in *Astrobiology* — the same journal, now part of SAGE Publishing, that carried Day 4's Meadows et al. review on oxygen false positives. This 2024 workshop (building on NASA's 2021 Standards of Evidence effort behind the CoLD scale) brought together scientists, science journalists and science-communication researchers specifically to study how astrobiology discoveries get distorted between the peer-reviewed paper and the public, and what could be done about it before the next contested claim.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with the CoLD scale itself, because everything else today reacts to it. Green and colleagues' 2021 *Nature* piece argues that the word "detection" is doing far too much work in astrobiology: a single word is being asked to cover everything from "we see something odd in the data" to "we have ruled out every abiotic explanation anyone can think of, repeatedly, using independent methods." Their proposed fix is a seven-level scale, each level requiring more than the last: early levels correspond to a plausible signal that has not yet been checked against known non-biological (abiotic) explanations; middle levels require that known abiotic pathways — the kind Day 4's Meadows et al. review catalogued for oxygen — be actively ruled out, first using the reasoning available at the time of discovery and then again as new abiotic mechanisms are proposed by other scientists; and the highest levels require independent confirmation, ideally through more than one line of evidence, before the finding is treated as settled. The point of the scale is less about the exact number seven than about the discipline it forces: instead of a single, load-bearing "we have detected X," a claim is supposed to be stated at whatever specific rung the evidence actually supports, and revised publicly as it moves up or down that ladder. Read against Day 4, K2-18 b never got announced with an explicit CoLD level attached — which is itself part of what today's other two readings identify as a real, unresolved gap between having a framework and actually using it.

That gap is exactly what the National Academies' independent review interrogates. Reviewing the NASA-convened community report that elaborated the CoLD scale into concrete practice, the Academies' committee found real value in the underlying idea but pushed back on how ready it was for actual use — including, in Chapter 3, a pointed discussion of communication. Their concern was less about the scale's logic than about what happens in the gap between "level 1" and "level 7": a discovery is not a single announcement, it is a multi-year process of papers, conference talks, leaks, follow-up observations, and rebuttals — precisely the arc Day 4 traced for K2-18 b — and the review argued the community had not yet worked out how a finding should be described while it is still moving through that process, as opposed to only once a final level is reached. This is a subtle but important distinction from the scale itself: even a perfectly calibrated seven-level system does not, on its own, tell a scientist what to say to a journalist about a result that currently sits at level 2 and might later move to level 4 or drop back to level 1.

The Bimm et al. 2025 workshop report picks up that exact thread and broadens it past the scale entirely, asking what actually goes wrong once a finding — at whatever confidence level — reaches an audience beyond specialists. Convened specifically because of the kind of confusion visible around K2-18 b and similar announcements, the workshop's participants (scientists, journalists, and communication researchers together) identified recurring failure points: ambiguous or hedged scientific language gets flattened into confident headlines; social media compresses a multi-paragraph caveat into a single dramatic claim shared out of context; and once an exaggerated version of a finding is circulating, a later, more careful correction or rebuttal (like the two independent reanalyses covered on Day 4) rarely reaches the same audience the original claim did. Their recommendations lean toward being proactive rather than reactive: training scientists in science communication before a discovery happens rather than after a misleading headline appears, building durable relationships between researchers and science journalists so nuance survives translation, and treating public trust as something that has to be actively maintained across a whole sequence of claims and corrections, not something that can be repaired after one bad news cycle.

Put together, today's three readings describe a field trying to build institutional infrastructure — a confidence scale, an independent audit of that scale, and a communication strategy for the process the scale describes — around a problem that Day 4 showed in action: rigorous, well-caveated science does not automatically produce a well-calibrated public understanding, and the gap between the two can open up within weeks of a paper's publication. This connects directly to Day 5's closing point about the Habitable Worlds Observatory. A telescope purpose-built to look for life on other Earths is not just a hardware and statistics problem; its science case is being planned, years before launch, alongside — and partly because of — open questions about how any result it eventually produces should be announced. The 2020 US Astronomy and Astrophysics Decadal Survey ("Pathways to Discovery in Astronomy and Astrophysics for the 2020s," National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2021), the same process that formally recommended the Habitable Worlds Observatory as NASA's next flagship astrophysics mission, put the mission's likely cost in the range of roughly $11 billion, with independent NASA-commissioned cost estimates spanning roughly $5 billion to $17 billion depending on design choices — a scale of public investment that raises the stakes on getting the eventual announcement right considerably higher than for any single JWST paper.

Zoom out further and a pattern connects all three of today's institutions to the rest of the week. Day 2 showed that a "detection" is really a probability distribution shaped by retrieval choices. Day 4 showed that a specific, quantified claim (3.4σ, two indistinguishable candidate molecules) can still generate a public narrative far more confident than the data supported. Today's readings show the field is aware of exactly that gap and is actively, if imperfectly, building the vocabulary (CoLD levels), the institutional oversight (the Academies' review), and the communication practice (the 2025 workshop's recommendations) to close it — precisely because the next several years are expected to bring more, not fewer, contested claims, on more capable and more expensive instruments than any built so far.

## Questions to think about

- The CoLD scale asks scientists to attach a specific confidence level to a claim rather than a single word like "detected." Would a headline that said "K2-18 b biosignature claim: CoLD level 2 of 7" have changed how the story was received — or would the number itself just have become the new thing to argue about?
- The National Academies review argues the community has not solved how to communicate a finding while it is still moving between confidence levels, not just once it settles. What would responsible reporting on an in-progress, not-yet-resolved claim actually look like, in practice, for a journalist or a research team?
- With Habitable Worlds Observatory cost estimates running into the tens of billions of dollars, how much should public communication strategy — as opposed to pure science return — factor into how space agencies justify and design a life-detection mission of that scale?
