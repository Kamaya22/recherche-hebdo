# Exoplanet atmospheres and the search for biosignatures — Day 7/7: Synthesis — what is settled, what remains open, and where to go next

## Context

This week moved from technique to mechanism to result to controversy to method to communication, and each day added a qualification the previous day had left out. Monday established the basic trick — a transiting planet's atmosphere leaves a wavelength-dependent fingerprint on starlight, and JWST's infrared reach turned that from a novelty into a routine measurement for large, hot planets. Tuesday complicated that picture by showing the fingerprint has to survive two demanding, non-neutral steps first: stripping out the host star's own contaminating signal, and inverting the cleaned spectrum through a statistical retrieval that returns a range of plausible atmospheres, not a single answer. Wednesday cashed that framework out against the actual observational record: secure, boring detections on hot giants; a contested, assumption-dependent multi-molecule case on the sub-Neptune TOI-270 d; and a clean, informative non-detection on the two best-placed rocky TRAPPIST-1 planets.

Thursday named the planet where all of that machinery collided with the public imagination — K2-18 b, where a Cambridge-led team's 3.4σ statistical case for dimethyl sulfide (DMS) and/or dimethyl disulfide (DMDS) was, within months, met by two independent reanalyses concluding the evidence was not yet strong enough, echoing a much older lesson (Meadows et al.'s review of abiotic oxygen) that no single gas is a safe "smoking gun." Friday went underneath that dispute to the two resources that make any of it possible or fragile in the first place — laboratory line lists and planetary-context reasoning — and to the next-generation instruments (ground-based high-resolution spectroscopy, the Habitable Worlds Observatory) that will need both to work well. Saturday then asked the meta-question the whole week had been circling: once a claim like K2-18 b's exists, what should the field, and the public, actually be told about how confident to be — the CoLD confidence scale, the National Academies' critique of it, and a 2025 workshop's recommendations for better science communication.

Today does not add a new mechanism or a new dispute. It asks what the K2-18 b story looks like now, a year and a half after Thursday's reading left it "not yet conclusive," and uses that update to take stock of the whole week. The two readings below were chosen because, unusually for a synthesis day, they are not surveys of past work — they are two 2026 papers that continue Thursday's exact controversy in real time, one doubling down on the biosignature case with a broader and more systematic search, the other arriving at an alternative explanation through an independent, more comprehensive reanalysis. Together they are the clearest possible illustration of what this whole week has been arguing: that a "detection" in this field is not an event, it is an ongoing, adversarial, self-correcting process — and K2-18 b is still, as of today, in the middle of it.

## Today's readings (~18 min)

**"A Systematic Search for Trace Molecules in the Atmosphere of Exoplanet K2-18 b"** (Pica-Ciamarra, Madhusudhan, Cooke, Constantinou & Binet, *The Astrophysical Journal Letters*, 2026, vol. 1002, no. 2) — https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/2041-8213/ae5dcc — ~9 min (abstract, introduction and discussion; the per-molecule Bayes-factor tables are not needed today). A peer-reviewed letter in ApJL, published by IOP Publishing on behalf of the American Astronomical Society (AAS journals have been fully open access since January 2022, as already noted on Days 2 and 4). The authors — the same Cambridge team behind Thursday's original DMS/DMDS claim — respond to the "which molecule, really" problem by testing 661 candidate molecules, spanning biotic, abiotic and anthropogenic gases, against K2-18 b's combined JWST spectrum. They report that DMS is the only molecule that consistently reaches a moderate statistical preference (Bayes factor ≥ 2) across the datasets tested independently, and that among the handful of other candidates that occasionally fit as well, DMS is the only one with a theorized production pathway that could plausibly generate the retrieved abundance — while stating clearly that near-infrared support for the result depends on how instrumental detector offsets are handled, and that the case still falls well short of a confirmed detection.

**"The atmospheric composition of the sub-Neptune K2-18 b and insights into its formation"** (Fernández-Rodríguez, Morello, Tan, Pallé, Swain, Poultourtzidis, Biagini, Changeat, Jiang, Pozuelos & Amado, *Astronomy & Astrophysics*, 2026, vol. 706) — https://www.aanda.org/articles/aa/full_html/2026/06/aa57718-25/aa57718-25.html — ~9 min (abstract, introduction and conclusions; the twelve-spectrum reduction comparison is not needed today). A peer-reviewed paper in A&A, the same fully open-access European journal cited on Days 3 and 4 (open access since 2024), by an independent team led from the Instituto de Astrofísica de Canarias. Reprocessing the public JWST data through twelve independently reduced versions of the transmission spectrum — varying the spectral binning, the limb-darkening treatment, and a new correction for an occulted stellar spot — they find methane robustly detected across every version, carbon dioxide present but far more model-dependent, and the earlier DMS hint disappearing entirely in their most complete retrievals. They instead favor a hydrogen-rich atmosphere with an unusually high carbon-to-oxygen ratio, which they argue is more consistent with the planet having formed in place, close to its star ("Inside-Out Planet Formation"), than with any biological explanation.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with what today's two papers actually do to the K2-18 b story, because the honest answer is: not resolve it. Pica-Ciamarra et al. do not simply repeat Thursday's claim — they stress-test it against 661 alternatives, which is precisely the kind of systematic, agnostic check Friday's and Thursday's readings implied was missing, and DMS survives that test as the single most consistent candidate. Fernández-Rodríguez et al., working independently and processing the same underlying JWST data through more reduction pipelines than any previous K2-18 b analysis, get methane and a formation story out of it, and no statistically robust DMS. Both papers are careful, peer-reviewed, and more thorough than anything Thursday's reading covered — and they still do not agree. That is not a failure of either paper; it is Tuesday's retrieval-degeneracy problem and Thursday's instrumental-noise problem, still both live, eighteen months and several new analyses after the original claim. If this week has one central, hard-won lesson, it is this one: for a planet this faint and this scientifically contested, "more data" and "more careful analysis" do not automatically converge on a single answer on any predictable timeline — they can just as easily produce two more sophisticated, still-disagreeing answers.

**What is genuinely settled**, across all seven days. On big, hot, gas-rich planets, JWST transit and emission spectroscopy works exceptionally well — Monday's WASP-39 b case and the broader catalog behind it are secure, and nobody in this week's readings disputes them. The mechanics of why the harder cases are hard are also settled: Tuesday's stellar contamination and retrieval degeneracy, and Friday's laboratory-line-list gap, are not contested findings, they are well-documented, load-bearing limitations that every other day's harder claims sit on top of. And the field's own epistemic norms have shifted, not just in rhetoric: Thursday's Meadows et al. review, Friday's peribiosignature framework, and Saturday's CoLD scale all point the same direction, away from any single "smoking gun" gas and toward whole-atmosphere, whole-system context — a shift today's readings exemplify rather than merely discuss, since Pica-Ciamarra et al.'s own contribution is precisely to check DMS against thousands of alternatives rather than assert it in isolation.

**What is still open, and where exactly.** Three seams run through the whole week. First, the causal-attribution seam that today's readings sit directly on top of: is DMS/DMDS really present on K2-18 b, and if so, is it biological, given Thursday's and Friday's catalog of non-biological ways sulfur photochemistry can behave on a planet whose bulk chemistry (today's elevated C/O ratio finding) may not resemble Earth's at all? Second, the instrumental-and-statistical seam: Wednesday's TOI-270 d case and today's two K2-18 b papers both show that which pipeline, which noise model, and which retrieval assumptions a team uses can change the headline finding, even among researchers acting in good faith with public data — a problem Friday's laboratory-data and instrumentation readings frame as a bottleneck the field is actively working, not a solved one. Third, the communication seam Saturday raised and today's readings make concrete: neither of today's two papers has been announced with an explicit CoLD confidence level, which means the same ambiguity-to-headline pipeline that produced Thursday's over-claimed coverage in 2025 is fully available to do the same thing again with either of today's results.

**Three to five big open questions** worth carrying forward from this week. (1) Will K2-18 b's DMS/DMDS question actually get resolved by more data, as Thursday's "roughly 25 more MIRI transits" estimate implied, or is the disagreement between today's two papers evidence that the bottleneck has shifted from data quantity to irreducible modeling choices — in which case, what would resolution even look like? (2) Given that Friday's laboratory line-list gap and today's competing retrievals both trace back partly to incomplete spectroscopic reference data, how much of the field's near-term progress depends on unglamorous laboratory physics rather than more telescope time? (3) If a planet's bulk formation history (today's Inside-Out Planet Formation argument) can produce an atmospheric chemistry that mimics or confounds a biosignature search, how should the field weight formation-history evidence alongside the planetary-context and false-positive frameworks Friday introduced? (4) With the Habitable Worlds Observatory's science case still being designed around exactly these open methodological questions, as Friday and Saturday both noted, should its planning prioritize a fundamentally different observational geometry (direct imaging in reflected light) precisely because transit spectroscopy's degeneracies, illustrated all week, may not resolve on rocky, temperate planets no matter how much JWST time is spent? (5) Saturday's readings argued the field lacks a template for communicating an in-progress, moving claim — with K2-18 b now the subject of two more competing 2026 papers, what would a responsible headline about either of today's results actually look like, given everything this week has shown about how such claims tend to travel?

**Where to go deeper**, one pointer per stage of this week's arc. For the observational technique itself, the NASA overview page from Monday remains the clearest plain-language starting point. For the two mechanical bottlenecks, Barstow and Welbanks' 2026 retrieval review (Tuesday) is the best entry into why "detected" is a probability statement, not a fact. For the observational record, Kreidberg and Stevenson's PNAS perspective (Wednesday) is the most honest single status report on rocky-planet characterization. For the false-positive problem in general, Meadows et al.'s oxygen review (Thursday) and Arthur, Nicholson and Mayne's planetary-context framework (Friday) are the two most useful frameworks, read together. For instrumentation and what comes next, the Habitable Worlds Observatory's own NASA program page (Friday) is the right jumping-off point. For how to think about an unresolved claim responsibly, Green et al.'s CoLD-scale paper and the National Academies' review of it (Saturday) are the field's own attempt at an answer. And for K2-18 b specifically, now that the story has moved past Thursday's snapshot, today's two 2026 papers are the most current statement of where the argument actually stands.

## Questions to think about

- Today's two papers used more rigorous methods than anything in Thursday's reading and still reached opposite conclusions about DMS. If that pattern continues — more careful science producing more sophisticated disagreement rather than convergence — what would eventually count as the field "settling" the K2-18 b question?
- The Fernández-Rodríguez et al. paper explains K2-18 b's chemistry through its formation history rather than its current biology or geology. How should a planetary-formation argument be weighed against the biological and abiotic-chemistry explanations this week spent most of its time on — is it a different category of evidence, or does it compete on the same footing?
- Having read all seven days, would you now describe exoplanet biosignature science as a field with a stable observational core (large, hot planets) and a genuinely unresolved frontier (small, temperate ones) — or does today's reading suggest even "the K2-18 b question" is less a single open question than several entangled ones (chemistry, instrumentation, formation history, communication) that happen to share one planet?
