# Exoplanet atmospheres and the search for biosignatures — Day 3/7: What JWST has actually found so far

## Context

The first two days of this week built the toolkit: how a transit dims starlight into a spectrum (Day 1), and how that raw spectrum has to be cleaned of stellar contamination and then inverted through a statistical retrieval before it becomes a claimed molecular inventory (Day 2). Today turns to the actual inventory JWST has produced — not a single dramatic result, but a two-track record that is easy to conflate if you only read headlines. On one track, JWST has been spectacularly successful at reading the atmospheres of large, hot, gas-rich planets: precise, uncontroversial detections of water, carbon dioxide and photochemical byproducts like sulfur dioxide (the WASP-39 b case from Day 1) are now almost routine. On the other track — smaller, cooler, more Earth- or Neptune-sized planets, the ones that actually matter for the biosignature question — the picture is far more mixed: some genuinely exciting multi-molecule detections on temperate sub-Neptunes, and, on the smallest rocky worlds, mostly rigorous non-detections.

Today's three readings map that second track directly. Two are 2026 peer-reviewed papers reporting concrete, opposite-looking outcomes for two different classes of small planet: a temperate sub-Neptune where carbon dioxide, water and a sulfur-bearing gas have now been measured with real statistical confidence, and two rocky, Earth-sized planets where JWST instead found nothing but bare, airless rock. The third is a 2025 PNAS perspective that steps back and honestly assesses where rocky-planet characterization stands after several years of JWST data. Together they set up something important for Day 4: one of today's papers involves an author (Nikku Madhusudhan) and a planet class (temperate sub-Neptunes, the so-called "Hycean world" hypothesis) that will resurface directly tomorrow in the most contested biosignature claim of the JWST era, K2-18 b.

## Today's readings (~22 min)

**"A first look at rocky exoplanets with JWST"** (Kreidberg & Stevenson, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 2025) — https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2416190122 — ~8 min. A peer-reviewed perspective by two leading JWST exoplanet observers (Laura Kreidberg, Max Planck Institute for Astronomy; Kevin Stevenson, Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory) in PNAS, which has been fully open access since 2021. It is the honest status report this week needs: JWST has delivered the most precise rocky-planet transmission spectra to date and the first thermal-emission detections for worlds cooler than 800 K, yet as of the paper's writing no atmosphere had been unambiguously confirmed on a small rocky exoplanet — the data are only beginning to constrain which volatiles could be present.

**"The atmospheric composition of TOI-270 d"** (Constantinou, Madhusudhan & Holmberg, *Astronomy & Astrophysics*, 2026) — https://www.aanda.org/articles/aa/full_html/2026/01/aa52192-24/aa52192-24.html — ~7 min (read the abstract, introduction and conclusions; the retrieval tables are not needed today). A&A is a peer-reviewed European astronomy journal, fully open access, published by EDP Sciences. The authors (University of Cambridge and Space Telescope Science Institute) reanalyze the full JWST dataset for TOI-270 d, a temperate sub-Neptune, and report confident detections of CO2 and CS2 and a lower-confidence hint of H2O, arguing the atmosphere is more hydrogen-rich (and hence more consistent with a "Hycean" ocean-world scenario) than an earlier 2024 analysis of the same planet had concluded.

**"No thick atmosphere around TRAPPIST-1 b and c from JWST thermal phase curves"** (Gillon, Ducrot, Bell et al., *Nature Astronomy*, 2026) — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41550-026-02806-9 — ~7 min (read the abstract and discussion; the phase-curve modeling detail is not needed today). A peer-reviewed paper in Nature Astronomy (Nature Portfolio, Springer Nature) from the large international TRAPPIST-1 JWST collaboration. It reports full-orbit thermal phase curves of the two innermost, Earth-sized TRAPPIST-1 planets and finds both consistent with bare, airless rock rather than any thick atmosphere — a clean, well-instrumented non-detection that is just as informative as a detection.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with what is genuinely settled, because it is easy to lose sight of it amid the more contested cases: on large, hot, gas-rich planets, JWST atmosphere science works extremely well. The WASP-39 b spectrum from Day 1 — water, carbon dioxide, sodium, potassium, and photochemically produced sulfur dioxide, all measured with high statistical confidence — is not an isolated case; it is representative of a now-large catalog of hot Jupiters and hot Neptunes with securely characterized atmospheres. These planets are easy targets precisely because they are big (a large, puffy atmosphere blocks more starlight) and hot (strong, well-separated molecular features), and none of them are remotely habitable. The interesting scientific and public tension is entirely about smaller, cooler planets, where the signal is faint and every one of Day 2's mechanical complications — stellar contamination, retrieval degeneracies — bites hardest.

The Kreidberg and Stevenson PNAS perspective lays out where that harder frontier stood as of 2025: real technical milestones (the most precise transmission spectra ever taken of rocky planets, the first successful measurement of thermal emission from planets below 800 K), but, at the time of writing, no confirmed atmosphere on any rocky exoplanet. The paper frames the data as "beginning to constrain" which combinations of carbon-, nitrogen- and oxygen-bearing molecules could plausibly be present — a much weaker claim than a detection, and a useful yardstick for judging any splashier headline against.

The TRAPPIST-1 b and c paper is a concrete instance of exactly that constraining power working in the negative direction. Using full-orbit thermal phase curves — tracking a planet's brightness continuously as it circles its star, rather than a single transit or eclipse snapshot — the team measured TRAPPIST-1 b's dayside at a scorching 490 ± 17 K with essentially no nightside glow and no offset between the hottest point and local noon: the signature of a dark surface with no atmosphere to redistribute heat around the planet, exactly like an airless rock or the Moon. TRAPPIST-1 c came back cooler (dayside 369 ± 23 K) with a similarly cold nightside, consistent with either a bare, moderately reflective surface or, at most, a thin, oxygen-rich atmosphere too tenuous to move heat efficiently. In both cases, models requiring a surface pressure above roughly one bar — Earth's own atmospheric pressure — are strongly disfavored. This matters directly for the biosignature question, because TRAPPIST-1's seven planets are the best-studied system of small, temperate worlds around a star cool enough (an M dwarf) to make transits frequent and observable at all; if even the two most favorably placed of them turn out airless, it narrows, real-time, which planets are worth the very expensive telescope time a biosignature search requires.

TOI-270 d tells the opposite story, and it is worth reading carefully because of what it reveals about Day 2's retrieval-degeneracy problem in action. This sub-Neptune, roughly twice Earth's radius, had already been observed with JWST once before, in a widely cited 2024 study (Benneke et al.) that circulated as a preprint; that first analysis inferred a hot, "mixed-envelope" atmosphere with a high mean molecular weight — essentially, a small gas giant with a dense, metal-rich atmosphere, not obviously interesting for the search for life. Constantinou, Madhusudhan and Holmberg's 2026 reanalysis, using the same underlying JWST data but different retrieval assumptions (notably about the planet's temperature structure), instead favors a lower mean-molecular-weight, hydrogen-rich atmosphere, with CO2 detected at high confidence (6.4σ), a sulfur-bearing gas (CS2) at moderate confidence, and a weaker hint of water. That combination is consistent with the "Hycean" hypothesis this same research group has championed elsewhere: a class of sub-Neptune with a thick hydrogen atmosphere sitting above a deep, possibly liquid, planet-wide water ocean — a genuinely novel kind of potentially life-relevant environment, distinct from anything in our own solar system. The key lesson is not "TOI-270 d is now confirmed to be a water world" — it is that two competent, peer-reviewed teams reached materially different pictures of the same planet's atmosphere from the same instrument's data, purely because of different modeling choices. That is precisely the degeneracy problem Day 2 previewed, now showing up in a live, published disagreement rather than as an abstract methodological caveat.

Zooming out, three findings sit side by side today: routine, secure detections on hot giant planets; a real but contested and choice-dependent multi-molecule detection on a temperate sub-Neptune, made by a team that will reappear tomorrow with an even more contested claim; and a clean, confident non-detection on the two best-placed rocky, Earth-sized planets currently available for study. None of this is a story of steady, linear progress toward a life detection. It is a story of a field whose observational reach has grown enormously while its interpretive tools remain the bottleneck — exactly the tension that explodes into open controversy in Day 4's look at K2-18 b.

## Questions to think about

- The TOI-270 d case shows two peer-reviewed teams reaching different conclusions from the same JWST data through different modeling choices. How should a non-specialist weigh a "detection" that depends on which retrieval assumptions were used?
- If TRAPPIST-1 b and c both turn out to be airless, what does that imply about the odds for the system's more distant, potentially more temperate planets (d through h), which are harder to observe but were originally considered the system's best habitability prospects?
- Kreidberg and Stevenson describe JWST rocky-planet data as only "beginning to constrain" atmospheric composition. What would you want to see — in terms of statistical confidence, independent confirmation, or number of planets — before treating a rocky-exoplanet atmosphere claim as settled?
