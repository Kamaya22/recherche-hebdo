# The gut virome — Day 1/7: What is the gut virome, and why has it been overlooked?

## Context

Say "gut microbiome" and almost everyone pictures bacteria — Bacteroides, Firmicutes, the trillions of microbial cells that help digest food, train the immune system, and influence everything from mood to metabolism. What gets left out of that picture is that every one of those bacterial cells is itself a habitat: the gut also contains a vast population of viruses, collectively called the gut virome, and the overwhelming majority of them are bacteriophages ("phages") — viruses that infect bacteria, not human cells. By some estimates, phages outnumber bacteria in the gut and make up roughly 97-98% of all viral particles found there, with eukaryotic viruses (which infect human or dietary cells) a small remainder.

The virome was overlooked for decades for a simple technical reason: viruses have no universal marker gene equivalent to the 16S rRNA gene that let researchers catalogue bacteria relatively easily from the 1990s onward. Virome research only became feasible once shotgun metagenomic sequencing — reading DNA fragments directly from a sample without needing to culture or target a known gene — became cheap enough to apply at scale, from roughly the mid-2000s. Even then, a new problem appeared: most phage genomes have no close relatives in reference databases, so a large share of virome sequencing reads simply cannot be matched to anything known. This "viral dark matter" is still the central practical obstacle in the field today, and it is the reason the gut virome remains the least-characterized major layer of the gut ecosystem, even as interest in it accelerates.

This week traces the gut virome from the ground up. Today sets the scene: what the virome is made of, how big a role phages play, and why so much of it is still unidentified. Later days will look at how phages actually interact with bacteria (Day 2), how the virome is established in infancy and changes with age (Day 3), the clinical frontier of phage therapy (Day 4), the unresolved question of whether virome changes cause or merely accompany diseases like inflammatory bowel disease (Day 5), the methods that make this research possible (Day 6), and a synthesis (Day 7).

## Today's readings (~20 min)

**The human virome: assembly, composition and host interactions** (Liang & Bushman, *Nature Reviews Microbiology*, 2021) — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41579-021-00536-5 (freely readable via PubMed Central: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8008777/) — ~10 min (introduction and gut-focused sections). A comprehensive, invited peer-reviewed review by two leading virome researchers (University of Pennsylvania), covering how the human virome — dominated by the gut — is assembled, what it's composed of, and how it interacts with its host across body sites and life stages. This is the standard current reference for the field's overall shape.

**Gut Virome: Role and Distribution in Health and Gastrointestinal Diseases** (Spencer, Olawuni & Singh, *Frontiers in Cellular and Infection Microbiology*, 2022) — https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fcimb.2022.836706/full — ~6 min. An open-access, peer-reviewed mini-review focused specifically on the gut (rather than the whole-body virome), summarizing phage dominance, how the virome shifts across life, and its associations with gastrointestinal disease — a good complement to the broader Liang & Bushman review.

**A highly abundant bacteriophage discovered in the unknown sequences of human faecal metagenomes** (Dutilh et al., *Nature Communications*, 2014) — https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms5498 — ~5 min (abstract and introduction). The original, peer-reviewed discovery paper for crAssphage, now understood to be one of the most common viruses in the human gut. Open-access, and a concrete illustration of how "dark matter" sequences can be turned into a defined, trackable organism — it is cited throughout this week as a recurring example.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

The gut virome is best understood as a population of viruses that mostly prey on the gut's resident bacteria rather than on us. Estimates vary with the study and the method, but most reviews, including Liang & Bushman's, converge on phages making up the large majority of viral particles recovered from human stool, vastly outnumbering the eukaryotic viruses (the kind that infect human cells, plant/dietary viruses passing through, and viruses of gut protozoa) that make up the rest. In absolute terms, phage populations in the gut are estimated in the range of 10^8-10^9 particles per gram of stool — on a similar order of magnitude to the bacteria they infect, if usually somewhat more numerous.

Why did this enormous population go largely unstudied for so long? Bacterial microbiome research took off in the 2000s on the back of 16S rRNA gene sequencing: because nearly all bacteria carry a recognizable version of this one gene, researchers could sequence just that gene and use it to identify and count bacterial taxa cheaply. Viruses have no equivalent universal marker — phage genomes are extremely diverse, and unlike bacteria, there is no single gene shared broadly enough across phage families to serve the same purpose. Studying the virome therefore required shotgun metagenomics: sequencing essentially all the DNA in a sample and reconstructing viral genomes afterward, a far more data- and computation-intensive approach that only became routine once sequencing costs fell steeply, well after bacterial microbiome studies had already matured.

Even with modern shotgun sequencing, virome research runs into a problem that mostly doesn't affect bacterial studies: a large share of the DNA sequences recovered from stool samples cannot be matched to anything in existing reference databases. This is sometimes called viral "dark matter." It exists because phage genomes evolve quickly, exchange genetic material promiscuously, and because the reference databases that let scientists identify a sequence (by comparing it to previously catalogued genomes) are built overwhelmingly from bacteria and human-infecting viruses that have been deliberately studied — phages that infect obscure or hard-to-culture gut bacteria have simply never been sequenced before, so there is nothing to match them against. Reviews differ on exactly how much of a typical gut virome sample remains unidentified this way, but the consensus across the field is that it is a substantial fraction, which is precisely why so much virome methodology (which we'll look at on Day 6) is built around reference-independent approaches that can characterize a virus without needing to already know what it is.

The 2014 discovery of crAssphage is a useful illustration of how this "dark matter" problem gets solved in practice, and why it matters beyond being a technical curiosity. Dutilh and colleagues were not looking for a specific virus; they were developing a method called cross-assembly, which compares unidentified sequence fragments across many different people's stool metagenomes to find ones that consistently cluster together — a purely statistical, reference-free signal that a given sequence belongs to one real, recurring organism, even without knowing what that organism is. That method turned up a roughly 97,000-base-pair viral genome that was, at the time, unrelated to anything previously described, yet present in a large share of published human stool metagenomes worldwide and, in the most abundant samples, comprising a striking share of all viral sequence reads. Because they found it through cross-assembly rather than by looking for a known virus, they named it crAssphage. Follow-up computational work predicted it infects bacteria in the Bacteroides group, and this was later confirmed directly when a related phage was isolated in culture and shown to infect the gut bacterium Bacteroides intestinalis. CrAssphage is now recognized as a prototype for an entire family of related "crAss-like" phages, and remains one of the most common single genetic sequences found in human stool worldwide — a striking fact given that it was completely unknown before 2014.

Taken together, these two threads — phage numerical dominance, and the large fraction of the virome that resists identification — explain both why the field lagged behind bacterial microbiome research and why it has become a priority since. A population this large, this close to the gut's bacterial ecosystem, and this poorly catalogued is a plausible source of real biological influence that bacterial-only studies would simply miss. The rest of this week asks what, specifically, that influence looks like: how phages actually shape bacterial communities (Day 2), how the virome is seeded in infancy (Day 3), and whether any of this can be turned into therapy (Day 4) or helps explain disease (Day 5).

## Questions to think about

1. Reference-free methods like cross-assembly let researchers detect a real, recurring organism without knowing what it is. What does this suggest about how much of biology more broadly might be "hiding" in plain sight simply because existing reference databases were built around what was easy to study first?
2. CrAssphage is both extremely common and, until 2014, completely unknown. How should the discovery of a single dominant, previously invisible organism change how confident we are in conclusions from microbiome studies that predate it?
3. If phages vastly outnumber bacteria in the gut and interact with them constantly, should "the gut microbiome" be understood as a bacteria-virus system by default, rather than treating viruses as an add-on to a primarily bacterial story?

## This week's theme

**The gut virome — bacteriophages as an overlooked layer of the human microbiome and their role in health and disease.**

Discussions of the "microbiome" almost always mean bacteria, but every gram of human stool also carries billions of viruses — the gut virome — and the overwhelming majority of them are bacteriophages, viruses that infect bacteria rather than human cells. These phages do not merely coexist with the bacterial microbiome: they shape which bacterial strains thrive, shuttle genes (including antibiotic-resistance and virulence genes) between bacteria, and are increasingly implicated in conditions ranging from inflammatory bowel disease to malnutrition. Yet the gut virome remains the least-characterized layer of the gut ecosystem — a large fraction of viral sequence data from stool samples still cannot be matched to any known organism, so-called viral "dark matter."

The topic matters for at least three reasons. First, phages are a plausible lever for manipulating the microbiome with surgical precision — unlike antibiotics or probiotics, a phage can target a single bacterial strain without collateral damage, which is driving renewed interest in phage therapy against antibiotic-resistant infections. Second, the virome develops in a structured way from birth through adulthood and appears to influence immune training in infancy, with implications for conditions like allergies and malnutrition. Third, because so much of the virome is still unidentified, the field is a useful case study in how metagenomics pushes the boundary of what counts as "known" biology.
