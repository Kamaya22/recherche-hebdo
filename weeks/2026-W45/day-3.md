# The gut virome — Day 3/7: Building a virome — from an infant's first week to old age

## Context

Day 1 established that the gut virome is a vast, phage-dominated population, much of it still unidentified "dark matter." Day 2 zoomed into mechanism: phages either kill their bacterial host outright (lytic cycle) or integrate quietly into its genome (lysogenic cycle), and these two outcomes were hypothesized to dominate at different points in life — "kill-the-winner," high-turnover predation in a chaotic infant gut, versus "piggyback-the-winner," lysogeny-favoring stability in a dense, settled adult gut. Today picks that hypothesis up directly: how does the virome actually get built, from a newborn's first exposure to phages through to old age, and is there such a thing as a "healthy," core virome that most people share?

This question matters for two reasons beyond curiosity. First, if the virome follows a predictable developmental trajectory — like the bacterial microbiome's well-documented maturation from infancy to an adult-like state — then disruptions to that trajectory (by birth mode, feeding, antibiotics, or illness) could have consequences that only show up as different conditions at different ages, which is exactly what later days on IBD (Day 5) and phage therapy (Day 4) will need to account for. Second, if specific phages — crAssphage above all — are so consistently present across most humans that they qualify as "core" members of a healthy virome, their presence or absence becomes a plausible biomarker, the virome equivalent of a vital sign.

Today's three readings move through infancy (when and how the virome is seeded), the statistical case for a shared "core" virome in healthy adults, and what is known about how the virome changes again in old age — setting up Day 4's look at turning phages into therapy and Day 5's harder question of whether virome shifts cause disease or merely accompany it.

## Today's readings (~22 min)

**Phages in the infant gut: a framework for virome development during early life** (Shamash & Maurice, *The ISME Journal*, 2021) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8776839/ — ~8 min. A peer-reviewed Perspective article (McGill University) in the official journal of the International Society for Microbial Ecology, synthesizing what is known about how phage communities establish themselves in the first years of life. Freely readable via PubMed Central; the journal itself was Springer Nature-published at the time (it has since moved to Oxford University Press and become fully open access).

**Healthy human gut phageome** (Manrique et al., *PNAS*, 2016) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5027468/ — ~7 min. The original peer-reviewed, open-access network-analysis study (San Diego State University and collaborators) that first proposed splitting the adult gut virome into "core" (shared by most people), "common," and "rare/person-specific" tiers — and found that crAssphage genomes were recovered among the core group. A foundational paper for the idea of a definable "healthy" virome.

**The gut and circulating virome: emerging players in aging and longevity** (review, *Frontiers in Aging*, 2026) — https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/aging/articles/10.3389/fragi.2025.1731621/full — ~7 min. A 2026 peer-reviewed, fully open-access review (Frontiers Media) synthesizing recent findings on how the virome's composition and activity change in later life, including a striking pattern in centenarians, and connecting these changes to immune aging and inflammation.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

The infant gut virome does not exist at birth. The current consensus, as Shamash and Maurice lay out, is that a newborn's gut starts essentially virus-free and is colonized by phages within the first week of life — presumably picked up from the mother, the birth environment, and early feeding. What follows is a fast, structured succession: early infancy is dominated by Caudovirales (the classic tailed phages that also dominate the adult gut, as Day 1 and 2 discussed), but by around two years of age, Microviridae — a family of small, single-stranded-DNA phages — become the most prevalent group, and by roughly two to three years both the phage and bacterial fractions of the gut approach an adult-like composition. This timeline tracks closely with the bacterial microbiome's own well-known maturation curve, which makes sense once you remember from Day 2 that phages depend entirely on which bacteria are available to infect.

CrAssphage is a useful illustration of that dependency. Rather than being present from birth, crAss-like phages typically do not appear until one to three months of age, and Shamash and Maurice argue this delay is best explained by the later arrival of their bacterial hosts: crAss-like phages infect Bacteroidetes (chiefly Bacteroidaceae and Prevotellaceae), and those bacterial families themselves tend to colonize the infant gut somewhat later than early pioneers like Enterobacteriaceae. In other words, a phage's developmental timeline on Day 3 is downstream of its host bacterium's timeline — which is one reason the virome is sometimes described as "riding along" with bacterial succession rather than developing independently. Beyond timing, the evidence on exactly how infants acquire their phages is genuinely mixed: some studies report that crAssphage is acquired early and consistently across different cohorts regardless of birth mode (vaginal vs. caesarean), while a larger mother-infant cohort study found that only some infants acquired detectable crAssphage at all, and reviews note that breast milk itself carries a meaningful share of the phages found in infants — so direct maternal transmission, environmental acquisition, and milk-borne transfer are all plausible, overlapping routes rather than one clean mechanism.

This developmental picture connects directly to Day 2's "kill-the-winner" versus "piggyback-the-winner" ecological models. The infant gut — young bacterial colonizers growing fast, high phage diversity, frequent lytic turnover — is the setting where kill-the-winner-style predation is thought to dominate, actively culling whichever bacterial strain is temporarily winning and keeping the young, unstable community from collapsing into a single dominant species. As the bacterial community stabilizes into the denser, slower-turnover adult-like state by age two or three, conditions are thought to shift toward piggyback-the-winner: more lysogeny, calmer phage dynamics, a gut where phages are less often actively killing their hosts and more often simply riding along inside them. This is a plausible, evidence-supported mapping rather than a fully settled fact — the field's two models were developed from the ecological dynamics first, and life-stage correlation is still being filled in — but it gives Day 3's developmental story a mechanistic backbone from Day 2's infection-outcome story.

Manrique and colleagues' 2016 PNAS study asked a different, more statistical question: once the adult virome has stabilized, do most healthy people actually share any of it? Comparing viral sequence data across 64 healthy adults from several countries, they built a network of viral genome clusters and found that a minority of these clusters — a "core" set — was shared across more than half of all the people sampled, a larger "common" set appeared in a fifth to a half of individuals, and the remainder was rare or essentially person-specific. This was an important conceptual move: it reframed "healthy virome" not as a particular fixed inventory of species everyone must carry, but as a statistical structure — a thin, widely shared core sitting on top of enormous person-to-person variation, the same pattern seen in bacterial microbiome studies. CrAssphage genomes were recovered among that shared core, consistent with its status (established on Day 1) as one of the most common single sequences in human stool worldwide, and consistent with later observations that crAssphage tends to persist durably even after a gut community is disrupted and reconstituted, for example following fecal microbiota transplantation. A separate, independently published study the same year, by Guerin, Shkoporov and colleagues in *Cell Host & Microbe* (2018), later catalogued 249 distinct crAss-like phage genomes across 702 fecal samples and organized them into four candidate subfamilies and ten candidate genera — evidence that "crAssphage" is really shorthand for an entire family of related, core-associated phages rather than one single organism. Manrique et al. also reported that their core bacteriophages were found in a significantly smaller share of people with gastrointestinal or irritable bowel disease — an early hint, not yet a mechanism, that Day 5 will return to when it asks whether virome changes cause disease or merely track it.

What happens after this adult "core" settles in? The 2026 Frontiers in Aging review synthesizes a smaller, newer body of evidence suggesting the virome does not simply stay fixed for the rest of life. It describes age-related shifts including the expansion of particular bacteriophage groups, reactivation of latent (prophage) viruses, and the persistence of certain commensal viral "pathobionts" — organisms that are normally harmless but may become more consequential in an aging gut — and links these shifts to immunosenescence (the general decline in immune competence with age) and to chronic low-grade inflammation ("inflammaging"). The most striking and somewhat counterintuitive finding the review highlights concerns centenarians: rather than showing a depleted, less healthy-looking virome, people who reach extreme old age tend to show greater viral diversity and more lytic (actively replicating) phage activity than typical older adults, alongside enrichment of phage-encoded metabolic functions — a pattern the authors tentatively interpret as a feature of resilient, rather than failing, aging, though the review is careful to flag that most of this evidence is correlational and that the same "viral dark matter" problem from Day 1 remains a major limitation at every stage of the life course, including old age.

Put together, today's readings suggest the virome is best understood not as a single thing a person "has," but as a developmental process: absent at birth, rapidly colonized and restructured through infancy in step with bacterial succession, settling by early childhood into an adult pattern with a thin but real shared "core" (crAssphage chief among it), and then shifting again — in ways still being mapped — in later life, with centenarians as a particularly interesting and still poorly understood edge case. This life-course framing is the backdrop the rest of the week needs: Day 4 asks whether any of this developmental or compositional knowledge can be turned into therapy, and Day 5 asks the harder causal question of what happens when this trajectory goes wrong.

## Questions to think about

1. If crAssphage's late appearance in infancy is explained by its bacterial host arriving late, what would that imply about trying to use "when a given phage appears" as a general developmental milestone, versus just a proxy for bacterial colonization timing?
2. Manrique et al. found healthy people share only a small "core" of viral clusters against a background of mostly person-specific viruses. Given how much of any individual's virome is "dark matter" (Day 1), how much of that apparent person-specificity might reflect a real biological fact, as opposed to how much might simply be the limits of matching unidentified sequences across different people's unidentified sequences?
3. The centenarian finding — more viral diversity and more lytic activity associated with exceptional longevity — cuts against a naive assumption that "more phage activity = more gut disruption = worse." What alternative explanations (beyond the virome directly causing resilience) could produce that same correlation?
