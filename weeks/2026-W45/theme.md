# The gut virome — bacteriophages as an overlooked layer of the human microbiome

Discussions of the "microbiome" almost always mean bacteria, but every gram of human stool also carries billions of viruses — the gut virome — and the overwhelming majority of them are bacteriophages, viruses that infect bacteria rather than human cells. These phages do not merely coexist with the bacterial microbiome: they shape which bacterial strains thrive, shuttle genes (including antibiotic-resistance and virulence genes) between bacteria, and are increasingly implicated in conditions ranging from inflammatory bowel disease to malnutrition. Yet the gut virome remains the least-characterized layer of the gut ecosystem — a large fraction of viral sequence data from stool samples still cannot be matched to any known organism, so-called viral "dark matter."

The topic matters for at least three reasons. First, phages are a plausible lever for manipulating the microbiome with surgical precision — unlike antibiotics or probiotics, a phage can target a single bacterial strain without collateral damage, which is driving renewed interest in phage therapy against antibiotic-resistant infections. Second, the virome develops in a structured way from birth through adulthood and appears to influence immune training in infancy, with implications for conditions like allergies and malnutrition. Third, because so much of the virome is still unidentified, the field is a useful case study in how metagenomics pushes the boundary of what counts as "known" biology, and how scientists build confidence in findings from mostly unannotated sequence data.

This week moves from the big picture (what the gut virome is, how big a role phages play, and why most of it is still unidentified) through the mechanisms of phage-bacteria interaction, the state of the art in phage therapy, open controversies about causality in disease, the methods that make virome science possible, and closes with the societal and clinical stakes.

## Tentative 7-day outline

1. **Day 1 — Panorama**: what the gut virome is, why bacteriophages dominate it, the scale of "viral dark matter," and why this layer of the microbiome was long overlooked.
2. **Day 2 — Mechanisms**: how phages infect bacteria (lytic vs. lysogenic cycles), horizontal gene transfer, and how phage-bacteria dynamics shape microbial community structure.
3. **Day 3 — Key findings / development across life**: how the virome is established in infancy, changes through childhood and aging, and what crAssphage and other core phages reveal about a "healthy" virome.
4. **Day 4 — State of the art**: phage therapy against antibiotic-resistant bacteria — current clinical use, trials, and engineering approaches.
5. **Day 5 — Controversies and open debates**: virome shifts in inflammatory bowel disease, obesity and other conditions — association versus causation, and why single-virus biomarkers have been elusive.
6. **Day 6 — Methods**: how researchers sequence and analyze the virome (viral-like-particle enrichment, reference-free assembly, host prediction) and why most of it still can't be identified.
7. **Day 7 — Synthesis**: what's settled vs. open, the biggest unresolved questions, and pointers for going deeper (including phage therapy's regulatory and clinical outlook).

The outline is a guide, not a contract, and may shift as the week's research turns up more specific angles.
