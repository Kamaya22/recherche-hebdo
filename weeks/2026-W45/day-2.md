# The gut virome — Day 2/7: How phages infect bacteria, swap genes, and shape the bacterial community

## Context

Day 1 established that the gut virome is overwhelmingly a bacteriophage population, and that a large share of it is still unidentified "viral dark matter." Today we zoom in from that panorama to mechanism: what actually happens when a single phage meets a single bacterium, and how millions of those encounters, repeated every day across trillions of bacterial cells, add up to population-level effects on the gut ecosystem.

Three things happen at this scale that matter for everything later in the week. First, a phage infecting a bacterium can take one of two fundamentally different paths — destroy the cell immediately (the lytic cycle) or quietly integrate into its genome and ride along (the lysogenic cycle) — and which path dominates in the gut shapes how much the virome perturbs versus stabilizes the bacterial community. Second, phages are not just predators: the same machinery that lets them package and inject DNA occasionally packages bacterial DNA instead, making phages a vehicle for horizontal gene transfer (HGT) between bacteria, including genes for antibiotic resistance and virulence. Third, zoomed out to the level of whole bacterial populations, the balance between lytic and lysogenic infection generates ecological dynamics — competing models describe the gut as alternating between phage-driven "predator-prey" cycles and a more stable, lysogeny-dominated regime.

Understanding these three threads — individual infection outcome, gene transfer, and population dynamics — is the mechanistic foundation for everything the rest of the week builds on: how the virome is seeded in infancy and changes with age (Day 3), where phage therapy's precision actually comes from (Day 4), why cause-and-effect in virome–disease associations is so hard to pin down (Day 5), and why most virome sequence data resists identification in the first place (Day 6).

## Today's readings (~20 min)

**Bacteriophage-Bacteria Interactions in the Gut: From Invertebrates to Mammals** (Kirsch, Brzozowski, Faith, Round, Secor & Duerkop, *Annual Review of Virology*, 2021) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8484061/ — ~8 min (sections on phage biology basics and ecological interactions). A peer-reviewed, invited review commissioned by Annual Reviews (a nonprofit scholarly publisher); the journal is subscription-access but this article's author-accepted manuscript is deposited and freely readable on PubMed Central. It lays out the lytic/lysogenic distinction and the ecological forces — predation, lysogeny, and gene transfer — through which phages shape gut bacterial communities.

**Phage-mediated horizontal gene transfer and its implications for the human gut microbiome** (Borodovich, Shkoporov, Ross & Hill, *Gastroenterology Report*, 2022) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9006064/ — ~7 min. A peer-reviewed, fully open-access review in a gastroenterology journal published by Oxford University Press. It is written by one of the field's leading gut-virome groups (APC Microbiome Ireland) and systematically covers the known mechanisms by which phages move bacterial genes around — directly relevant given how numerous tailed phages are in the human gut.

**Piggyback-the-Winner in host-associated microbial communities** (Silveira & Rohwer, *npj Biofilms and Microbiomes*, 2016) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5515262/ — ~5 min. A short, peer-reviewed, open-access Perspective in a Nature Portfolio journal, proposing an ecological model (building on earlier work by Knowles, Rohwer and colleagues) for how lysogeny versus lytic replication shifts with bacterial density — a direct, concise counterpoint to the older "kill-the-winner" predator-prey model.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

At the level of a single bacterium, a phage's encounter has two possible outcomes, and the distinction matters enormously for everything downstream. In the **lytic cycle**, a phage attaches to a specific receptor on the bacterial surface, injects its genome, hijacks the host's machinery to manufacture new phage particles, and ultimately bursts (lyses) the cell to release dozens to hundreds of progeny phages — a one-way, destructive outcome for that bacterium. In the **lysogenic cycle**, by contrast, a "temperate" phage integrates its genome into the bacterial chromosome as a dormant **prophage**, where it is copied passively every time the bacterium divides, doing no obvious harm for many generations. Prophages are not necessarily permanent: various stresses (DNA damage, antibiotics, or other triggers that activate the bacterial SOS response) can induce a prophage to excise itself and switch back into the lytic cycle, lysing its long-time host. Kirsch et al. note that temperate phages carrying the capacity for lysogeny are common among gut-associated phages, and that this lysogeny/induction switch, well characterized in simple laboratory systems like *E. coli* and phage lambda, is far less well understood inside the dense, chemically complex environment of the gut.

The same molecular machinery that lets phages package and inject their own DNA is also the basis for **horizontal gene transfer (HGT)**: an error, or sometimes a specialized evolved mechanism, that causes phage particles to package bacterial DNA instead of (or in addition to) their own, and then deliver that DNA into the next bacterium they infect. Borodovich and colleagues catalogue several distinct routes: **generalized transduction**, where phage packaging machinery mistakenly grabs essentially random fragments of the host chromosome; **specialized transduction**, where a prophage excises imprecisely and carries nearby host genes along with it; **lateral transduction**, a mechanism discovered only in 2018 (Chen et al., *Science*, in *Staphylococcus aureus*) in which an integrated prophage begins replicating in place, inside the bacterial chromosome, before it is excised — producing many copies of large flanking chromosomal segments that get packaged into phage particles at a frequency reported to be orders of magnitude higher than classical transduction; and **molecular piracy**, where unrelated mobile genetic elements (such as staphylococcal pathogenicity islands) hijack a phage's packaging apparatus to spread themselves. Because tailed phages vastly outnumber any other category of virus-like particle in human stool, Borodovich et al. argue they are plausibly the single most abundant class of gene-transfer vehicle in the gut — a route by which antibiotic-resistance genes, virulence factors, and other adaptive traits could move between bacterial strains and species without any contact beyond a shared viral infection. It is worth being precise about the evidence here: that phages *can* carry out all of these transfer mechanisms is well established in cultured, often non-gut model systems; how much this actually drives antibiotic-resistance spread specifically inside the living human gut is much less directly measured, and remains an active research question rather than a settled fact.

Zooming out from single infections to whole bacterial populations, these two outcomes (kill now vs. integrate and wait) translate into two competing ecological models of how phages shape microbial community structure. The older model, **"kill-the-winner,"** treats phage predation as analogous to predator-prey (Lotka-Volterra) dynamics: whichever bacterial strain becomes locally dominant and fast-growing is disproportionately targeted by the phages specialized to infect it, which lyse it back down and let slower-growing strains recover — a negative-feedback mechanism that is thought to maintain bacterial diversity by preventing any one strain from taking over. Silveira and Rohwer's **"piggyback-the-winner"** model, by contrast, argues that at high bacterial abundance and growth rate — the normal situation inside a dense, well-fed adult gut — temperate phages are favored to switch toward lysogeny rather than lysis: riding along passively inside already-successful hosts is a better evolutionary bet than risking lysis when hosts are abundant and dividing fast. Evidence reviewed by Kirsch et al. and others suggests these two regimes may map onto different life stages: kill-the-winner dynamics, with high phage abundance and diversity, appear to dominate the rapidly-shifting infant gut, while piggyback-the-winner dynamics, with rising lysogeny and comparatively lower phage particle counts, are hypothesized to prevail in the more stable healthy adult gut (a theme Day 3 picks up in more detail).

These models are not mutually exclusive in space. A related and complementary idea, the "bacteriophage adherence to mucus" (BAM) model proposed by Barr and colleagues in a widely cited 2013 *PNAS* study, found that phages (via immunoglobulin-like protein domains on their surface) preferentially bind mucin glycoproteins across mucus layers from humans down to simple marine animals, concentrating phages at the mucosal surface where bacteria tend to be denser. This offers one plausible explanation for how both predatory kill-the-winner dynamics and more passive piggyback-the-winner dynamics could coexist in different physical niches of the same gut: more lytic predation in the comparatively sparse gut lumen, more lysogeny in the dense, mucus-adherent bacterial biofilm nearer the gut lining. None of this is fully settled — both the exact triggers for lysogenic induction inside the gut, and how universally the BAM model applies to temperate (versus strictly lytic) phages, remain open questions the field is still working through.

Put together, today's readings reframe the virome not just as a passive byproduct of bacterial life, but as an active structuring force with two distinct levers: it can directly cull dominant bacterial strains (lysis), and it can quietly rewire bacterial genomes by moving genes between them (lysogeny plus transduction). Both levers operate continuously and simultaneously across the gut's trillions of bacteria-phage encounters, which is why the next few days — on how this system is seeded in infancy, exploited for therapy, and implicated in disease — all trace back to the lytic/lysogenic balance and gene-transfer machinery covered today.

## Questions to think about

1. If piggyback-the-winner is correct that lysogeny rises when bacteria are abundant and growing fast, what would that predict about how antibiotic use — which kills bacteria and can also trigger prophage induction through the SOS response — might temporarily shift the gut's phages toward more lytic activity?
2. Lateral transduction was discovered as recently as 2018, in a bacterium (*Staphylococcus aureus*) that is not even a dominant gut organism. Given how much gut virome sequence data remains unidentified "dark matter" (Day 1), how confident should we be that we already know the main mechanisms by which gut phages transfer genes, as opposed to just the ones studied so far?
3. The BAM model suggests gut geography (mucus layer vs. open lumen) might determine whether a phage behaves more "predatory" or more "passive." What would this imply for the common practice of characterizing "the gut virome" from a single, well-mixed stool sample?
