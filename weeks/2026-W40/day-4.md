# The sociology of loneliness — Day 4/7: Is loneliness really rising? Trends over time, across countries, and through the pandemic

## Context

Day 1 opened with a number that has since anchored this whole week: the WHO Commission's estimate that roughly one in six people worldwide feel lonely today. Day 3 showed how costly that feeling (and the related but distinct state of social isolation) can be, with hazard ratios for mortality, cardiovascular disease and dementia that have held up remarkably well across a decade of replication. But none of that establishes the claim that has done the most to put loneliness on the political agenda — that it is a *rising* "epidemic," spreading faster than it used to and demanding emergency-level public-health intervention. That claim is logically separate from the prevalence and health-risk findings of Days 1 and 3: a problem can be common and costly without becoming more common over time, and the case for treating loneliness as an epidemic specifically rests on trend data, not snapshot data.

This distinction matters well beyond semantics. If loneliness is accelerating — driven by some genuinely new social force such as smartphones, remote work, or the collapse of civic and religious participation — the policy response looks different than if loneliness has always been roughly this common and we are simply measuring and discussing it more than before. Governments that create "Ministers for Loneliness" or commission WHO reports are implicitly betting on the first story. Today we test that bet directly against the trend literature: cross-temporal meta-analyses that track loneliness scores across decades, large cross-country comparisons that look for geographic patterns rather than a single global trend, and the natural experiment of COVID-19, which let researchers compare the same populations' loneliness levels immediately before and during a uniquely disruptive period of enforced isolation.

A methodological note before diving in, because it shapes how to read every number below: measuring change in loneliness over calendar time is harder than measuring loneliness at a single point, because it requires comparable survey instruments, sampling methods, and populations administered years or decades apart — conditions real-world survey archives rarely meet perfectly. Several of today's readings are explicit about working around this with statistical corrections (for instrument type, age range, publication bias) rather than claiming clean comparisons; keep that caveat in mind as a feature of the evidence base, not a reason to dismiss it. Day 5 later this week will return to measurement controversies in more depth; today's focus is squarely on what the best available trend data show.

## Today's readings

**Is Loneliness in Emerging Adults Increasing Over Time? A Preregistered Cross-Temporal Meta-Analysis and Systematic Review** (Buecker, Mund, Chwastek, Sostmann & Luhmann, *Psychological Bulletin*, 2021, vol. 147, pp. 787–805) — https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000332 — ~7 min (abstract, results summary). A preregistered meta-analysis in the American Psychological Association's flagship synthesis journal, pooling 449 mean loneliness scores from 345 studies and over 124,000 emerging adults (ages 18–29) who completed the UCLA Loneliness Scale between 1976 and 2019. Because it is preregistered and restricted to a single consistently used instrument, it is one of the most methodologically careful attempts to isolate a genuine historical trend from measurement noise.

**Loneliness Before and During the COVID-19 Pandemic: A Systematic Review with Meta-Analysis** (Ernst, Niederer, Werner, Czaja, Mikton, Ong, Rosen, Brähler & Beutel, *American Psychologist*, 2022, vol. 77, pp. 660–677) — https://doi.org/10.1037/amp0001005 — ~6 min (abstract, results summary). A systematic review and meta-analysis in the American Psychological Association's flagship general journal, pooling 34 studies (215,026 participants) comparing loneliness before and during the pandemic, with a core analysis of 19 longitudinal studies that followed the same people across both periods — letting the pandemic function as a natural experiment on whether mass enforced isolation actually moved the needle on loneliness, and by how much.

**The Prevalence of Loneliness Across 113 Countries: Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis** (Surkalim, Luo, Eres, Gebel, van Buskirk, Bauman & Ding, *The BMJ*, 2022, vol. 376, article e067068) — https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2021-067068 — ~7 min (abstract, results summary, discussion). A systematic review and meta-analysis published in The BMJ (whose original research articles are free to read), pooling 24 studies covering 212 estimates from 106 countries collected between 2000 and 2019. Rather than tracking change over time in one place, it is the best current source for geographic variation — essential context for judging whether "rising loneliness" is a uniform global phenomenon or a patchwork that looks very different depending where, and in whom, you look.

## Guided summary

Start with the longest and most carefully controlled time series available: Buecker and colleagues' 2021 meta-analysis of emerging adults from 1976 to 2019. It found that loneliness levels did increase with calendar year, by about 0.56 standard deviations on the UCLA Loneliness Scale over the 43-year window — a real, statistically robust trend, not a measurement artifact, since the analysis was preregistered and restricted to a single consistent instrument. But the headline number hides the finding that matters most for judging the "epidemic" framing: the upward trend was concentrated in North American samples and considerably weaker, in some analyses negligible, in European and Asian samples. In other words, the single best long-run trend estimate we have does not describe a universal human condition getting steadily worse everywhere; it describes a pattern that is substantially a North American story, which should immediately raise the question of what is different about North American social, economic or technological conditions over this period — a question this week will return to on Day 6 when we look at interventions, since the right fix for a regionally concentrated trend likely differs from the right fix for a universal one.

That regional unevenness is reinforced, from a different angle, by Surkalim and colleagues' 2022 BMJ meta-analysis, which does not track change over time but instead maps loneliness prevalence across 113 countries and territories for the period 2000–2019. Rather than one global number, it finds "substantial geographical variation," with northern European countries consistently showing the lowest levels of loneliness and other regions, including parts of the Middle East and sub-Saharan Africa, showing considerably higher rates, alongside large and unevenly distributed gaps in available data — entire regions, especially low- and middle-income countries, for which almost no loneliness data exists at all for any time period. This matters directly for the "epidemic" framing: declaring a global epidemic implies a reasonably global evidence base, but the data genuinely do not cover most of the world, and where they do exist, the picture is one of persistent geographic difference rather than convergence toward a single rising trend.

The clearest natural experiment this week has access to is the COVID-19 pandemic itself, and Ernst and colleagues' 2022 meta-analysis in American Psychologist is the most rigorous attempt to quantify what it actually did to loneliness. Pooling 19 longitudinal studies that followed the same individuals before and during the pandemic (215,026 participants across the full review), they found a statistically significant increase in loneliness, but a small one in effect-size terms (a standardized mean difference around 0.2–0.3, in the range conventionally labelled "small"). This is a genuinely useful falsification test: if loneliness responds this modestly to a global event that forcibly cut off in-person contact for hundreds of millions of people for months, it suggests loneliness is not simply a linear function of opportunities for social contact — consistent with Day 1's point that loneliness (the subjective feeling) and social isolation (the objective contact count) are related but distinct, since the pandemic clearly reduced objective contact far more than it moved the subjective feeling. It is also worth noting, since several other studies (not a formal reading today, but worth flagging) that followed populations further into the pandemic found loneliness receding again as restrictions lifted, with levels by 2022–2023 often back close to pre-pandemic baselines — the pandemic bump looks, so far, like a temporary shock rather than a permanent step-change in the underlying trend.

Not every available data source agrees on even the direction of travel, and that disagreement is itself informative. A 2024 study in the *Journal of Affective Disorders* (Smith, López Sánchez, Pizzol and colleagues) examined perceived loneliness among roughly 180,000 adolescents across 28 countries in Africa, Asia and the Americas between 2003 and 2017, using the Global School-based Student Health Survey. Rather than finding a uniform rise, the study found statistically significant increasing trends in six countries, decreasing trends in six different countries, and no significant trend at all in the remaining sixteen — with the steepest rise recorded in Egypt and the steepest fall in Samoa. A separate, frequently cited 2023 analysis of United States Time Use Survey data (Kannan & Veazie, in the open-access journal *SSM – Population Health*) reported a rise in time spent alone between 2003 and 2020 and became one of the studies cited in US policy discussions of a loneliness epidemic; it measures a related but distinct construct (objective time alone, not felt loneliness), and its interpretation has subsequently been debated, with critics arguing the visualized trend looks much larger than the underlying effect size once plotted on a non-truncated scale — a dispute this week will not resolve, but one worth knowing exists before treating any single trend line as settled.

Putting today's evidence together: there is real, replicated evidence that loneliness has risen over recent decades in at least one well-studied population (North American emerging adults) and real, if modest and apparently temporary, evidence that the COVID-19 pandemic nudged loneliness upward globally. But there is no comparably strong evidence of a uniform, accelerating, worldwide rise — the country-level and regional data instead show a mix of rising, falling, and flat trends, large data gaps outside high-income countries, and genuine debate over how to interpret the trend lines that do exist. The honest summary is not "the epidemic framing is a myth" nor "the epidemic framing is fully vindicated," but something more specific: loneliness is common and costly (established on Days 1 and 3) in a way that looks reasonably stable or slowly worsening in some populations and places, stable or improving in others, with COVID-19 as a real but apparently transient global disruption rather than the start of a new permanent trajectory.

## Questions to think about

1. If the clearest rising trend (Buecker et al.'s 43-year North American finding) is regionally concentrated rather than global, what region-specific explanations would you want investigated before assuming a single universal cause — and how would that change what "fixing the loneliness epidemic" should even mean as a policy goal?
2. The COVID-19 pandemic produced a huge, well-documented reduction in objective social contact but only a small, apparently temporary rise in subjective loneliness. What does that gap tell you about how much objective contact matters compared with other factors (expectations, social skills, the meaning people attach to isolation) in determining whether someone feels lonely?
3. Large parts of the world — especially low- and middle-income countries — are essentially missing from the loneliness trend literature. Before extending claims like "loneliness is rising worldwide" to those regions, what would you want to see established first, and how might the drivers of loneliness there differ from the North American and European contexts that dominate today's evidence base?
