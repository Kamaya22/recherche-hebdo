# Long COVID — Day 6/7: What's actually being tested in the clinic, and the cost of a still-unproven illness

## Context

Days 2–3 laid out the competing biological hypotheses for long COVID (viral persistence, autoimmunity, microclotting, autonomic/neuro-inflammatory dysfunction); Day 4 showed why none of them has yet produced a validated diagnostic test; Day 5 showed how that diagnostic vacuum became entangled with a decades-old biomedical-versus-psychosomatic controversy inherited from ME/CFS research, and how ME/CFS's own history offers concrete methodological lessons — above all, that mixing heterogeneous patient subgroups under one broad label can dilute a real biological signal into statistical noise. Today asks the practical question that patients, clinicians, and health systems cannot wait for a fully settled mechanism to answer: given everything covered this week, what treatments are actually being trialed, do any of them work, and what does the illness cost society while the science catches up?

The short answer on treatments is sobering: as of today there is still no approved treatment for long COVID anywhere in the world. What exists instead is a widening portfolio of repurposed-drug trials, each one implicitly a test of one of this week's mechanistic hypotheses, plus a separate and increasingly cautious line of research into rehabilitation — cautious precisely because of the post-exertional malaise (PEM) problem Day 3 introduced and Day 5's ME/CFS history warned about explicitly. The second half of today looks at the societal side: how much long COVID costs economies and individuals, and why a condition with no biomarker (Day 4) and no agreed definition (Day 5) is also unusually hard for disability and insurance systems to adjudicate.

## Today's readings (~21 min)

1. **Nirmatrelvir–ritonavir versus placebo–ritonavir in individuals with long COVID in the USA (PAX LC): a double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled, phase 2, decentralised trial** (Baden et al., for the RECOVER-VITAL platform, *The Lancet Infectious Diseases*, 2025) — https://www.thelancet.com/journals/laninf/article/PIIS1473-3099(25)00073-8/fulltext (DOI: 10.1016/S1473-3099(25)00073-8) — ~8 min (abstract, Findings, and Interpretation). *The Lancet Infectious Diseases* is a leading peer-reviewed clinical-infectious-disease journal (Elsevier, London). This NIH-funded, 959-participant, 69-site US trial directly tested Day 2's viral-persistence hypothesis by giving 15 or 25 days of Paxlovid (versus 5-day-equivalent placebo) to people with established long COVID, and is the largest and most rigorous test yet of whether an antiviral can treat the condition once it has set in.

2. **Prevalence and Impact of Postexertional Malaise on Recovery in Adults With Post-COVID-19 Condition: A Systematic Review With Meta-analysis** (Pouliopoulou, Hawthorne, MacDermid, Billias, Miller, Quinn et al., *Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation*, 2025) — https://www.archives-pmr.org/article/S0003-9993(25)00501-5/fulltext (DOI: 10.1016/j.apmr.2025.01.471) — ~7 min (abstract, pooled-prevalence results, and the section on rehabilitation-trial reporting of PEM). *Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation* is a peer-reviewed rehabilitation-medicine journal (Elsevier). This meta-analysis pools the evidence on how common PEM is in long COVID and — most usefully for today — audits how well rehabilitation trials have actually measured it, making it the most direct empirical follow-up to Day 5's warning about GET-style trials that don't screen for PEM.

3. **Economic burden of long COVID: macroeconomic, cost-of-illness and microeconomic impacts** (Bansal, *npj Primary Care Respiratory Medicine*, 2025, vol. 35, article 53) — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41533-025-00460-8 (DOI: 10.1038/s41533-025-00460-8) — ~6 min (At a glance / summary figures and the sections on labour-market and health-system impacts). *npj Primary Care Respiratory Medicine* is a fully open-access Nature Portfolio journal. This brief communication synthesises the available macroeconomic, health-system-cost, and individual-cost evidence into the clearest current picture of what long COVID costs — and where the data are still too patchy to give a precise global number.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with the antiviral result, because it is the cleanest illustration of how a trial can directly test one of this week's mechanistic stories. If SARS-CoV-2 (or fragments of it) persist somewhere in the body and keep provoking immune activity — the viral-persistence hypothesis from Day 2 — then a strong, extended course of an antiviral already approved for acute infection should, in principle, help. RECOVER-VITAL/PAX LC tested exactly this: 15 or 25 days of nirmatrelvir–ritonavir versus placebo, in patients pre-classified into three of long COVID's most common phenotypes (cognitive dysfunction, autonomic dysfunction, exercise intolerance — the same three domains Day 3 covered). The result was unambiguous: no meaningful benefit in any of the three groups, on any of the drug's two durations, over six months of follow-up, alongside a real safety cost (more gastrointestinal side effects on-drug, and a handful of serious adverse events including suspected drug-induced liver injury). This does not disprove viral persistence as a mechanism — persistent viral reservoirs, if they exist, may sit in tissue compartments a short oral antiviral course cannot adequately reach, or may no longer be the active driver by the time symptoms are chronic — but it does close off the simplest, most obvious therapeutic prediction that hypothesis made.

Metformin's story, run in parallel by the same broader research effort, makes an important methodological point that echoes Day 5 directly. In the 2023 COVID-OUT trial (Bramante et al., *The Lancet Infectious Diseases*), giving metformin within three days of a positive SARS-CoV-2 test reduced long COVID diagnoses over the following ten months by roughly 40% (6.3% vs 10.4% on placebo) — a genuinely large prevention effect for a cheap, generic, well-tolerated drug. But a separate 2026 trial testing the same drug as a treatment for fatigue in people who already had long COVID (Reis et al., *Annals of Internal Medicine*, an adaptive randomised trial in 399 adults in Brazil) found no benefit from metformin at all; the trial's other arm, fluvoxamine (an SSRI with additional anti-inflammatory, sigma-1-receptor-agonist activity), did significantly reduce fatigue and improve quality of life, with a sustained effect at 90 days and fewer side effects than either metformin or placebo. Read together, prevention and treatment are not the same problem: a drug that can stop inflammatory or vascular processes from becoming chronic in the first days of infection may do nothing once those processes (or their downstream consequences) are already established — which is exactly the kind of phenotype-and-timing-sensitive effect that Jason and colleagues' Day 5 argument would predict gets lost if trials pool patients too broadly or intervene too late in the illness course.

Beyond antivirals and repurposed metabolic/psychiatric drugs, a wider pipeline is now testing other mechanistic hypotheses directly: the RECOVER Treating Long COVID (RECOVER-TLC) platform's first round of trials includes baricitinib (a JAK inhibitor, testing the immune-dysregulation hypothesis from Day 2 on cognitive and cardiopulmonary symptoms), low-dose naltrexone (tested for fatigue, with a hypothesised anti-neuroinflammatory mechanism), GLP-1 receptor agonists, and stellate ganglion block (a targeted procedure aimed at the autonomic nervous system, testing Day 3's dysautonomia hypothesis directly). Notably, the roughly 600 candidate treatments submitted by long COVID patients themselves to RECOVER-TLC's public portal between September 2024 and August 2025 is an unusually direct instance of patient-community input shaping which hypotheses get funded and tested — itself a partial institutional response to the trust deficit Day 5 described.

Rehabilitation is where this week's mechanistic and historical threads meet most directly. The Pouliopoulou et al. meta-analysis found that PEM affects roughly 36% of community-dwelling adults with long COVID (pooled across studies, with wide uncertainty — 19–57% — reflecting how differently PEM has been defined and measured across the literature), and, critically, that a large share of long COVID exercise and rehabilitation trials still do not screen participants for PEM or measure it as an outcome before prescribing graded activity increases. That is precisely the failure mode Day 5's Vink & Vink-Niese review documented for ME/CFS's graded exercise therapy trials, now showing up as an ongoing gap in long COVID's own evidence base rather than a solved problem from the past. The field's better answer, where it has been tried, looks different from classic GET: structured pacing protocols (using tools like the Borg CR-10 exertion scale to keep activity below each patient's individual PEM threshold rather than increasing it on a fixed schedule) and "sub-symptom-threshold" exercise programmes have shown improvements in PESE episodes, symptoms, and quality of life in smaller studies — an approach built around respecting PEM rather than trying to train through it.

Turning to the societal side, the Bansal review's synthesis puts numbers on what all of this uncertainty costs. Its central, if necessarily rough, estimate is a global annual economic burden on the order of $1 trillion, driven by a combination of direct healthcare costs, an average of roughly $9,000 in individual costs per affected person in the United States (with some patients facing far higher out-of-pocket expenses), and around $170 billion in annual lost earnings in the US alone from reduced work capacity — effects the review notes can persist for up to three years post-infection. The review is explicit about its own limitations: cost estimates vary widely across studies because they use different definitions (recall Day 5), different time horizons, and different methods, and almost no long-term, standardized, cross-country data exist yet — a research gap with real consequences, since disability insurers, employers, and health systems are already making coverage and accommodation decisions under exactly this uncertainty.

Put together, today's material shows why the biological, diagnostic, and social threads of this week cannot really be separated. A condition with no validated biomarker (Day 4) is a condition where clinical trials must rely on self-reported, heterogeneous symptom clusters to select participants — which, per Day 5's central methodological lesson, makes it structurally harder to detect a real drug effect even when one exists for a subgroup. And a condition adjudicated by symptom report rather than test result is, for exactly the same reason, harder for a disability examiner or insurer to verify — which is part of why the economic burden the Bansal review documents falls so unevenly, and why the accommodations and payouts patients receive so often depend on the credibility of a "diagnosis of exclusion" that Day 5 showed remains scientifically and institutionally contested.

## Questions to think about

1. RECOVER-VITAL tested an antiviral in phenotype-defined subgroups and still found no effect, while the metformin story shows the same drug can prevent an outcome it fails to treat once established. What would a next-generation viral-persistence trial need to look like — in timing, patient selection, or drug choice — to give that hypothesis a fairer test?
2. Pouliopoulou et al. found many long COVID rehabilitation trials still don't screen for or measure PEM, repeating a mistake Day 5 traced back decades in ME/CFS research. Why might that mistake be so persistent even after it is well documented — and what would it take to make PEM-screening a non-negotiable standard in trial design?
3. The Bansal review shows real economic costs but acknowledges the underlying data are inconsistent and incomplete. If you were designing a disability or insurance policy for long COVID today, how would you balance the risk of under-supporting genuinely disabled patients (given no biomarker to confirm claims) against the risk of a definition so broad it becomes impossible to fund or administer?
