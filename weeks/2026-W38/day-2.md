# Long COVID — Day 2/7: Three competing biological hypotheses — viral persistence, autoimmunity, and microclots

## Context

Day 1 established that long COVID is currently defined clinically — by a symptom pattern and a time course — rather than by any confirmatory biological test. Today we start unpacking why that is: it is not that researchers lack hypotheses about what causes long COVID, but that there are several plausible, only partly overlapping mechanisms under active investigation at once, each with real supporting evidence and each, on its own, unable to explain every patient. Today covers three of the leading candidates: (1) persistence of SARS-CoV-2 itself, or fragments of it, somewhere in the body after the acute infection has cleared; (2) dysregulation of the immune system that tips over into autoimmunity, where the body's own defenses start attacking its own tissues; and (3) damage to the endothelium — the thin layer of cells lining blood vessels — that leads to abnormal blood clotting at a microscopic scale.

These three hypotheses are not mutually exclusive, and a recurring theme in today's readings is that they plausibly feed into one another: a persistent viral reservoir could keep provoking the immune system, chronic immune activation could damage the vascular endothelium, and endothelial damage could in turn help a virus or its remnants persist by disrupting normal immune surveillance and blood flow to tissues. Keeping track of these possible feedback loops matters because it is one reason a "just find the one true cause" approach has been slow to pay off, and why treatment trials (which we will cover on Day 6) increasingly test combinations or subtype-specific strategies rather than a single silver-bullet drug. Day 3 will pick up a fourth major thread — the neurological and autonomic nervous system angle, including brain fog and dysautonomia — which connects back to some of the same immune and vascular processes discussed today.

## Today's readings (~20 min)

1. **Viral persistence in long COVID: Research advances and treatment strategies** (Liu, Guo & Wang, *Infectious Diseases & Immunity*, 2025) — https://mednexus.org/doi/abs/10.1097/ID9.0000000000000163 — ~7 min (abstract plus the tissue-reservoir section). *Infectious Diseases & Immunity* is a peer-reviewed, fully open-access (CC BY-NC-ND) journal sponsored by the Chinese Medical Association and published by Wolters Kluwer Health; this review is indexed in PubMed/PMC. It surveys clinical evidence that SARS-CoV-2 RNA or antigens can persist in tissue reservoirs — for example in intestinal mucosa months after acute infection — and links this persistence to disrupted local microbiome balance, increased gut permeability, and downstream immune and vascular effects.

2. **Autoimmunity in long COVID** (Talwar, Harker, Openshaw & Thwaites, *Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology*, 2025) — https://www.jacionline.org/article/S0091-6749(25)00171-X/fulltext — ~7 min (abstract plus the autoantibody-evidence section). Published online in February 2025 as an open-access review in JACI — the flagship peer-reviewed journal of the American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology — by a team based at Imperial College London who have published extensively on COVID-19 immunology. It reviews evidence for autoantibody production during and after SARS-CoV-2 infection, and mechanisms (molecular mimicry, sustained interferon signaling, neoantigen release from tissue damage) by which a viral infection could trigger genuine autoimmunity.

3. **Vascular Complications of Long COVID — From Endothelial Dysfunction to Systemic Thrombosis: A Systematic Review** (Stojanovic et al., *International Journal of Molecular Sciences*, 2025) — DOI 10.3390/ijms27010433 — ~6 min (abstract plus the endothelial-dysfunction section). A peer-reviewed, fully open-access systematic review (MDPI) by a Belgrade-based cardiovascular medicine group, based on a structured search of PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science through September 2025. It synthesizes evidence that SARS-CoV-2 causes direct endothelial injury and platelet activation leading to "immunothrombosis," with patients showing microclots, elevated D-dimer, and a persistently increased risk of both venous and arterial thrombotic events.

## Guided summary

The first hypothesis, viral persistence, proposes that in at least some patients, SARS-CoV-2 — or pieces of it, such as RNA fragments or spike protein, rather than necessarily a fully replicating virus — does not get fully cleared by the immune system and continues to reside somewhere in the body. The Liu, Guo & Wang review highlights the gastrointestinal tract as a leading candidate reservoir: studies have detected SARS-CoV-2 RNA in intestinal mucosa many months after the acute infection resolved, notably in patients who also had inflammatory bowel disease, a population in whom gut biopsies are more routinely available for research. The proposed mechanism is not simply "leftover virus sitting quietly": persistent viral material in gut tissue is associated with disruption of the local microbiome and increased permeability of the intestinal lining, which could allow viral proteins or bacterial products to leak into the bloodstream and trigger immune and inflammatory responses at distant sites in the body — a plausible bridge between a localized viral reservoir and the multi-system nature of long COVID symptoms. The review frames viral persistence not as a standalone explanation but as a hub hypothesis that connects to several of the other mechanisms under investigation, including immune dysregulation, coagulation abnormalities, and reactivation of unrelated latent viruses (such as Epstein-Barr virus) that a persistently activated immune system may fail to keep suppressed.

The second hypothesis, immune dysregulation tipping into autoimmunity, starts from the observation that viral infections are a well-known trigger for autoimmune disease more broadly, and asks whether SARS-CoV-2 is doing something similar in long COVID. The Talwar et al. review in JACI lays out two main proposed routes. The first is molecular mimicry: if a piece of the virus structurally resembles one of the body's own proteins, antibodies or immune cells trained to attack the virus may, by mistake, also attack the look-alike self-protein. The second is that tissue damage caused during infection releases "neoantigens" — proteins from damaged cells that the immune system does not normally see and treats as foreign — combined with sustained interferon signaling (interferons are immune-signaling proteins that stay elevated longer than expected in some long COVID patients), which together can push the immune system into a self-reactive state. The review is careful to note that the evidence for autoantibodies after SARS-CoV-2 infection is real and reasonably well replicated — several studies find that both pre-existing and newly formed autoantibodies increase after infection — but that whether these autoantibodies actually persist long enough, and are present in sufficient quantity and at the right location, to cause long COVID symptoms is still genuinely debated rather than settled.

The third hypothesis concerns the blood vessels themselves rather than the immune system in the abstract. The endothelium is the single-cell-thick lining of every blood vessel in the body, and it plays an active role in regulating blood flow, inflammation, and clotting — it is not just a passive pipe. The Stojanovic et al. systematic review describes how SARS-CoV-2 infection can directly injure endothelial cells and, combined with a pro-inflammatory cytokine environment, activates platelets in a process the authors call "immunothrombosis" — clotting driven by immune activation rather than by the mechanical injuries (cuts, fractures) that normally trigger it. The clinical signature described is fibrinogen dysregulation, persistently elevated D-dimer (a blood marker of clot breakdown), and impaired fibrinolysis — the body's normal process for dissolving clots after they form does not work as well as it should. Other research groups (not part of today's formal readings, but discussed in the broader literature this systematic review surveys) have further described "fibrinaloid microclots": small, amyloid-like clots that are unusually resistant to the body's normal clot-dissolving enzymes and that can lodge in small blood vessels, potentially limiting oxygen delivery to tissue — a mechanism that has been proposed to connect directly to fatigue and post-exertional malaise. Clinically, this translates into a documented, elevated risk of both venous thromboembolism (blood clots in the legs or lungs) and arterial events such as heart attack and stroke that can persist for months after the acute infection, not just during it.

Taken together, these three mechanisms illustrate why "what causes long COVID" resists a one-line answer. They are supported by genuinely different types of evidence — tissue biopsies and viral RNA detection for persistence, autoantibody assays and interferon signatures for autoimmunity, coagulation markers and imaging for vascular dysfunction — and none of the three reviews claims to have found the single explanation. Instead, each frames its mechanism as one contributor operating alongside, and plausibly interacting with, the others: a persistent viral reservoir could sustain chronic immune activation, chronic immune activation could injure the endothelium, and a damaged, hyperclotting endothelium could in turn impair immune surveillance and give a viral reservoir more room to persist. This interlocking, rather than competing, picture is one reason mechanism research in long COVID increasingly tries to identify which combination of processes is dominant in a given patient, rather than looking for a single universal cause — a distinction that will matter directly when we get to treatment trials on Day 6, since a drug that targets viral persistence would not be expected to help a patient whose main problem is microclotting, and vice versa.

## Questions to think about

1. If viral persistence, autoimmunity, and endothelial/microclot dysfunction can all plausibly feed into one another, how would a researcher design a study to figure out which mechanism came first in a given patient, rather than just observing that all three are present?
2. The autoimmunity review notes that autoantibodies are detected after SARS-CoV-2 infection, but that their causal role in symptoms is still debated. What kind of additional evidence (beyond detecting the antibodies) would be needed to move from "association" to "this specific autoantibody is causing this specific symptom"?
3. Microclots and endothelial dysfunction produce measurable, objective markers (D-dimer, imaging, biopsy). Why might a mechanism with an objective biomarker be especially attractive as a target for future diagnostic tests, even if it does not turn out to be the dominant cause of long COVID in most patients?
