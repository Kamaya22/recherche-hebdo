# Long COVID — Day 3/7: The neurological and autonomic angle — brain fog, dysautonomia, and the ME/CFS connection

## Context

Day 2 covered three interlocking mechanistic hypotheses for long COVID — viral persistence, autoimmunity, and endothelial/microclot dysfunction — and noted that they plausibly feed into one another rather than competing for a single "true cause." Today we turn to the symptom cluster that patients themselves rank among the most disabling and the hardest to explain to others: the neurological and autonomic manifestations, above all "brain fog" (the everyday name for measurable deficits in memory, attention, processing speed and word-finding) and dysautonomia, a broad term for malfunction of the autonomic nervous system that most visibly shows up as postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS) — a racing heart and drop in blood pressure on standing.

These are not a separate track from Monday's and yesterday's material; they are downstream of it. Chronic immune activation (Day 2's autoimmunity hypothesis) and endothelial/microclot dysfunction (Day 2's vascular hypothesis) are both leading candidate drivers of neuroinflammation, which in turn is one of the best-supported explanations for brain fog. The autonomic nervous system, meanwhile, is regulated in large part by the vagus nerve, which several groups now propose is a direct target of SARS-CoV-2 damage — giving a plausible single-nerve explanation for why cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, and cognitive symptoms so often cluster together in the same patients. This is also the day where the two-decade-old research field of myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) becomes directly relevant: as flagged on Day 1, post-exertional malaise is a defining feature both conditions share, and today's readings show that the overlap extends deep into objective autonomic physiology, not just self-reported symptoms.

Today's three readings each anchor one leg of this picture: a 2025 review of the neuroimmune biology behind brain fog, a large 2026 case-control study directly comparing autonomic testing results in long COVID and ME/CFS patients, and a 2024 hypothesis paper proposing vagus nerve/HPA-axis/mitochondrial dysfunction as a unifying mechanism. Day 4 will pick up the diagnostic-test side of this story — why none of today's mechanisms has yet produced a validated clinical biomarker — and Day 5 will return to the ME/CFS connection from the angle of its contested research history.

## Today's readings (~21 min)

1. **Neuroimmune pathophysiology of long COVID** (Moen, Baker & Iwasaki, *Psychiatry and Clinical Neurosciences*, 2025) — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/pcn.13855 — ~9 min (introduction plus the neuroinflammation/blood-brain-barrier sections). *Psychiatry and Clinical Neurosciences* is the peer-reviewed flagship journal of the Japanese Society of Psychiatry and Neurology, published by Wiley; this specific review is open access, and its senior author, Akiko Iwasaki, is a Howard Hughes Medical Institute immunologist at Yale whose lab has produced some of the most-cited work on long COVID immunology. The review synthesizes autopsy and animal-model evidence for how SARS-CoV-2 infection triggers sustained neuroinflammation, microglial activation, and disruption of the blood-brain barrier, without requiring the virus itself to persist inside the brain.

2. **Shared autonomic phenotype of long COVID and myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome** (Novak, Systrom, Fishman et al., *PLOS ONE*, 2026) — https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0341278 — ~7 min (abstract plus results tables). *PLOS ONE* is a fully open-access, peer-reviewed megajournal published by the nonprofit Public Library of Science; this is a retrospective case-control study run out of the Brigham and Women's Faulkner Hospital Autonomic Laboratory, comparing standardized autonomic test results across 143 long COVID patients, 170 pre-pandemic ME/CFS patients, 290 patients with hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, and 73 healthy controls tested between 2018 and 2023 — one of the largest head-to-head autonomic comparisons of the two conditions published to date.

3. **Connecting dots of long COVID-19 pathogenesis: a vagus nerve-hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal-mitochondrial axis dysfunction** (Camici, Del Duca, Brita & Antinori, *Frontiers in Cellular and Infection Microbiology*, 2024) — https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cellular-and-infection-microbiology/articles/10.3389/fcimb.2024.1501949/full — ~5 min (the vagus-nerve and integrative-model sections). *Frontiers in Cellular and Infection Microbiology* is a peer-reviewed, fully open-access journal (Frontiers Media); the authors are affiliated with Italy's National Institute for Infectious Diseases "Lazzaro Spallanzani." The paper proposes that three of the body's core anti-inflammatory control systems — vagal reflexes, the HPA hormonal axis, and mitochondrial redox regulation — are functionally interconnected, and that SARS-CoV-2 can damage all three simultaneously, offering one candidate unifying account of why autonomic, endocrine, and metabolic symptoms so often co-occur.

## Guided summary

Brain fog is increasingly treated by researchers not as a vague complaint but as a measurable neurological deficit with an emerging, if still incomplete, biological explanation. The Moen, Baker & Iwasaki review lays out converging evidence — much of it from autopsy studies of patients who died with COVID-19, and from animal models designed to isolate specific mechanisms — for a chain of events: SARS-CoV-2 infection triggers sustained activation of microglia (the brain's resident immune cells), which lose their normal "homeostatic" resting state and instead maintain a chronic low-level inflammatory posture; this is accompanied by disruption of the blood-brain barrier, the selective membrane that normally shields brain tissue from circulating immune signals and pathogens, allowing inflammatory molecules from the rest of the body to reach neural tissue more easily. Notably, this does not require the virus to be actively present or replicating in the brain itself — peripheral inflammation and vascular damage (Day 2's territory) appear sufficient to sustain the neuroinflammatory state. Adding a direct, patient-level biological signature to this picture, a 2025 imaging study in *Brain Communications* (Takahashi and colleagues, Yokohama City University) used a novel PET-imaging method to directly visualize AMPA receptors — proteins essential for learning and memory — in the brains of 30 long COVID patients compared with 80 healthy controls, and found a widespread increase in AMPA receptor density that correlated with the severity of patients' cognitive symptoms. That kind of finding matters because it moves brain fog from "a symptom patients report" toward "a measurable molecular state," with an obvious next question of whether AMPA receptor density could become a diagnostic biomarker or even a drug target — a thread Day 4 will pick up directly.

Turning to the autonomic side, dysautonomia in long COVID is not a single problem but a family of malfunctions in the systems that normally run on autopilot — heart rate and blood pressure regulation, gut motility, temperature control, and sleep. The most clinically recognizable form is POTS: an excessive rise in heart rate (and often a fall in blood pressure) within minutes of standing up, which can cause dizziness, palpitations, and exercise intolerance severe enough to be disabling. One proposed biological contributor, discussed in a 2025 study in the *Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology*, is the presence of autoantibodies that bind to G-protein-coupled adrenergic receptors — the same receptors that normally receive the body's "fight or flight" signals to tighten blood vessels and speed the heart when standing up. If these autoantibodies interfere with normal receptor signaling, that could directly explain the exaggerated or blunted cardiovascular responses seen on standing, and would also connect dysautonomia back to Day 2's autoimmunity hypothesis — one more example of the mechanisms this week are not really separate tracks but different windows onto overlapping biology.

The Novak, Systrom and colleagues study puts numbers on just how closely long COVID's autonomic profile resembles that of ME/CFS, a post-viral condition studied for decades before the pandemic. Comparing standardized autonomic test results across their four patient groups, they found reduced orthostatic cerebral blood flow velocity in 92% of long COVID patients and 88% of ME/CFS patients, mild-to-moderate widespread autonomic failure in 95% and 89% respectively, small fiber neuropathy (damage to the thin nerve fibers that regulate pain sensation and autonomic function) in 67% and 53%, and a formal POTS diagnosis in 22% and 19%. The strikingly similar rates across nearly every measure — despite the two patient groups having different triggering illnesses and, for the ME/CFS group, no history of COVID-19 at all — is the strongest evidence yet that long COVID and ME/CFS are not just superficially similar in symptoms but converge on a shared underlying autonomic and cerebrovascular phenotype. That does not prove they share one single cause, but it does suggest that treatments and diagnostic tools developed for one condition may transfer to the other — a possibility we will return to on Day 6.

Finally, the vagus nerve offers one candidate explanation for why cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, endocrine, and cognitive symptoms so often show up together in the same long COVID patient rather than in isolation. The vagus nerve is the body's longest cranial nerve and its principal channel for two-way communication between the brain and most internal organs; it also runs a well-characterized "cholinergic anti-inflammatory reflex" that normally dampens excessive immune activation. Camici and colleagues propose that SARS-CoV-2 can damage the vagus nerve directly (through local viral or immune injury) and indirectly (through the systemic inflammation discussed on Day 2), and that this damage, together with dysfunction of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) hormonal stress axis and impaired mitochondrial redox regulation, removes several of the body's normal brakes on inflammation at once. Because the vagus nerve also innervates the gut, this model connects naturally to a separate but related line of research — the gut-brain axis — where several groups have proposed that long COVID-associated dysbiosis (an altered gut microbial community) and increased intestinal permeability, discussed briefly on Day 2 in the context of viral reservoirs, could feed neuroinflammation and mood/cognitive symptoms via disrupted production of tryptophan-derived signaling molecules that the gut microbiome normally supplies. Encouragingly, if speculatively, this shared vagal mechanism is also why vagus nerve stimulation — a treatment already FDA-approved for epilepsy and treatment-resistant depression — is now being tested experimentally for long COVID's cognitive and autonomic symptoms, a trial thread Day 6 will cover in more depth.

Taken together, today's readings reinforce a pattern already visible on Day 2: the neurological and autonomic symptoms of long COVID are not a separate, purely "in the head" phenomenon set apart from the immune and vascular story, but a plausible downstream consequence of it, mediated substantially through neuroinflammation and vagal/autonomic damage. That matters for Day 5's controversies discussion, because it directly undercuts framings that treat brain fog, fatigue, or POTS as primarily psychological — while also explaining why, absent a single validated biomarker, these symptoms have sometimes been dismissed or misattributed in clinical practice.

## Questions to think about

1. The Novak et al. study found very similar rates of autonomic abnormalities in long COVID and pre-pandemic ME/CFS patients who were never infected with SARS-CoV-2. What would this similarity — and its limits — tell you about how specific "long COVID" is as a diagnostic category, versus being one trigger among several for a more general post-infectious syndrome?
2. Brain fog can now be linked to a measurable finding (elevated AMPA receptor density on PET imaging) in a small study of 30 patients. What further evidence would you want before treating that finding as a reliable diagnostic biomarker rather than an interesting but preliminary correlation?
3. The vagus nerve hypothesis proposes one nerve as a common thread linking cardiovascular, gut, endocrine, and cognitive symptoms. If true, what would you expect to see in patients whose vagus nerve is damaged for an unrelated reason (e.g., after certain abdominal surgeries) — and does that give researchers a way to test the hypothesis independently of long COVID itself?
