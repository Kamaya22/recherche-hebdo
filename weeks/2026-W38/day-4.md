# Long COVID — Day 4/7: Why there is still no diagnostic test for long COVID

## Context

Days 2 and 3 built up a detailed mechanistic picture of long COVID: viral persistence, autoimmunity, endothelial/microclot dysfunction, and — downstream of those — neuroinflammation and autonomic/vagal damage. Each of those hypotheses came with real, peer-reviewed supporting evidence: tissue biopsies, autoantibody assays, coagulation markers, PET imaging, standardized autonomic testing. A natural question after two days of mechanisms is: if researchers can point to so many measurable biological abnormalities, why does a patient still have to be told "we can't confirm this with a test"? Today's readings answer that question directly, and the answer is less about a lack of biological signal than about turning a *population-level* signal — true on average, across a research cohort — into a *test that works for the individual patient sitting in a clinic*.

This distinction matters because it is the crux of why long COVID remains, in 2026, a diagnosis of exclusion: clinicians rule out other explanations for a patient's symptoms and diagnose long COVID by what is left over, rather than confirming it directly with a blood test, scan, or biopsy. Today's central reading is the largest and most direct test yet of whether *routine* clinical laboratory measurements — the ordinary blood panels a doctor already orders — could close that gap. Spoiler: they largely cannot, and the accompanying editorial explains why that negative result is itself an important, clarifying finding rather than a dead end. We then broaden the lens with a systematic review of the much larger universe of *research-grade* candidate biomarkers that have been proposed, to see where the more promising leads actually sit. Day 5 will pick up the practical and social consequences of this diagnostic gap — including its collision with the decades-old ME/CFS "is this real" debate — and Day 6 will show how trial designers are trying to work around the absence of a test when selecting and stratifying patients for treatment studies.

## Today's readings (~21 min)

1. **Differentiation of Prior SARS-CoV-2 Infection and Postacute Sequelae by Standard Clinical Laboratory Measurements in the RECOVER Cohort** (Erlandson et al., for the RECOVER-Adult Consortium, *Annals of Internal Medicine*, 2024) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11408082/ — ~10 min (abstract, Results, and Discussion). *Annals of Internal Medicine* is the flagship peer-reviewed journal of the American College of Physicians; this is a large NIH-funded cohort study (10,094 participants across 83 US sites) from the RECOVER initiative, freely available via PubMed Central under the NIH public-access policy. It directly tested whether any of 25 routine clinical laboratory values — the kind of complete blood count, metabolic panel, and inflammatory markers already ordered in everyday care — could distinguish people with long COVID from those without.

2. **Long COVID Diagnostics: An Unconquered Challenge** (Antar & Auwaerter, *Annals of Internal Medicine*, 2024) — https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/M24-0892 — ~5 min. This is the editorial commissioned by the same journal issue to accompany the RECOVER laboratory study above, written by two Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine infectious-disease physicians. (Note: unlike the RECOVER study itself, this editorial's open-access status could not be independently confirmed at the time of writing — check for a paywall.) It puts the negative result in clinical and historical context, explaining why the absence of a diagnostic signal in routine bloodwork does not mean long COVID lacks a biological basis.

3. **Blood Biomarkers of Long COVID: A Systematic Review** (Thomas, Faghy, Chidley et al., *Molecular Diagnosis & Therapy*, 2024) — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39103645/ — ~6 min (abstract and the three-category summary of findings). *Molecular Diagnosis & Therapy* is a peer-reviewed journal in Springer Nature's Adis portfolio, indexed in PubMed/MEDLINE; this PROSPERO-registered systematic review followed PRISMA methodology across the Cochrane, Embase, PubMed and Web of Science databases. Rather than routine labs, it catalogues the much larger universe of *research-grade* candidate biomarkers proposed across 46 studies and over 4,400 participants.

## Guided summary

The RECOVER-Adult study is, to date, the most rigorous test of a very practical question: could the tests a doctor already has on hand — a basic metabolic panel, a complete blood count, inflammatory markers like CRP — be repurposed to diagnose long COVID? The study compared 25 such routine laboratory values across four groups drawn from over 10,000 participants: people with no history of SARS-CoV-2 infection, people who had been infected but had no long-COVID symptoms (a "PASC index" of zero), people who met a research definition of long COVID (PASC index of 12 or higher), and an intermediate group. Using propensity-score-weighted models to control for the many other things that differ between these groups, the authors did find some statistically significant differences: people with prior infection tended to have slightly lower platelet counts, slightly higher blood glucose, and subtle signs of reduced kidney function compared with the uninfected group. But — and this is the key finding — none of the 25 measures reliably distinguished people with long COVID from people who had recovered fully after the same infection. The differences that existed were small, overlapped heavily between groups, and were not consistent enough to be useful for an individual diagnosis. In plain terms: if you handed a clinician one patient's routine labs with the SARS-CoV-2 history removed, they could not reliably tell from those 25 numbers alone whether that patient had long COVID.

The Antar and Auwaerter editorial frames why this negative result is genuinely informative rather than simply disappointing. First, it rules out a comparatively easy and appealing possibility — that long COVID might quietly show up in tests already ordered for other reasons — which means clinicians cannot lean on routine bloodwork to either confirm or dismiss a patient's reported symptoms, and should not interpret "normal labs" as evidence that a patient's symptoms are not real. Second, the editorial connects the finding back to the mechanistic diversity from Days 2 and 3: if long COVID really is an umbrella covering several distinct biological subtypes (viral persistence in some patients, autoimmunity in others, microclotting or autonomic/vagal damage in others still), then averaging routine lab values across a large, mechanistically mixed group of "long COVID" patients could dilute or cancel out a signal that might be detectable within a correctly identified subtype. That is a specific, testable explanation for why an unselected cohort study did not find a diagnostic marker even though Days 2–3 covered real, replicated abnormalities (autoantibodies, D-dimer elevations, PET-detected AMPA receptor changes) in more narrowly defined patient groups. Third, the editorial is explicit that a negative result for *routine* labs does not mean no biomarker is possible — it means the search has to move to more specialized, mechanism-targeted tests, and probably to combinations of markers rather than any single one.

That is exactly the terrain the Thomas, Faghy and Chidley systematic review maps out. Looking beyond routine bloodwork to the wider research literature, the review identified 525 distinct blood biomarkers proposed across 45 observational studies and one interventional study, spanning 4,415 participants — and grouped them into three broad categories that will be familiar from Day 2: markers of immunological and inflammatory dysfunction (the largest category, including cytokines and autoantibody-related measures), markers of endothelial and vascular dysfunction (D-dimer, fibrinogen, and related clotting/vascular-injury markers), and markers of metabolic and clotting abnormalities. The sheer number — 525 candidate markers, and no single one validated — is itself a data point: it indicates an active, productive research field generating plausible leads, but one that has not yet converged on a small panel with proven sensitivity and specificity for clinical use. The review's authors point to a recurring methodological obstacle behind that lack of convergence: studies differ in which version of the long COVID case definition they use, which comparison group they use (healthy uninfected controls versus infected-but-recovered controls make very different comparisons), and how long after infection they sample — differences that make it hard to know whether an inconsistent finding across studies reflects a real absence of effect or simply incomparable study designs. Their recommendation is for future work to report comparator groups more clearly and to track how the same biomarkers change from the acute infection through the chronic phase in the same patients, rather than only comparing biomarker snapshots across different groups of people at a single time point.

Put together, today's two studies describe two different tiers of the same diagnostic search. Tier one — the tests already used in everyday medicine — has now been rigorously tried at scale and, for now, has failed to produce a usable diagnostic signal; that negative finding usefully closes off a shortcut clinicians might otherwise have hoped for. Tier two — specialized, mechanism-targeted biomarkers coming out of research labs — has generated hundreds of candidate leads with real biological plausibility, but not yet the kind of large-scale, prospectively validated, subtype-aware study needed to turn any of them into an approved clinical test. The practical upshot for patients today is stark: a "normal" set of standard blood tests neither confirms nor rules out long COVID, which is precisely why the WHO/CDC clinical definitions covered on Day 1 remain the operative standard, and why diagnosis continues to proceed by excluding alternative explanations rather than by a positive biological confirmation. Day 5 will pick this thread up directly, since a diagnosis made by exclusion — with no test to point to — is also the diagnosis most vulnerable to being doubted, dismissed, or reframed as psychological, a dynamic that has a long and instructive precedent in ME/CFS research history.

## Questions to think about

1. The RECOVER study found statistically significant but clinically small differences in some routine lab values (platelets, glucose, kidney markers) between infected and uninfected groups, yet concluded these were not useful for diagnosing long COVID. What is the difference between a finding being "statistically significant" and being "clinically useful," and why might a large study (10,000+ participants) make it easier to detect the first without necessarily achieving the second?
2. The editorial suggests that averaging lab values across a mechanistically diverse group of long COVID patients could hide a real signal that exists only within a specific biological subtype (e.g., the microclotting subtype from Day 2). How would you design a follow-up study to test that explanation, given that there is currently no validated way to sort patients into subtypes in the first place?
3. The systematic review found 525 candidate biomarkers but no validated diagnostic panel. If you were prioritizing which of those 525 candidates to study next with limited research funding, what criteria would you use — and how would "already has a plausible mechanism" versus "already has an available, cheap clinical assay" pull in different directions?
