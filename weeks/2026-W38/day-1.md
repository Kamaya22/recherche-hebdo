# Long COVID — Day 1/7: What is long COVID, and why is it still so hard to pin down?

## Context

More than five years after SARS-CoV-2 began circulating widely, a large share of people who were infected are still dealing with the aftermath. Long COVID — officially "post COVID-19 condition" — is not a single disease but an umbrella term for a constellation of symptoms that persist or appear after the acute infection has cleared. It has become one of the largest unresolved problems in contemporary medicine, both because of its scale (tens of millions of affected people worldwide) and because, unusually for a condition this common, there is still no validated diagnostic test, no single accepted biological mechanism, and no approved treatment specifically for it.

This week we will build up a picture of long COVID from the ground up: today's panorama covers what the condition is, how it is defined, how common it is, and why defining and counting it has proven so difficult. Days 2 and 3 will dig into the competing biological mechanisms under investigation. Day 4 looks at the diagnostic challenge in more depth. Day 5 covers the scientific and social controversies — including the fraught history long COVID shares with ME/CFS research. Day 6 turns to what is actually being tested in clinical trials, and to the societal stakes (disability, healthcare systems, workplaces). Day 7 ties it together.

A note on why "definition" matters so much here: unlike, say, a fracture visible on an X-ray, long COVID is currently defined **clinically** — by a pattern of symptoms and a time course — rather than by a biological test. That single fact shapes almost everything else this week: how prevalence is measured, why estimates vary so widely between studies, why clinical trials struggle to enroll comparable patients, and why the science has become entangled with debates about legitimacy and belief. Keep it in mind as a throughline for the week.

## Today's readings (~20 min)

1. **Post COVID-19 condition (long COVID)** (World Health Organization, fact sheet, updated 2023) — https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/post-covid-19-condition-(long-covid) — ~5 min. The WHO's official fact sheet gives the clinical case definition reached by international Delphi consensus in 2021 (symptoms starting within 3 months of probable or confirmed infection, lasting at least 2 months, not explained by another diagnosis), plus headline figures on frequency and the most commonly reported symptoms. As the UN's public-health agency and the body that coordinated the consensus definition process, WHO is the primary authoritative source for what counts as "long COVID" at all.

2. **Long COVID: major findings, mechanisms and recommendations** (Davis, McCorkell, Vogel & Topol, *Nature Reviews Microbiology*, 2023) — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41579-022-00846-2 (freely readable via PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9839201/) — ~10 min (read the introduction and "Epidemiology" sections). This is the most widely cited peer-reviewed review of long COVID research to date, produced by a team that includes patient-researchers from the Patient-Led Research Collaborative alongside academic co-authors. It surveys prevalence estimates, the more-than-200 reported symptoms across organ systems, and frames the scale of the problem — useful as our anchor reference for the rest of the week, since later days will return to its mechanism sections.

3. **Long COVID Basics** (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, ongoing guidance page) — https://www.cdc.gov/long-covid/about/index.html — ~5 min. The CDC's plain-language overview complements the WHO/Nature sources with the US surveillance perspective — notably the CDC Household Pulse Survey, an ongoing population survey that has tracked long COVID prevalence in US adults since 2022 and is one of the few data sources allowing trends over time to be monitored.

## Guided summary

Long COVID is the general name for symptoms that persist, recur, or newly appear after a SARS-CoV-2 infection, beyond the timeframe expected for acute COVID-19 to resolve. The WHO's 2021 Delphi-consensus clinical case definition — reached by international expert agreement rather than derived from a biological test — describes it as occurring in people with a history of probable or confirmed infection, "usually 3 months from the onset," with symptoms lasting at least 2 months that cannot be explained by an alternative diagnosis. The CDC uses a related but not identical threshold (symptoms or conditions present at least 3 months after infection), and a separate WHO case definition exists for children and adolescents. This lack of a single, universally used cutoff is not a minor technicality: it is one of the reasons prevalence estimates for long COVID vary so much across studies, sometimes by a factor of two or three, even before methodological differences (self-report vs. clinical diagnosis, which symptoms are counted, how long people are followed) are taken into account.

On scale, the 2023 *Nature Reviews Microbiology* synthesis by Davis, McCorkell, Vogel and Topol estimated that long COVID occurs in at least 10% of SARS-CoV-2 infections and that at least 65 million people worldwide had it as of that review — a number the authors note was rising as the pandemic continued, since incidence accumulates with every reinfection. More recent US-specific data from the CDC's Household Pulse Survey, current through 2024, estimated around 5–6% of US adults had long COVID at any given time (roughly 17 million people), and that among the majority of US adults who have had COVID at some point, close to 3 in 10 report having experienced long COVID at some point and roughly 1 in 10 have it currently. A notable and somewhat sobering finding from that survey series is that these rates have stayed fairly stable over the past year or two, rather than declining — consistent with long COVID behaving, for a meaningful fraction of patients, as a chronic rather than self-resolving condition.

What makes long COVID unusual among common chronic conditions is the sheer symptom diversity: the Davis et al. review counts more than 200 distinct symptoms reported across nearly every organ system — cardiovascular, respiratory, gastrointestinal, endocrine, and, especially prominently, neurological and "systemic" symptoms. In practice, a smaller cluster of symptoms dominates most patient and survey reports: persistent fatigue, cognitive difficulties often described as "brain fog" (problems with memory, concentration, and word-finding), and post-exertional malaise (PEM) — a delayed, disproportionate worsening of symptoms, typically 12 to 72 hours after physical, cognitive, or emotional exertion. PEM is not simply tiredness after activity; it is increasingly treated by researchers as a distinctive biological signature, because it does not track with normal deconditioning and because objective markers (such as abnormal responses on repeated cardiopulmonary exercise testing) have been documented in some patients. This symptom, alongside profound fatigue, is also the clearest point of overlap with myalgia encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) — a connection we will return to on Day 3 (mechanisms) and Day 5 (controversies), since ME/CFS research has decades of prior — often contentious — history that long COVID research is now, to a significant extent, inheriting.

Two things are worth holding onto going into the rest of the week. First, "long COVID" almost certainly is not one disease with one cause: it is very likely an umbrella covering several distinct (if sometimes overlapping) biological processes triggered by the same initial event. Second, because the current definition is purely clinical — a symptom pattern and a time course, with no confirmatory test — every downstream challenge this week will trace back to that fact: it is why mechanism research (Days 2–3) has to work by testing several hypotheses in parallel rather than starting from a known cause; why diagnosis (Day 4) still relies on excluding other explanations rather than confirming long COVID directly; why controversies (Day 5) partly reduce to disagreements about what "counts"; and why clinical trials (Day 6) have struggled with patient heterogeneity — treatments that work for one biological subtype may show no effect when tested across an unselected group of "long COVID" patients who may not share the same underlying mechanism.

## Questions to think about

1. If long COVID prevalence estimates range from roughly 5% to 30%+ depending on which population and definition is used, what would you want to know about a study before trusting a specific number it reports?
2. Long COVID is currently defined by symptoms and time course rather than a biological test. What are the practical consequences of that — for patients seeking a diagnosis, for researchers designing studies, and for policymakers deciding how to allocate healthcare resources?
3. Post-exertional malaise is a hallmark symptom shared with ME/CFS. Why might a "delayed, disproportionate crash after exertion" be a particularly useful clue for narrowing down biological mechanisms, compared to a more generic symptom like fatigue?

## This week's theme

**Long COVID — the biology of post-viral illness, diagnostic challenges, and what treatments are being tested.**

Five years after the acute phase of the COVID-19 pandemic subsided in most countries, long COVID remains one of the largest unresolved problems in modern medicine: tens of millions of people worldwide report symptoms — fatigue, cognitive impairment, post-exertional malaise, cardiovascular and autonomic dysfunction — that persist for months or years after the initial infection. It is also a uniquely instructive case study in how biomedicine handles a novel, contested, multi-system illness in real time: competing mechanistic hypotheses are being tested simultaneously, no validated biomarker exists yet, and the condition has reopened long-standing tensions — familiar from ME/CFS research — between biomedical and psychosomatic framings of post-viral illness. Understanding where the science is solid, where it is contested, and what is actually being tested in clinical trials is a template for how we study, diagnose, and eventually treat post-infectious chronic illness more broadly.
