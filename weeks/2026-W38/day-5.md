# Long COVID — Day 5/7: Controversies, definitional divides, and what ME/CFS history teaches (and warns)

## Context

Day 4 ended on a pointed observation: because no blood test, scan, or biopsy can confirm long COVID, it remains a diagnosis of exclusion — and a diagnosis made by ruling things out, rather than by positive confirmation, is also the diagnosis most vulnerable to being doubted, minimized, or reframed as "just" psychological. Today we look at that vulnerability directly. Long COVID did not arrive into a scientific vacuum: it arrived into a decades-old, still-unresolved argument about how medicine should handle multi-system illnesses that present with real disability but no agreed biological marker. Myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) — flagged as a close cousin of long COVID since Day 1, and shown on Day 3 to share both symptoms (fatigue, post-exertional malaise) and measurable autonomic abnormalities — is the clearest precedent, and long COVID researchers have inherited both its hard-won lessons and its unresolved fights.

Three threads run through today's material. First, a genuine scientific and clinical controversy: how much of long COVID's persistence is explained by ongoing biological processes (Days 2–3's viral, immune, vascular, and neuro-autonomic mechanisms) versus psychological factors such as depression, anxiety, or illness beliefs — and, separately, the much narrower and more contested claim that psychological factors are the primary *cause* of the illness (a claim most researchers now reject, but whose institutional legacy is still being unwound). Second, a bureaucratic complication that compounds the first: WHO, the US CDC, and the UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) do not use the same case definition, which makes it harder to compare studies, harder to know whether a "long COVID" cohort in one country is comparable to one in another, and harder to settle the biomedical-versus-psychosomatic debate with a single body of evidence. Third, a set of concrete methodological lessons — about case-definition rigor, patient subgrouping, and the risk of diluting a real biological signal — that ME/CFS researchers learned the hard way over four decades, and that a 2023 review argues long COVID researchers should not have to relearn from scratch. Day 6 will pick up where this leaves clinical trial design and treatment; today is about understanding why the science and the politics of diagnosis became so entangled in the first place.

## Today's readings (~21 min)

1. **The Updated NICE Guidance Exposed the Serious Flaws in CBT and Graded Exercise Therapy Trials for ME/CFS** (Vink & Vink-Niese, *Healthcare*, 2022) — https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9032/10/5/898 (DOI: 10.3390/healthcare10050898) — ~8 min (abstract, and the sections on trial design flaws and the 2021 NICE reversal). *Healthcare* is a peer-reviewed, fully open-access journal published by MDPI (Basel, Switzerland). The authors — independent ME/CFS researchers unaffiliated with the trials they critique — dissect why the UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), after reviewing the evidence base underlying cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) and graded exercise therapy (GET) as ME/CFS treatments, reversed its own prior guidance in 2021 and concluded GET should not be offered and CBT should be offered only as supportive management, not as a cure. This is the single clearest case study of the biomedical-versus-psychosomatic dispute actually changing an official clinical guideline.

2. **What Long COVID investigators can learn from four decades of ME/CFS research** (Jason, Natelson, Bonilla et al., *Brain, Behavior, and Immunity — Integrative*, 2023) — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2949834123000211 (vol. 4, article 100022) — ~7 min (introduction and the case-definition and subgrouping sections). *Brain, Behavior, and Immunity — Integrative* is a peer-reviewed, fully open-access companion journal to *Brain, Behavior, and Immunity*, published by Elsevier on behalf of the Psychoneuroimmunology Research Society (PNIRS). Written by a team including Leonard Jason (DePaul University) and Benjamin Natelson (Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai), both veteran ME/CFS researchers, it distills what forty years of ME/CFS case-definition disputes and biomarker searches can teach long COVID researchers trying to avoid repeating the same missteps.

3. **Psychological factors associated with Long COVID: a systematic review and meta-analysis** (Engelmann, Reinke, Stein, Salzmann, Löwe, Toussaint & Shedden-Mora, *eClinicalMedicine*, 2024) — https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2024.102756 — ~6 min (abstract, Results, and the interpretation of causality in the Discussion). *eClinicalMedicine* is a fully open-access clinical journal in The Lancet Discovery Science family (Elsevier). This systematic review and meta-analysis — 113 studies, over 312,000 participants — is the most comprehensive current synthesis of how psychological variables actually relate to long COVID, and offers a useful, evidence-based counterweight to both ends of the "is it psychological or not" argument.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

The historical background matters before the details do. Long before COVID-19 existed, ME/CFS had already become the textbook example of a "contested illness": a condition with severe, well-documented disability but no confirmatory biomarker, in which the dominant clinical model for much of the 1990s–2010s treated deconditioning and "abnormal illness beliefs" as central to the illness, and treated CBT and graded exercise as logical treatments — the idea being that gradually increasing activity would reverse a vicious cycle of fear-avoidance and physical unfitness. The largest test of that model was the UK's PACE trial (2011), a government-funded study that reported CBT and GET as moderately effective. That result shaped clinical guidance for a decade. But patient organizations and independent researchers argued for years that the trial had serious methodological problems — including a definition of "recovery" that was loosened after the data were collected, non-blinded designs relying on subjective outcomes, and selective reporting. The Vink & Vink-Niese review lays out these criticisms systematically and connects them to NICE's own 2021 re-evaluation: after an extensive independent review of the evidence, NICE concluded that GET should not be offered to ME/CFS patients at all (some patients report the fixed, incremental activity increases it prescribes triggering post-exertional malaise crashes) and that CBT should be repositioned as supportive symptom management rather than a treatment aimed at cure. This was, in effect, an official acknowledgment that a biomedical illness had been treated, for a decade, using tools built on a largely psychosomatic premise — and it is the single most concrete precedent long COVID researchers and clinicians look to when warning against repeating the same mistake.

That history is also why definitional precision has become such a flashpoint. WHO's Delphi-consensus definition (Day 1) sets long COVID as symptoms starting within roughly three months of infection and lasting at least two months; the CDC uses a related but not identical threshold and explicitly allows for asymptomatic or unrecognized initial infections; NICE, in its COVID-19 rapid guideline (NG188), defines "post-COVID-19 syndrome" instead as symptoms persisting 12 weeks or longer, without listing a fixed symptom list, describing them instead as often multisystemic and heterogeneous "clusters." None of these differences is arbitrary or academic: a 12-week NICE threshold captures a different, generally more chronic population than a WHO threshold anchored at roughly two months past a three-month onset window, and "clusters of symptoms" language leaves more room for clinical judgment (and, historically, for a clinician's own priors about how much of the presentation is psychological) than a WHO checklist does. Cross-national studies and treatment guidelines that use different definitions are, to a real extent, not describing exactly the same patient population, which is part of why prevalence estimates and trial results (Day 6 will show this concretely) have been so hard to compare across countries.

The Jason, Natelson et al. review turns this into a set of concrete, actionable lessons rather than just a cautionary tale. Their central argument is that ME/CFS research spent decades hampered by using multiple, overlapping case definitions (some requiring six months of fatigue with almost no other criteria, others requiring the specific, more restrictive combination of post-exertional malaise plus cognitive and autonomic symptoms) that captured meaningfully different patient groups under the same name — including, in some looser definitions, patients whose primary problem was actually major depression rather than a post-infectious illness. When a research cohort mixes several distinct populations under one label, biological signals that are real within a narrower, more homogeneous subgroup get diluted or washed out when averaged across the whole mixed group — precisely the mechanism Day 4's editorial invoked to explain why routine blood tests failed to distinguish long COVID patients as a whole from recovered controls. Jason and colleagues argue long COVID research is at risk of repeating this exact error at a larger scale, given how heterogeneous "long COVID" symptom presentations are, and recommend prioritizing tightly defined subgroups (anchored on objective features like documented post-exertional malaise or autonomic test abnormalities, discussed on Day 3) over broad, inclusive definitions when searching for mechanisms or biomarkers — even though broad definitions remain useful for public-health surveillance and access to care.

The Engelmann et al. meta-analysis brings the debate to its current, most defensible scientific position. Pooling 113 studies and over 312,000 participants, the authors found that both depression (odds ratio 2.35) and anxiety (odds ratio 2.53) were significantly more common in people with long COVID than in comparison groups — a real, replicated, and fairly large association. Critically, though, the authors are explicit that an elevated association is not evidence that psychological distress is the underlying cause of long COVID's physical symptoms; the same association is consistent with several other explanations that are at least as plausible given Days 2–3's findings — that chronic inflammation and neuroinflammation from the biological mechanisms already covered this week could independently drive both mood symptoms and physical symptoms from a shared cause; that living with a severe, disabling, poorly understood, and often disbelieved chronic illness would predictably cause depression and anxiety as a consequence rather than a cause; or some combination of both operating simultaneously and reinforcing each other. Their own conclusion is that psychological factors "may contribute to symptom persistence alongside pathophysiological mechanisms" — a genuinely intermediate position that neither dismisses a psychological dimension nor treats it as the primary explanation the way the pre-2021 ME/CFS model did.

Put together, today's three readings show a field trying, in real time, to avoid a mistake it can already see clearly in its own recent past. The 2021 NICE reversal shows what happens when a psychosomatic-leaning model becomes official guidance without sufficiently rigorous supporting evidence, and how difficult and slow it is to walk that back once entrenched — an instructive and cautionary tale, not a hypothetical one. The persistence of different case definitions across WHO, CDC, and NICE shows that this is not only a scientific dispute but also an institutional and administrative one, since guidelines embed assumptions about mechanism (a 12-week symptom-cluster definition invites more clinical judgment, and historically more room for psychosomatic framing, than a WHO checklist tied to a specific viral exposure). And the current best evidence — real, moderate psychological associations that plausibly run in multiple causal directions at once — is a deliberately unsatisfying middle position, precisely because a fully satisfying one is not yet supported by the evidence available.

## Questions to think about

1. The Vink & Vink-Niese review argues the PACE trial's flaws (loosened recovery criteria after the fact, non-blinded subjective outcomes) mattered enough to eventually reverse a decade of clinical guidance. What features would you want a treatment trial for a subjective, symptom-based condition like long COVID to have from the start, to avoid a similar decade-long detour?
2. Jason and colleagues warn that overly broad ME/CFS case definitions diluted real biological signals by mixing distinct patient subgroups together. If you were designing a long COVID biomarker study today, how would you decide where to draw the line between a definition narrow enough to find a real subtype-specific signal and one so narrow it excludes patients who genuinely have the condition?
3. The Engelmann et al. meta-analysis found a real statistical association between long COVID and depression/anxiety without being able to establish which way the causal arrow (or arrows) point. What kind of study design — beyond a cross-sectional comparison — could help distinguish "chronic illness causes distress," "shared biology causes both," and "distress worsens physical symptoms" from one another?
