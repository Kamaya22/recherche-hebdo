# The psychology of conspiracy beliefs — Day 7/7: Synthesis — what the evidence supports, what remains open, and where to go next

## Context

This week moved from panorama to mechanism to evidence to controversy to method, and each day complicated the picture the previous day had left looking tidier than it should have. Monday framed conspiracy belief as consequential, universal, emotional, and social — a graded trait most people hold to some degree rather than a rare pathology. Tuesday located a largely universal cognitive substrate underneath that framing (pattern perception and agency detection, both demonstrably manipulable in controlled experiments), with dispositional traits like need for cognitive closure playing a real but modest role that depends on how much a person already trusts institutions. Wednesday added the social activators — group identity and "uniqueness" motives, economic precarity, and alienation-driven compensation for lost meaning — that plausibly explain why that universal substrate produces more belief in some people, places, and moments than others.

Thursday and Friday then supplied the correction the first three days did not: real-world consequences turned out to be genuine but far more modest and domain-specific than public discourse assumes (a small causal effect on vaccination intention, a statistically common but individually weak association with violence-justifying attitudes, and — most strikingly — a democratic-attitudes-to-conspiracy-belief pathway that ran backwards in at least one national sample). Friday then turned the same scrutiny on Wednesday's own causal story: two independent 2026 studies using designs built to test direction found no evidence that precarity causes later conspiracy belief, one of them finding the opposite pattern, while a separate measurement study found that self-reported "agreement" with a conspiracy claim usually does mean something like firm conviction rather than tentative hedging — reassuring for the field's instruments, but a reminder that the "irrational or reasonable" question depends on which specific claim is being judged. Saturday closed on response: prevention (technique-based inoculation) reliably outperforms correction (after-the-fact debunking), but the average effect size is modest (Hedges' g ≈ 0.16–0.3) and the one lab-to-field generalization success (Roozenbeek et al.'s YouTube trial) sits next to a documented failure to replicate a similar intervention on Indian-language headlines.

Today does not add a new mechanism or a new dispute; it asks what all of that adds up to. The two readings below were chosen because each looks across the whole field rather than at one slice of it: one is the largest quantitative synthesis yet of *why* people believe, pooling the cognitive, personality, and social-identity threads Tuesday and Wednesday introduced separately into a single set of numbers; the other is a review that explicitly organizes the field into the individual, intergroup, and national levels of analysis this week's outline followed, and closes by naming where the evidence is thinnest — which turns out to line up closely with the blind spot this synthesis returns to below.

## Today's readings (~19 min)

**Reasons to believe: A systematic review and meta-analytic synthesis of the motives associated with conspiracy beliefs** (Mikey Biddlestone, Ricky Green, Karen M. Douglas, Flávio Azevedo, Robbie M. Sutton & Aleksandra Cichocka, *Psychological Bulletin*, 2025) — https://kar.kent.ac.uk/107924/3/Main%20text_CLEAN.pdf — ~10 min (the introduction and general discussion give the headline pattern; the effect-size tables by individual motive can be skimmed). A meta-analysis of 971 effect sizes from 279 independent studies and more than 137,000 participants — by a wide margin the largest quantitative synthesis this literature has produced — testing epistemic, existential, and social motives for conspiracy belief side by side rather than one at a time. *Psychological Bulletin* is the American Psychological Association's flagship peer-reviewed synthesis journal; Douglas and Sutton co-authored two of this week's earlier readings (Monday and Wednesday), and this is the authors' accepted manuscript, self-archived via the University of Kent's institutional repository — the same archiving route already used and verified for this author group's other 2025-2026 papers this week.

**Individual, intergroup and nation-level influences on belief in conspiracy theories** (Matthew J. Hornsey, Kinga Bierwiaczonek, Kai Sassenberg & Karen M. Douglas, *Nature Reviews Psychology*, 2023) — https://kar.kent.ac.uk/98282/1/Hornsey_v3.pdf — ~9 min (read the framing of the three levels and the concluding discussion of gaps; the individual- and intergroup-level sections cover ground this week's Tuesday and Wednesday readings already established). A commissioned review that organizes the entire field into three levels of analysis — individual (cognitive, clinical, personality, developmental), intergroup (societal fault lines), and national (economic, political, cultural, historical context) — the same three-level structure this week's outline followed without naming it. *Nature Reviews Psychology* is a peer-reviewed invited-review journal, Nature Portfolio; also freely available as the authors' accepted manuscript via the University of Kent's repository.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

**What is genuinely settled.** Two things held up across every day this week without serious challenge. First, the cognitive substrate: Tuesday's evidence that pattern perception and agency detection causally shape conspiratorial and paranormal belief rests on experimental manipulation, not just correlation (van Prooijen, Douglas & De Inocencio's Study 5, where inducing belief in one conspiracy theory increased subsequent pattern-seeing) — about as close to causal proof as this field gets, and Biddlestone et al.'s meta-analysis confirms the pattern holds at scale: all three motive classes they tested were significant, with the single strongest correlates being intuitive (rather than deliberative) thinking style and a tendency toward odd beliefs and experiences, echoing Tuesday's mechanism almost exactly. Second, the social activation of that substrate: group-identity and status motives (Wednesday), and now Biddlestone et al.'s finding that "being antagonistic and acting superior" is among the strongest correlates of conspiratorial ideation, converge on the same picture — conspiracy belief is not one thing but is reliably tied to feeling threatened, distrustful, and status-anxious, regardless of which specific study or method is used to measure it.

**What is still open, causally.** The open question is not "are these things correlated" but "which way, if any, does the arrow run." This is where the week's most genuine surprises live: Thursday found that authoritarian attitudes predicted later conspiracy belief in New Zealand, not the reverse; Friday found two independent 2026 panel-and-experiment studies disagreeing about whether economic precarity causes conspiracy belief, one of them suggesting the arrow runs backwards. Hornsey et al.'s review, published in 2023 — before either of Friday's 2026 studies existed — already flagged this as an open seam by treating "national-level" economic and political context as a distinct, under-tested level of analysis rather than a settled input to the individual-level story. Three years on, the field has produced sharper tools to test that seam (cross-lagged panels, preregistered experiments) but not yet a stable answer.

**What is still open, geographically and methodologically.** A pattern this synthesis had not named directly before now: nearly every study read this week drew on samples from the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, Poland, or another Western European country. Hornsey et al.'s "national-level" framing exists precisely because conspiracy belief's predictors and consequences plausibly depend on context that varies by country — institutional quality, media environment, history of actual conspiracies by the state — yet the evidence base to test that dependence properly, across a genuinely global set of countries, does not yet exist at the scale the rest of the field's claims would need to generalize confidently. Saturday's reading already surfaced one concrete instance of this gap failing a direct test: an inoculation-game intervention that worked in Western samples showed no improvement in discriminating true from false headlines when re-tested on Indian-language content. Friday's measurement finding (conviction and plausibility judgments correlate at 0.82-0.92, not 1.0) is a smaller-scale version of the same caution: even the field's basic instruments have only been validated in the same narrow set of countries and languages where nearly everything else in this week's readings was tested.

**Three big open questions**, distilled from the week: (1) Does economic precarity, institutional distrust, or neither causally drive conspiracy belief — and what design would finally distinguish "half the model was tested" objections from a genuine, decisive null? (2) If the cognitive substrate (pattern perception, agency detection) is close to universal, as Tuesday's evidence suggests, why does measured conspiracy belief and its national-level predictors vary as much as Hornsey et al.'s review implies across countries that have barely been sampled — and would that variation hold up once non-Western, non-WEIRD populations are studied as thoroughly as the US and Germany have been? (3) Given that prevention (inoculation) reliably outperforms correction (debunking) in the studies read so far, but the size of that advantage is modest and its generalization outside Western, English- and Dutch-language contexts has already failed once (the Indian replication), how much of "prebunking works" is a general finding about human cognition versus a finding about specific platforms, languages, and cultural repertoires of manipulation tactics?

**Where to go deeper.** For the cognitive and evolutionary mechanisms, van Prooijen & van Vugt's evolved-functions review (Tuesday) remains the clearest single entry point. For the social and political drivers, Robertson et al.'s social-identity review and the unresolved precarity dispute (Wednesday and Friday, read together) are the most direct statements of what is agreed and what is not. For consequences, Taubert et al.'s vaccine-hesitancy scoping review (Thursday) is the most methodologically careful of the three consequence domains covered. For interventions, Stasielowicz's 2026 meta-analysis (Saturday) is the single most current quantitative answer to "what works." And for a map of the field as a whole, plus its own account of where the evidence is thinnest, today's two readings — Biddlestone et al. on motives and Hornsey et al. on levels of analysis — are the most useful starting points for anyone who wants to go beyond this week's readings.

## Questions to think about

1. Biddlestone et al.'s meta-analysis found that antagonism and feelings of superiority are among the strongest correlates of conspiratorial ideation. Does that sit comfortably with Monday's reframing of conspiracy belief as an ordinary, near-universal trait rather than a marker of a flawed or hostile personality — or does it complicate that reframing?
2. This week's readings were drawn overwhelmingly from US, UK, and continental European samples, following the field's own published concentration. If a genuinely representative, worldwide research base existed, which of this week's "settled" findings do you expect would hold up, and which do you expect would turn out to be an artifact of where the research happened to be conducted?
3. Having read all seven days, would you now describe the psychology of conspiracy belief as a field with a stable core (the cognitive substrate) and a genuinely contested periphery (precarity, causal direction, generalizability) — or does Friday and today's reading suggest even the "core" claims are more provisional than Monday's framing implied?
