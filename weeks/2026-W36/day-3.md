# The psychology of conspiracy beliefs — Day 3/7: Social and political drivers

## Context

Monday's panorama framed conspiracy belief as universal, emotionally driven, and inherently social. Tuesday drilled into the "emotion and cognition" half of that picture: pattern perception and agency detection as the largely universal cognitive substrate, with dispositional traits like need for cognitive closure playing a real but modest role that depends heavily on how much a person already trusts the relevant institutions. That last point is the bridge to today.

If cognitive substrate is close to universal — present in nearly everyone — then it cannot, by itself, explain why conspiracy belief is higher in some people, places, and moments than others. Something has to activate it. Today looks at the three social and political conditions the literature treats as the main activators: institutional distrust (often channelled through economic precarity), group identity and intergroup dynamics, and feelings of powerlessness or alienation from society. It also circles back to Monday's finding that interpersonal ties, more than social-media exposure, predict conspiracy belief — today's readings help explain *why* trusted networks would be the channel through which these social and political pressures actually spread.

A caveat worth holding onto going in: unlike Tuesday's readings, where an experimental design (manipulating pattern perception) supported a causal story, several of today's claims rest on correlational or contested causal evidence. One of today's three readings has already triggered a direct empirical challenge from other researchers — a preview of the "controversies and open debates" territory Friday will cover in full.

## Today's readings (~20 min)

**How Social Identity Shapes Conspiratorial Belief** (Claire E. Robertson, Clara Pretus, Steve Rathje & Jay J. Van Bavel, *Current Opinion in Psychology*, 2022) — https://repositori.upf.edu/bitstream/10230/55497/1/robertson-cop-hows.pdf — ~6 min. A short expert review (the journal's format is commissioned reviews by leading researchers) arguing that conspiracy belief is often driven by social-identity needs — belonging, feeling good about one's group, feeling secure in the group's status — that are distinct from purely epistemic motives like wanting a coherent explanation. Freely available via Universitat Pompeu Fabra's institutional repository, which lets us confirm this is the authors' own archived copy of the published review rather than a summary.

**Of Precarity and Conspiracy: Introducing a Socio-Functional Model of Conspiracy Beliefs** (Jais Adam-Troian, Maria Chayinska, Maria-Paola Paladino, Özden Melis Uluğ, Jeroen Vaes & Pascal Wagner-Egger, *British Journal of Social Psychology*, 2023) — https://boa.unimib.it/retrieve/c54ec06b-8d06-4ca9-a43c-1b79f065147c/Adam-Troian-2023-British%20Journal%20of%20Social%20Psychology.pdf — ~7 min. A theoretical model proposing that economic precarity (chronic material insecurity, not just low income) breeds conspiracy belief mainly by eroding trust in institutions and generating perceptions of higher-status groups as a hostile out-group. Freely available via the University of Milano-Bicocca's institutional repository (one of the authors' home institution).

**When Alienated From Society, Conspiracy Theory Belief Gives Meaning to Life** (Tatjana Schnell, Roberto Viviani, Claudia Lenz & Henning Krampe, *Heliyon*, 2024) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11324984/ — ~7 min. A survey of a representative sample of 974 German residents testing whether conspiracy belief about COVID-19 compensates for a loss of meaning caused by feeling alienated from society (discriminated against, treated unequally, or restricted in one's rights). Heliyon is a peer-reviewed, fully open-access journal under the Cell Press/Elsevier umbrella.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with group identity, because it connects most directly to Tuesday's evolutionary account. Van Prooijen and van Vugt's "alliance/coalition detection" mechanism (Tuesday) is, in effect, the raw cognitive material that Robertson, Pretus, Rathje, and Van Bavel's review puts to social use. Their argument is that conspiracy belief is not one motivational phenomenon but at least two, working through different pathways. "Social identity" motives pull people toward the *content* of a specific conspiracy theory — one that flatters their in-group, blames a disliked out-group, or reinforces a threatened group status; think of Monday's PNAS Nexus survey finding that Americans overwhelmingly suspect the opposing political coalition, not their own, of conspiring. "Uniqueness" motives, by contrast, pull people toward the *qualities* of conspiratorial explanations themselves — feeling like one of the few who "sees through" a false official story, regardless of which group is blamed. The distinction matters practically: a message correcting factual content might address the first pathway but do nothing for the second, since what a uniqueness-motivated believer wants is the feeling of exclusive insight, not a specific villain.

Adam-Troian and colleagues' socio-functional model shifts the lens from identity to material circumstances. Their claim is that it is specifically the *subjective, chronic* experience of economic insecurity — precarity, not simply low income at a single point in time — that predisposes people toward conspiratorial thinking, and that it does so largely indirectly: precarity erodes trust in institutions and fuels a sense that better-off groups and the institutions that serve them are an adversarial out-group, and that hostile framing is what makes conspiratorial explanations feel plausible. This is a genuinely useful complement to Tuesday's finding that political trust outweighed personality traits like need for cognitive closure — it offers a candidate answer for *why* some people end up with low institutional trust in the first place, tracing it to a material, structural condition rather than a fixed disposition. It is worth flagging, though, that this is presented as a theoretical model rather than as an unambiguous fact: a subsequent 2024 study in the same journal reported a successful conceptual replication of the institutional-trust pathway, but a newer 2026 study using longitudinal and experimental designs — methods built specifically to test causation rather than mere association — found no evidence that precarity actually causes later conspiracy belief, and no effect of experimentally manipulated financial insecurity either. The original authors have already published a rebuttal contesting that newer study's methods. This is a live, unresolved dispute in the literature, not a settled finding, and Friday's reading will return to it.

Schnell, Viviani, Lenz, and Krampe's survey approaches the same territory — insecurity, institutional relationships — from the angle of meaning rather than material precarity. Their finding is a genuine interaction, not just a main effect: belief in COVID-19 conspiracy theories was associated with a *greater* sense of meaning in life, but only among people who also felt alienated from society, and that alienation was itself predicted by experiences of discrimination or feeling one's rights were restricted. Framed the other way around: for people who did not feel alienated, conspiracy belief showed no such association with meaning. The authors interpret this as compensation — when ordinary channels of belonging and standing in society are blocked, a conspiracy narrative can supply an alternative story that restores a sense of coherence and agency, echoing the "threat management" mechanism from Tuesday's evolutionary account, but now anchored to a specific social experience (discrimination, exclusion) rather than a generic sense of threat.

Put the three readings together and a division of labour appears. Group identity (Robertson et al.) explains *which* conspiracy theories a given person is likely to find attractive — usually ones that flatter their own group and implicate a rival one. Precarity and institutional distrust (Adam-Troian et al., contested as the mechanism may be) offer one candidate explanation for *why* institutional trust erodes in the first place, which Tuesday's reading already showed is a stronger predictor of conspiracy belief than personality traits. Alienation and blocked meaning (Schnell et al.) explain *what conspiracy belief is doing for the believer psychologically* once it takes hold — restoring a sense of coherence that discrimination or exclusion has taken away. None of these three mechanisms requires low intelligence or a "flawed" mind; all three describe ordinary responses to real, or realistically perceived, social conditions.

This also gives a fuller answer to a question Monday's reading raised but could not resolve: why do interpersonal ties predict conspiracy belief more strongly than social-media exposure? If conspiracy belief is substantially about group identity, institutional distrust rooted in shared material circumstances, and compensating for felt exclusion, then family, friends, and coworkers — people who share your economic situation, your group memberships, and often your experiences of being treated fairly or unfairly by institutions — are exactly the network through which those specific social and political conditions would be discussed, validated, and reinforced. A social-media feed can expose someone to conspiratorial content, but it cannot, by itself, replicate the shared lived circumstances that make a given conspiratorial explanation feel personally relevant.

## Questions to think about

1. Robertson et al. distinguish "social identity" motives (which group is blamed) from "uniqueness" motives (the feeling of special insight). If you were designing an intervention, would you expect the same message to work against both, or would you need two different approaches?
2. The Adam-Troian model has already produced a supportive replication and a contradicting one using stronger causal-inference methods. Before Friday's reading on controversies, what would make you more confident one way or the other — more replications of the same design, or a different kind of study entirely?
3. Schnell et al. found conspiracy belief tracked with *more* meaning in life specifically for people who felt alienated. If conspiracy belief is partly serving a genuine psychological need (restoring meaning), what does that imply about interventions that only target the accuracy of the belief itself, without addressing the underlying alienation?
