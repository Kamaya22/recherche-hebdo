# The psychology of conspiracy beliefs — Day 4/7: State of the art on real-world consequences

## Context

The first three days built a mechanistic picture from the inside out: Monday framed conspiracy belief as universal and emotionally driven; Tuesday located a largely universal cognitive substrate (pattern perception, agency detection) underneath it; Wednesday added the social and political conditions — institutional distrust, group identity, alienation — that activate that substrate in some people, places, and moments more than others. Along the way, Wednesday's reading already flagged a live dispute: the claim that economic precarity causally drives conspiracy belief has produced one supportive replication and one contradicting study using stronger causal-inference methods, with the matter still unresolved.

Today turns the question around: given that people do hold these beliefs, what actually happens downstream? Public debate often treats the answer as obvious — conspiracy belief drives vaccine refusal, undermines democracy, and radicalizes people toward violence — but "obvious" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. The three readings today each revisit one of those claims (health behavior, democratic attitudes, violence) using a recent scoping review or longitudinal study, and each one asks not just "is there an association" but "how strong is it, and is there any evidence it is actually causal rather than a reflection of who tends to hold conspiratorial views in the first place." This is the same causal-inference caution that ran through Tuesday's and Wednesday's readings, now applied systematically to the consequences side of the ledger rather than the causes side.

A note on scope: "consequences" here means measured, published effects on behavior and attitudes — not a claim that conspiracy belief is harmless. The point of today's readings is that the size, domain, and causal status of these effects vary a great deal, and that variation matters for anyone deciding where to focus resources (a question day 6's readings on counter-messaging will return to directly).

## Today's readings (~19 min)

**Conspiracy narratives and vaccine hesitancy: a scoping review of prevalence, impact, and interventions** (Frederike Taubert, Georg Meyer-Hoeven, Philipp Schmid, Pia Gerdes & Cornelia Betsch, *BMC Public Health*, 2024) — https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11606073/ — ~7 min. A scoping review that screened 500 articles down to 205 included studies to map what is actually known about vaccine-related conspiracy narratives: how prevalent belief in them is, how it relates to vaccination intention and uptake, and which interventions reduce its impact. BMC Public Health is a peer-reviewed, fully open-access journal (BioMed Central, Springer Nature), and this is the most comprehensive recent synthesis of the health-behavior literature, distinguishing correlational from experimental evidence explicitly.

**Linking Conspiracy Beliefs with Violence: A Scoping Review of the Empirical Literature** (Elizabeth Belton, Toby Mulholland & Kate Murphy, *Terrorism and Political Violence*, 2025) — https://research-repository.griffith.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/b312b549-4699-4225-8eed-a165f21f36e2/content — ~6 min. A scoping review of 33 quantitative studies (published 2000–mid-2023) that measure the association between conspiracy belief and violence-justifying norms, attitudes, intentions, or actions. *Terrorism and Political Violence* is a peer-reviewed specialist journal (Taylor & Francis/Routledge); this open copy is the authors' version self-archived in Griffith University's institutional repository (Belton's home institution), which lets us confirm the reported statistics against the accepted text.

**Conspiracy beliefs and democratic backsliding: Longitudinal effects of election conspiracy beliefs on criticism of democracy and support for authoritarianism during political contests** (Elizabeth F. Thomas et al., *Political Psychology*, 2025) — https://research-repository.griffith.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/d5c18ccf-4594-4623-85b1-e897ba8b4333/content — ~6 min. A longitudinal panel study (random-intercept cross-lagged models) tracking conspiracy belief and anti-democratic attitudes across the 2020 US presidential election (N ≈ 609) and the 2020 New Zealand general election (N ≈ 603). *Political Psychology* is the peer-reviewed flagship journal of the International Society of Political Psychology, published by Wiley; the article is published open access under a Creative Commons Attribution license, and this institutional-repository copy is the authors' archived version of that same open text.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with health behavior, where the evidence base is largest and, on causal grounds, strongest. Taubert and colleagues found that across more than 100 correlational studies, belief in a specific vaccine-related conspiracy narrative — or a general "conspiracy mentality" — was associated with lower vaccination intention or uptake in nearly all of them, across a range of vaccines and countries (most study concentration was in Europe and North America, and most concerned COVID-19 vaccination in 2021–2022). Reported prevalence of belief varied enormously, from 2% to 77% depending on the specific narrative and population, which is itself a caution against treating "belief in conspiracy theories" as a single, stable quantity. The more interesting methodological point is what happens when the review restricts itself to studies that can actually speak to causation: only seven experimental studies exposed participants to conspiracy narratives and measured downstream vaccination intentions, and six of the seven found a small negative effect. That is a real, if modest, causal signal — probably the closest thing in today's three readings to solid evidence that exposure to conspiratorial content changes health-relevant intentions, not just that conspiracy-minded people happen to avoid vaccines for other reasons. The review's other useful finding, for day 6's topic, is that several intervention types — social norm feedback, fact-checking labels, and prebunking — reduced belief in the narratives themselves, and in some cases increased vaccination intentions too.

Move to violence, where the evidence looks superficially similar (a large majority of studies find "a" significant association) but is much weaker once you look past the headline count. Belton, Mulholland, and Murphy's scoping review found that 30 of 33 included studies (91%) reported a statistically significant association between conspiracy belief and violence-justifying norms, attitudes, or intentions — but the strength of that association was typically very weak to weak (correlation below 0.39 in 77% of reported correlations), with only nine studies finding a moderate association and just two finding a strong one. Two further limitations matter more than the effect-size numbers alone. First, almost all of this evidence measures attitudes toward violence (would you justify it, do you intend to engage in it) rather than actual violent behavior; studies that look at real behavior are confined to samples of already-radicalized extremist offenders, which cannot tell us how common the belief-to-action pathway is in the general population. Second, "violence" itself was operationalized inconsistently across studies — sometimes general aggression, sometimes specifically political or extremist violence — which makes the 91% figure less impressive than it sounds, since a statistically significant but tiny correlation with a broad, loosely defined outcome is a different claim than "conspiracy belief meaningfully predicts political violence."

Political attitudes toward democracy itself is where today's readings produce the most genuinely surprising result, precisely because Thomas and colleagues used a design built to interrogate direction of causation rather than just measure association. Using random-intercept cross-lagged panel models across two national elections, they found little evidence that election conspiracy beliefs temporally preceded increased criticism of democracy or support for authoritarianism — the pattern popular commentary usually assumes. In the New Zealand sample, the relationship ran the other way: people who already supported authoritarian governance or were already critical of democracy were more likely to subsequently endorse election conspiracy theories, not the reverse. This echoes a shape we have now seen twice this week — Wednesday's disputed precarity-to-conspiracy-belief pathway, and now this democratic-attitudes-to-conspiracy-belief pathway — where a plausible, widely assumed causal story (material or political conditions → conspiracy belief → downstream harm) turns out, under a design capable of testing direction, to run at least partly backwards or not to hold up as a within-person causal effect at all. A complementary cross-national finding worth noting here: a 2024 study in *Public Opinion Quarterly* (Christian Schnaudt) analyzing survey data from 29 countries found that the negative relationship between conspiracy belief and trust in electoral integrity was strongest specifically where the actual quality of elections was high — a reminder that these relationships are not fixed, uniform effects but depend on the real institutional context they occur in.

Put the three readings side by side and a pattern emerges that should temper the "obvious" story about consequences. The domain with the best causal evidence (health behavior) shows a real but modest effect — six of seven experiments found a small negative impact of conspiracy exposure on vaccination intention, not a large one. The domain with the most alarming framing in public discourse (violence) shows a statistically common but individually weak association, measured almost entirely as attitudes rather than behavior, with genuine behavioral evidence limited to already-radicalized samples that cannot establish how the pathway operates more broadly. And the domain where causal direction has actually been tested with a design suited to the question (democratic attitudes) found the assumed direction largely unsupported, with some evidence the arrow runs the other way. None of this means conspiracy belief is inconsequential — the vaccination-intention effect alone has clear public-health stakes at population scale — but it does mean that "conspiracy beliefs cause X" is, across most of the literature examined today, a substantially stronger claim than the underlying evidence currently supports.

## Questions to think about

1. Of the three domains covered today, only vaccine-related health behavior has meaningful experimental evidence for a causal effect. What would it take to design an ethical, informative experimental (rather than purely correlational or panel) study of the belief-to-violence pathway — and is it even possible?
2. Thomas et al. found that in New Zealand, authoritarian attitudes predicted later conspiracy belief rather than the reverse. If that reverse-causation pattern generalizes, how should it change public messaging that frames conspiracy belief as a cause of democratic backsliding rather than a symptom of it?
3. The violence scoping review found a statistically significant association in 91% of studies but a weak effect size in most of them. How should communicators and policymakers talk about a finding that is real, common across studies, and yet small — without either dismissing it or overstating it?
