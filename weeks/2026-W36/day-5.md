# The psychology of conspiracy beliefs — Day 5/7: Controversies and open debates

## Context

The first four days built what looked, by Thursday, like an increasingly settled picture: a largely universal cognitive substrate (pattern perception, agency detection) gets activated by social and political conditions (institutional distrust, group identity, alienation), and produces real but more modest and more context-dependent consequences than popular discourse assumes. Along the way, though, the week has flagged three loose threads rather than tying them off: Wednesday's reading on economic precarity ended on an explicit, unresolved dispute between a supportive replication and a contradicting study using stronger causal methods; Thursday found that in at least one domain (democratic attitudes), a widely assumed causal arrow ran backwards under a design built to test direction; and every day this week has relied, without much scrutiny, on the assumption that a survey item like "I agree the coin-toss sequence was rigged" or "the election was stolen" cleanly measures something called "conspiracy belief."

Today pulls on those threads directly, because they are not incidental footnotes — they are the field's live controversies. Two are covered here. First, an empirical one: does economic precarity actually cause conspiracy belief, as the socio-functional model from Wednesday proposes, or does the causal arrow run the other way, or not run causally at all? Two new 2026 studies attack this question with designs built specifically to test direction, and their answers complicate the picture further rather than resolving it in either direction. Second, a foundational, mostly overlooked measurement question that quietly underlies everything the week has covered so far: when someone reports "agreeing" with a conspiracy theory on a 1-7 scale, are researchers measuring a firmly held, near-unshakeable conviction — the kind of belief the word "irrational" is usually reserved for — or a softer, more provisional judgment that a claim seems plausible given the available evidence? That distinction is exactly what separates "conspiracy belief is a pathology" from "conspiracy belief can be a reasonable, if sometimes mistaken, response to uncertainty," and it turns out the field has rarely checked which one its own instruments are actually capturing.

A methodological note before diving in: today's readings, more than any other day this week, are read *against* each other rather than simply added together. Two of them investigate the same causal question with different designs and reach different emphases; understanding today's topic means sitting with that disagreement rather than picking a winner, because the disagreement itself is the state of the art.

## Today's readings (~20 min)

**Testing the socio-functional model: Does precarity cause conspiracy belief?** (Matthew D. Marques, Malcolm N. Williams, Elena J. R. Clarke, Nasreen Fida, Josh Heffernan, Susan R. Hill, John R. Kerr, Mathew Ling, Claire Parkinson, Melina Thermos & Jarrod Tier, *British Journal of Social Psychology*, 2026) — https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/bjso.70037 — ~7 min. Three preregistered studies (two observational longitudinal panels, over 3 and 11 months, plus a between-groups experiment) designed specifically to test whether precarity — introduced by Wednesday's reading as a proposed driver of conspiracy belief — has a causal effect on it, either directly or via institutional distrust, during the ongoing cost-of-living crisis. *British Journal of Social Psychology* is the peer-reviewed journal of the British Psychological Society (already cited Wednesday for the original socio-functional model this paper tests); this is the direct empirical follow-up the week has been building toward.

**Longitudinal analysis shows possible distinct patterns of associations between conspiracy beliefs and either institutional distrust or the sense of precarity** (Magdalena Adamus et al., *British Journal of Social Psychology*, 2026) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12704043/ — ~7 min. An independent three-wave longitudinal panel study (N = 925 adults) using preregistered cross-lagged models to test the same causal question from a different data set. Freely readable via PubMed Central (NIH's open archive), which lets us confirm this is the accepted, peer-reviewed version rather than a working paper.

**Convictions or Plausibility Judgements? The Ambiguity of Self-Reported Agreement With Conspiracy Theories** (Kenzo Nera & Robbie M. Sutton, *European Journal of Social Psychology*, 2026) — https://kar.kent.ac.uk/112466/1/Nera%20Sutton%20AAM%20EJSP%202025.pdf — ~6 min. Three studies (combined N > 1,800) asking a question that sits underneath every survey-based finding cited so far this week: when people report "agreeing" with a conspiracy theory, are they expressing a firm conviction that the claim is definitely true, or a more tentative judgement that it is merely plausible? Freely available as the authors' accepted manuscript via the University of Kent's institutional repository, which lets us confirm this is the peer-reviewed text rather than a preliminary draft.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with the precarity dispute, because it is the cleanest illustration of how contested "settled-looking" findings in this field can be. Recall Wednesday's socio-functional model: chronic economic insecurity erodes institutional trust, which in turn makes conspiratorial explanations of the world feel more plausible. Marques and ten colleagues set out to test that causal chain directly, using the strongest tools available for the job — two cross-lagged panel studies spanning 3 and 11 months, plus an experiment that manipulated participants' sense of precarity directly. The result was a clean, three-for-three null: precarity earlier in time did not predict increases in conspiracy belief later in the same panels, and experimentally increasing participants' sense of precarity produced no measurable effect on conspiracy belief, whether directly or through decreased trust in government. Taken at face value, this looks like a falsification of a specific causal claim from a well-cited 2023 model — precisely the kind of self-correction good science is supposed to produce.

But "taken at face value" is doing real work in that sentence, and this is where the controversy proper begins. The original authors have replied — in a piece titled, tellingly, "Half the Test" — arguing that Marques and colleagues tested only the precarity-to-belief link directly, without longitudinally testing the model's proposed mediator, institutional distrust, and that their own data show a bidirectional relationship between distrust and conspiracy belief that a design testing only precarity cannot see. That reply currently exists as a posted response rather than a peer-reviewed publication in its own right, so it is flagged here as a preprint — not yet peer reviewed — rather than treated as an equally weighted source; the disagreement is worth reporting precisely because it remains unresolved, not because either side has settled it. What is worth taking away, independent of who is right about the model's "full architecture," is a methodological lesson that recurs across this week: a null result from a well-designed causal test is informative, but a complex, multi-step theoretical model can rarely be falsified by testing just one of its links.

The second, fully independent study this week brings to the precarity question — Adamus and colleagues' three-wave panel of 925 adults — adds a twist rather than a tiebreaker. Their preregistered cross-lagged models found a pattern that runs, if anything, in the *opposite* direction from the socio-functional model's prediction: a sense of precarity tended to follow adherence to conspiracy beliefs over time rather than precede it, while institutional distrust and conspiracy belief showed their own distinct, separable pattern of association not reducible to precarity. Put the two 2026 studies side by side and a shape from Thursday's reading reappears: for a second time this week, a plausible and widely assumed direction of causation (material hardship → distrust → conspiracy belief) fails to hold up under designs capable of actually testing direction, and in at least one data set the arrow may run backwards — believing in conspiracies may deepen one's sense of precarity, rather than the reverse. Neither study, by itself, closes the question; together, they shift the burden of proof onto anyone still asserting the original causal story as settled fact.

Now to the measurement question, which is less a single controversy than the hidden assumption underneath most of the studies read this week. Nera and Sutton's starting observation is almost embarrassingly simple once stated: standard conspiracy-belief scales ask people to rate their "agreement" with a claim on a numeric scale, and researchers then treat that number as if it straightforwardly measures how strongly someone believes the claim is *true*. But "I agree" could mean "I am certain this is a fact" (a conviction) or "given what I know, this seems more likely than not" (a plausibility judgement) — and those are psychologically and normatively different things. A person voicing a cautious plausibility judgement about a claim under genuine uncertainty is behaving quite differently from one who is dogmatically, unshakeably convinced — yet a conventional 7-point agreement scale cannot tell the two apart, which is exactly the ambiguity that fuels the "are believers irrational or just epistemically humble" debate this week's theme opened with.

Their actual findings, across three studies, are more reassuring for the existing literature than the setup might suggest — a genuinely useful, if slightly deflationary, contribution to the week's theme of over-pathologizing. In Study 1, the large majority (76.9%) of participants who reported complete agreement or disagreement with a specific conspiracy claim said they believed the available evidence definitively proved their position — meaning that, at the extremes of the scale at least, people really do mean something like firm conviction, not tentative hedging, and these self-described "certain" participants also reported feeling comparatively well-informed about the topic. In Study 3, self-reported agreement correlated more strongly with participants' independent veracity judgements (r = 0.92) than with a separately measured plausibility judgement (r = 0.82) — a real, detectable distinction, but a modest one. The authors' own conclusion is appropriately measured: the conviction/plausibility distinction is conceptually real and worth keeping in mind, but the empirical overlap between the two is close enough that it is unlikely to fundamentally undermine the validity of the large existing body of survey-based conspiracy-belief research this week has drawn on. In other words: the field's instruments are not perfectly clean, but they are not measuring something else entirely either.

A complementary study worth a brief mention connects the measurement question back to the "is it rational" framing directly. A 2025 study in *Social Psychological and Personality Science* (Marius Frenken, Annika Reusch & Roland Imhoff) tested whether cognitive skills and rational-thinking dispositions predict belief in conspiracy theories independently pretested as plausible versus those independently pretested as wildly implausible. They did not, for the plausible ones: measures of cognitive skill and rational thinking style predicted lower endorsement only of theories that were already judged (by independent raters) to be far-fetched, and showed essentially no relationship with endorsement of theories that had some independent claim to plausibility. That is a genuinely important qualifier to any blanket claim that conspiracy belief signals flawed reasoning: it may do so specifically for the implausible end of the spectrum, while endorsing a conspiracy theory that turns out to have a real evidentiary basis is, unsurprisingly, not a marker of poor thinking at all. This dovetails with a decades-old and still-unresolved debate among philosophers of knowledge over whether conspiracy theories should be judged as a category ("generalism," which tends to treat conspiracist explanation as inherently suspect) or one claim at a time on its own evidence ("particularism") — a debate that has, by most philosophers' own account, reached something of a stalemate over how to avoid begging the question either way.

Put the week's fifth day together with what came before, and the "controversies" are not a detour from the main story — they are the main story catching up with its own uncertainty. A causal claim that looked well-supported on Wednesday (precarity drives belief) looks considerably shakier by Friday, contested by two new 2026 studies that disagree with each other about what a null result even settles. A measurement assumption every study this week has quietly relied on (that agreement scales measure belief cleanly) turns out to be mostly, but not entirely, defensible. And whether conspiracy belief looks "irrational" turns out to depend heavily on which specific claim is being asked about, not on some general deficiency in the believer. None of this means the field has learned nothing — if anything, the sophistication of the designs used to interrogate these questions (cross-lagged panels, experiments, plausibility-calibrated stimulus sets) is itself evidence of a maturing science. But Saturday's turn to methods and counter-messaging should be read with this day's caution in mind: an intervention built on an oversimplified causal story, or on an unexamined measurement instrument, risks solving a problem the evidence does not actually support existing in the form assumed.

## Questions to think about

1. Marques et al. found a triple null result for precarity causing conspiracy belief; Adam-Troian and colleagues argue the test was incomplete because it did not longitudinally model institutional distrust as a mediator. When a large multi-study test of a theory comes back null, how much weight should "you tested only half the model" carry as a rebuttal — and what would a genuinely decisive test of the full model need to look like?
2. Nera and Sutton found that agreement, plausibility, and veracity judgements were tightly correlated (r's of 0.82–0.92) but not identical. If you were redesigning a conspiracy-belief survey from scratch, would you bother separating these constructs, given the authors' own conclusion that doing so is unlikely to overturn past findings?
3. Frenken, Reusch, and Imhoff found that rational-thinking measures predict rejection of implausible conspiracy theories but not plausible ones. If "conspiracy belief" is not one psychological phenomenon but at least two — believing far-fetched claims versus believing claims with genuine evidentiary support — what would that imply for how Monday through Thursday's findings about mechanisms and consequences should be qualified?
