# The psychology of conspiracy beliefs — Day 2/7: Cognitive mechanisms

## Context

Monday's panorama ended on a reframing: conspiracy belief looks less like a rare pathology and more like a graded trait most people hold to some degree, driven more by emotion and social context than by a deficit in reasoning ability. Today drills into the first half of that picture — the "emotion" and "cognition" side, not yet the "social" side (that is Wednesday's topic). The question is mechanistic: what is actually happening, cognitively, when an ambiguous or threatening situation gets interpreted as evidence of a hidden plot rather than coincidence, error, or bad luck?

Two ordinary mental processes keep showing up in the literature as candidate building blocks: pattern perception (the ability to detect meaningful regularities in the world, essential for learning and prediction) and agency detection (the tendency to attribute events to an intentional actor rather than to chance). Both are adaptive most of the time — missing a real pattern or a real predator is usually costlier than seeing one that isn't there. Conspiracy theories, on this account, are not a separate cognitive faculty but a by-product of these ordinary systems firing in situations of uncertainty or threat, sometimes producing "illusory" patterns and "hyperactive" agency attributions. A separate, more personality-flavored strand of research asks whether some people are simply more prone to this than others, via traits like the need for cognitive closure — a discomfort with ambiguity and a corresponding drive to settle on *an* explanation, any explanation, quickly.

Today's three readings cover, in order: the leading theoretical account of *why* these mechanisms exist at all (an evolutionary framing), the best-known experimental demonstration that illusory pattern perception actually predicts conspiracy belief, and a large, more recent study that puts the "need for cognitive closure" story to a tougher test than most of the earlier literature — with a more deflationary result than the popular version of the theory suggests.

## Today's readings (~20 min)

**Conspiracy Theories: Evolved Functions and Psychological Mechanisms** (Jan-Willem van Prooijen & Mark van Vugt, *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, 2018) — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1745691618774270 — ~7 min. A theoretical review asking whether conspiracy belief is best explained as a by-product of several psychological systems that evolved for other reasons (pattern recognition, agency detection, threat management, alliance/coalition detection) or as a dedicated adaptation for spotting real conspiracies. *Perspectives on Psychological Science* is a top-tier, peer-reviewed review journal of the Association for Psychological Science; this paper is one of the most-cited theoretical syntheses in the field and is the clearest single account of where the "pattern perception" and "agency detection" building blocks referenced across the literature actually come from.

**Connecting the dots: Illusory pattern perception predicts belief in conspiracies and the supernatural** (Jan-Willem van Prooijen, Karen M. Douglas & Clara De Inocencio, *European Journal of Social Psychology*, 2018) — https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5900972/ — ~7 min. Five linked experiments (correlational and manipulation-based) testing, directly, whether the tendency to see patterns in randomly generated coin-toss sequences, in abstract paintings, and in real-world events predicts belief in conspiracy theories and the supernatural — and whether inducing belief in one conspiracy theory makes people see more patterns elsewhere. Freely readable via PubMed Central (NIH's open archive), which lets us confirm this is the peer-reviewed published version rather than a preprint.

**Need for cognitive closure, political trust, and belief in conspiracy theories during the COVID-19 pandemic** (Alexander Jedinger & Lena Masch, *Frontiers in Social Psychology*, 2024) — https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/social-psychology/articles/10.3389/frsps.2024.1447313/full — ~6 min. A longitudinal, nationally representative panel study of the German population (N ≈ 2,883) that measured need for cognitive closure *before* the pandemic and conspiracy belief *during* it, avoiding the reverse-causation problem that affects many single-wave studies of this trait. Frontiers in Social Psychology is a peer-reviewed, fully open-access journal; the study is included specifically because its findings complicate the tidier story told by earlier, smaller studies.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Van Prooijen and van Vugt start from a puzzle: if conspiracy beliefs are so often false and sometimes costly (refusing vaccines, distrusting elections), why would evolution have left us so susceptible to them? Their answer is that we probably did not evolve *to believe conspiracy theories* specifically; we evolved several other capacities that, combined, make conspiratorial explanations easy to generate and hard to resist in the right circumstances. Pattern recognition lets us learn cause-and-effect from experience, and erring on the side of seeing a pattern (a "false positive") is usually cheaper than missing a real one. Agency detection lets us anticipate the intentions of other people (and historically, predators and rival groups) — and again, mistakenly seeing intent behind a rustling bush, or behind a policy you dislike, is a cheaper error on average than failing to notice a real threat. Add a third ingredient, threat management (heightened vigilance when we feel anxious, powerless, or under threat — which is exactly when Monday's reading found conspiracy belief spikes), and a fourth, alliance/coalition detection (tracking which groups are cooperating against which), and you get a mind that is, by default, primed to construct exactly the kind of narrative a conspiracy theory offers: intentional actors, coordinated in secret, working against "us." The theory's strength is that it explains *why* these beliefs are universal across cultures and history (Monday's second organizing principle) without requiring a special "conspiracy module" in the brain — it is an emergent property of otherwise useful systems.

Van Prooijen, Douglas, and De Inocencio's five studies turn that theoretical claim into a testable, and tested, prediction: if pattern perception is doing real causal work, then people who see more illusory patterns in genuinely random material should also endorse more conspiracy theories, and manipulating one should move the other. That is what they found. In Study 1, the tendency to see non-random structure in randomly generated coin-toss sequences correlated with conspiracy and paranormal belief. Study 3 sharpened this: pattern perception in genuinely chaotic abstract paintings predicted irrational belief, but pattern perception in paintings that had *actual* structure did not — it is specifically the perception of patterns where none exist that matters, not pattern-spotting in general. Most striking is Study 5: experimentally inducing belief in one specific, unrelated conspiracy theory made participants subsequently see more patterns in a set of world events, which in turn predicted broader conspiratorial and paranormal belief. That directionality matters — it suggests the relationship is not just "pattern-prone people happen to also be conspiracy-prone people" as a stable trait, but that being pulled into one conspiratorial narrative can measurably shift how much structure a person perceives elsewhere, a feedback loop worth keeping in mind for Thursday's discussion of real-world consequences.

The Jedinger and Masch study is included precisely because it pushes back against the neatest part of this story: the idea that some people are simply high in "need for cognitive closure" (an intolerance of ambiguity and a drive to settle quickly on an explanation) and that this trait alone substantially predicts conspiracy belief. Most of the earlier literature on this — including well-known single-wave survey studies — cannot rule out reverse causation (does needing closure cause conspiracy belief, or does already holding conspiratorial beliefs make you report wanting more certainty?). Jedinger and Masch avoid this by measuring need for cognitive closure in a large, representative German sample *before* the COVID-19 pandemic began, then measuring conspiracy belief and political trust during it. Their result: need for cognitive closure had only a small effect on later conspiracy belief, while political trust — trust in institutions and government — was a far stronger predictor. People high in need for closure *and* low in political trust were the ones who gravitated to conspiratorial accounts of the pandemic; people high in need for closure but high in political trust did not. In other words, the "personality drives conspiracy belief" story is not wrong, but on its own it is a much weaker predictor than a purely dispositional account suggests, and it operates mainly by interacting with the person's actual level of institutional trust.

Taken together, today's readings suggest a layered model rather than a single mechanism. Pattern perception and agency detection look like the more fundamental, largely universal cognitive substrate — present in everyone, and demonstrably manipulable in controlled experiments, which is about as close to causal evidence as this field gets. Need for cognitive closure and similar dispositional traits look real but modest, and their effect appears to depend heavily on context (how much someone already trusts the relevant institutions) rather than acting as a standalone "conspiracy personality." This matters for what comes later this week: if the cognitive substrate is close to universal, interventions aimed only at "irrational" individuals will miss most of the story, and the social and political conditions that activate that substrate — the subject of tomorrow's reading — carry at least as much explanatory weight as any fixed trait.

## Questions to think about

1. If illusory pattern perception is a by-product of a generally useful cognitive system rather than a flaw specific to "conspiracy-minded" people, what does that imply about trying to "fix" conspiratorial thinking through education about probability and randomness alone?
2. Study 5 in the van Prooijen et al. paper suggests belief in one conspiracy theory can causally increase general pattern perception. What would this predict about people who hold multiple, even contradictory, conspiracy beliefs at once — a pattern documented in the survey literature?
3. Jedinger and Masch found political trust mattered more than need for cognitive closure. How might that change where you would put resources if you were designing a public-health communication strategy: toward addressing individual cognitive style, or toward rebuilding institutional trust?
