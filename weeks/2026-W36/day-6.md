# The psychology of conspiracy beliefs — Day 6/7: Methods and counter-messaging

## Context

Friday's reading ended on a caution rather than a conclusion: a widely cited causal claim (precarity drives belief) looked considerably shakier under scrutiny, a foundational measurement assumption (that agreement scales cleanly capture belief) turned out to be mostly, but not entirely, defensible, and whether conspiracy belief looks "irrational" depends heavily on which specific claim is being asked about. That caution matters directly for today's topic, because it is precisely the kind of oversimplified causal story or unexamined measurement instrument Friday warned about that an intervention can be built on without anyone noticing until it fails to work.

Today turns from diagnosis to response: given everything the week has established about mechanisms (Tuesday), social and political drivers (Wednesday), and the genuine but more modest and more context-dependent real-world consequences (Thursday), what does the evidence actually show about reducing conspiracy belief — and how do researchers even test whether an intervention works? The field draws a basic distinction between two families of counter-messaging: reactive approaches that engage with a conspiracy theory after someone has already encountered it (correcting it with counter-evidence, fact-checking a specific claim, "debunking") and proactive approaches that try to build resistance before exposure (warning people about manipulation tactics in advance, "prebunking" or psychological inoculation). That distinction also maps onto a methodological one worth tracking through today's readings: most of this literature is built on small lab experiments with self-selected participants, which are good at establishing whether an effect exists at all but poor at telling us whether it survives contact with a real information environment — a gap only a handful of large field trials have actually tested.

Today's three readings move from the broadest possible view to the most concrete: first, the most comprehensive quantitative synthesis yet of everything that has been tried against conspiracy belief specifically; second, a systematic review that sorts those attempts into a rough taxonomy of what tends to work and what does not; and third, one of the few interventions in this entire field that has been tested not just in a lab but at real, ecological scale in the wild.

## Today's readings (~20 min)

**The Effectiveness of Interventions Addressing Conspiracy Beliefs: A Meta-Analysis** (Łukasz Stasielowicz, *European Journal of Social Psychology*, 2026) — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.70041 — ~7 min. A Bayesian three-level meta-analysis pooling 273 effect sizes from 56 independent samples (total N ≈ 27,996), making it the largest and most methodologically comprehensive synthesis of conspiracy-belief interventions to date. *European Journal of Social Psychology* (already cited on Monday and Wednesday this week) is a peer-reviewed journal of the European Association of Social Psychology, published by Wiley; this specific article sits behind a subscription paywall, though its abstract and key figures are publicly summarized by the publisher and indexers.

**The efficacy of interventions in reducing belief in conspiracy theories: A systematic review** (Cian O'Mahony, Maryanne Brassil, Gillian Murphy & Conor Linehan, *PLOS ONE*, 2023) — https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0280902 — ~7 min. A systematic review of 25 studies (total N = 7,179) that sorts interventions into a rough taxonomy — reactive counterarguments and fact-checking versus proactive priming, inoculation, and critical-thinking instruction — and compares their effect sizes directly. *PLOS ONE* is a large, fully open-access, peer-reviewed multidisciplinary journal published by the nonprofit Public Library of Science, which lets us read and verify the full methodology and results ourselves rather than relying on a summary.

**Psychological inoculation improves resilience against misinformation on social media** (Jon Roozenbeek, Sander van der Linden, Beth Goldberg, Steve Rathje & Stephan Lewandowsky, *Science Advances*, 2022) — https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.abo6254 — ~7 min. Seven preregistered studies — six randomized lab experiments (combined n = 6,464) plus an ecologically valid field study running real inoculation-video ads on YouTube (n = 22,632) — testing whether "technique-based" prebunking, which teaches people to recognize manipulation tactics rather than correcting one specific claim, holds up outside the lab. *Science Advances* is the fully open-access, peer-reviewed sister journal of *Science*, published by the American Association for the Advancement of Science; the field study is the rare case in this literature of a lab-demonstrated effect being tested at real platform scale.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with Stasielowicz's meta-analysis, because its scale lets it say something the smaller studies discussed on earlier days could not: across the accumulated literature, the average effect of an intervention on conspiracy belief is small (Hedges' g ≈ 0.16) but highly variable — some interventions barely move belief at all, and a minority move it substantially. The meta-analysis's real contribution is in its moderator analysis, which asks what separates the interventions that work from the ones that don't. Three factors stood out as capable of pushing an intervention's effect above g = 0.3, roughly double the average: fact-checking the specific conspiratorial claim being tested rather than arguing against conspiratorial thinking in the abstract, tailoring the intervention's content to the particular belief in question rather than using one generic anti-conspiracy message for everyone, and — a direct methodological echo of Friday's reading — avoiding single-item measures of conspiracy belief in favor of multi-item scales, which are less noisy and better able to detect a real effect when one exists. That last point matters beyond this one meta-analysis: it suggests that some of the "ineffective" interventions reported across the literature may look that way partly because the measurement tool used to detect change was too blunt to register it, not necessarily because nothing happened.

O'Mahony and colleagues' systematic review, though smaller in scope, adds a sharper qualitative picture of *which kinds* of interventions cluster at the effective and ineffective ends of that distribution. Their central finding is a fairly clean split: interventions that respond to a conspiracy theory after the fact — presenting counterarguments, contradicting scientific evidence, generic fact-checking — mostly produced small-to-very-small effects, closely matching Thursday's finding that most debunking-style corrections in the misinformation literature are real but modest. By contrast, three proactive approaches — analytical priming (nudging people toward more deliberate, analytic thinking before they evaluate a claim), informational inoculation (pre-exposing people to a weakened, refuted version of a conspiratorial argument before they encounter the real thing), and structured critical-thinking instruction — produced medium-to-large effects. The single largest effect in the whole review came from the most resource-intensive intervention: a three-month university course explicitly teaching students to distinguish science from pseudoscience and to recognize common errors of perception and logic. That contrast is worth sitting with rather than skipping past: the more effective interventions were also, in general, the ones that took more time, more preparation, and more sustained engagement from participants — a tradeoff any real-world counter-messaging campaign has to reckon with, since a three-month course does not scale to a social-media feed the way a fact-check label does.

Roozenbeek and colleagues' study is where the "prevention beats cure" pattern gets its most rigorous methodological test, because it is explicitly designed to answer a question neither of the first two readings can: does an inoculation effect demonstrated in a lab survive being deployed at real scale, to a real, self-selected, distracted online audience, rather than to a captive study sample? Their approach is "technique-based" rather than claim-specific: instead of debunking one particular conspiracy theory or false claim, their short videos train people to recognize five general manipulation tactics common across misinformation — emotionally manipulative language, false dichotomies, incoherence, scapegoating, and ad hominem attacks — summarized elsewhere by the same research group under the acronym DEPICT, in which the "C" stands specifically for spreading Conspiracy theories as one of the six manipulation techniques the broader program targets. The six lab-based randomized experiments established, under controlled conditions, that watching a video improved people's ability to spot these techniques and to discern trustworthy from untrustworthy content. The genuinely notable result is that the seventh study — running the same videos as real ad impressions on YouTube to over 22,000 people who had not opted into a study and could skip the ad at any moment — found the same pattern: improved technique recognition, and better-quality sharing decisions, that held up across the political spectrum. Effects surviving the jump from lab to field at this scale are uncommon enough in psychology generally that the result is treated as a significant methodological benchmark for the whole prebunking research program, not just a replication of the lab finding.

That benchmark comes with a real limit, though, which is worth holding onto rather than treating today's readings as a settled success story. An earlier, related study from the same research tradition — the "Bad News" game, a interactive intervention in which players learn manipulation tactics by producing simulated misinformation themselves — was tested with roughly 15,000 participants and reported a similar pattern of pre-to-post improvement in spotting misleading headlines (Roozenbeek & van der Linden, *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*, 2019, an open-access Nature Portfolio journal). A 2025 replication attempt applying the same game to Indian true and fake news headlines, published in *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* (Tina Seabrooke, Ariana Modirrousta-Galian & Philip A. Higham), found no evidence of improved discrimination between true and fake headlines once the study design controlled for order effects — only a shift toward more cautious responding, not genuinely better detection. Placed next to Roozenbeek et al.'s successful YouTube field trial, this is a useful corrective rather than a contradiction: it suggests that "prebunking works" is not yet a single, generalizable finding so much as a finding that has been well-demonstrated in some formats, platforms, and — so far, predominantly Western — populations, and that needs separate testing, not just assumed transfer, when the language, culture, or specific technique changes. This is the same generalizability caution Wednesday's reading raised about precarity research conducted mostly in the US and Germany, now showing up again on the intervention side of the field.

Step back, and today's three readings — read as a set — sketch how this specific corner of psychology actually accumulates evidence about what works. Surveys and single lab experiments are cheap and useful for generating hypotheses but cannot, on their own, establish whether an effect is real, how large it is relative to everything else that has been tried, or whether it would survive outside a controlled setting. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses like O'Mahony's and Stasielowicz's aggregate many such studies to establish which categories of approach tend to work and by how much, but they inherit the limitations — including the measurement weaknesses Friday's reading flagged — of the underlying primary studies. Large field trials like Roozenbeek et al.'s are the rarest and most expensive rung, but they are the only design that can speak directly to whether an effect generalizes beyond the artificial conditions of a study session, and their scarcity in this literature is itself informative: it means most of what the field currently believes about "what works" is still resting on a smaller evidentiary base than the confident tone of some public-facing summaries suggests.

Put together, the pattern that recurs across all three readings — prevention tends to outperform correction, and general technique-based training tends to travel further than one-off fact-checks — is about as close to a consensus finding as this week's topic has produced. But the size of that advantage is modest in absolute terms (average effects in the g = 0.15–0.3 range, not a cure), it is unevenly demonstrated outside lab settings, and — as the Indian replication failure shows — it cannot yet be assumed to hold across languages, cultures, and specific games or claims without direct testing. That combination of "the general direction looks right" and "the details and scale of the effect remain genuinely open" is exactly the note tomorrow's synthesis will need to hold onto.

## Questions to think about

1. Stasielowicz's meta-analysis found that fact-checking a specific claim was one of the moderators associated with *larger* intervention effects, while O'Mahony's review found that generic counterargument-and-fact-checking approaches were mostly ineffective. What design differences between studies might explain that apparent tension, and how would you test which reading is closer to the truth?
2. Roozenbeek et al.'s YouTube field trial is one of only a handful of interventions in this entire literature tested at real platform scale outside a lab. Given how expensive and rare field trials are, how much should a single large field study like this one shift your confidence relative to dozens of smaller lab RCTs pointing the same direction?
3. The three-month "science versus pseudoscience" course in O'Mahony's review had the largest effect in the whole systematic review, but it does not scale the way a short video or a fact-check label does. If you were allocating a fixed public-health communication budget, how would you weigh a small effect deliverable to millions against a large effect deliverable to a few hundred people a year?
