# The psychology of conspiracy beliefs — Day 1/7: Panorama of the field

## Context

Conspiracy theories used to be treated as a psychological curiosity — the province of a small, credulous fringe. That picture no longer survives contact with the data: large nationally representative surveys now consistently find that most adults endorse at least one conspiratorial claim, and psychological research on the topic has grown so fast that more than half of all academic articles on the subject have been published in just the last few years. This week looks at why that is, moving from the basic cognitive and social mechanisms (days 2-3), through what the evidence actually shows about real-world consequences (day 4), the field's live controversies and blind spots (day 5), and how researchers measure belief and design counter-messaging (day 6), before tying it together on day 7.

Today's goal is a panorama: what researchers mean by "conspiracy belief," roughly how common it is, and the analytical scaffolding — individual, intergroup, and societal levels of explanation — that later days will fill in with mechanisms and evidence.

## Today's readings

Douglas, *European Journal of Social Psychology*, 2018) — https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6282974/ — ~6 min. This introduction to a special issue on the social psychology of conspiracy theories distills the field into four organizing principles. It is the single most-cited framing paper in the discipline and is freely readable via PubMed Central (NIH's open archive), which also lets us verify it is a genuine peer-reviewed publication rather than an opinion piece.

**Contemporary trends in psychological research on conspiracy beliefs: A systematic review** (Irena Pilch, Agnieszka Turska-Kawa, Paulina Wardawy, Agata Olszanecka-Marmola & Wiktoria Smołkowska-Jędo, *Frontiers in Psychology*, 2023) — https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9945548/ — ~8 min. A PRISMA-guided systematic review of studies published between 2018 and 2021, screened from Scopus and Web of Science. It maps how the field measures conspiracy belief, which personality and cognitive variables get studied most, and where the methodological gaps are — useful as a map of the territory before we go into specific mechanisms.

**Information from social ties predicts conspiracy beliefs: Evidence from the attempted assassination of Donald Trump** (Katherine Ognyanova, James N. Druckman, Jonathan Schulman, Matthew A. Baum, Roy H. Perlis & David Lazer, *PNAS Nexus*, 2025) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12203515/ — ~6 min. A nationally representative 50-state US survey (fielded after the July 2024 assassination attempt) that both quantifies how widespread conspiratorial thinking is today and tests what actually predicts it — interpersonal networks versus social-media exposure. PNAS Nexus is a peer-reviewed, fully open-access journal from Oxford University Press affiliated with the US National Academy of Sciences.

## Guided summary

Start with a definition. A conspiracy theory, in the psychological literature, is an explanation of an event or state of affairs that invokes a secret, malevolent plot by powerful actors, and that stands in tension with a more mundane, officially accepted account. What matters for psychology is not whether any particular claim happens to be true (some conspiracies are real — Watergate, the tobacco industry's internal research on cancer) but the *belief process*: why some explanations of this shape feel compelling to some people in some circumstances.

Van Prooijen and Douglas's four organizing principles are a useful map for the whole week. First, conspiracy beliefs are **consequential**: they are not idle trivia but measurably shape health decisions (vaccine uptake), political behavior (trust in elections), and occasionally violence. Second, they are **universal**: cross-cultural and historical evidence shows conspiratorial thinking appears in essentially every society and era, not just modern, Western, or politically polarized ones — which argues against explanations that treat it as a symptom of a particular political moment. Third, belief is driven more by **emotion** than by cold deliberation: anxiety, uncertainty, and a sense of lacking control are consistently better predictors of conspiratorial thinking than measures of general reasoning ability. Fourth, conspiracy belief is inherently **social**: it correlates strongly with group identity, intergroup threat, and the sense that one's own group is being treated unfairly — a thread days 2 and 3 will unpack in more depth.

The Pilch et al. systematic review adds texture to that picture by looking at what the field has actually been measuring. Two things stand out. One is a methodological point worth carrying into every later day this week: most studies rely on the same handful of self-report scales (the "Generic Conspiracist Beliefs Scale" is the most common), which is efficient for cross-study comparison but risks the field converging on whatever those scales happen to capture well, at the expense of harder-to-measure aspects of belief (intensity, behavioral consequences, how beliefs actually spread in real social networks). The other is that personality correlates — lower agreeableness, higher schizotypy, a need for uniqueness or cognitive closure — recur across dozens of studies, which is a reasonably robust finding, even though correlation with a personality trait says little about what actually triggers belief in a given moment.

The Ognyanova et al. survey grounds the theoretical picture in a concrete number: in their nationally representative sample fielded in 2024, the large majority of US adults endorsed at least one item on a standard conspiratorial-thinking scale, and a meaningful share endorsed several. Two findings matter beyond the headline prevalence figure. First, this looks less like a small group of committed "conspiracy theorists" and more like a graded trait that most people hold to some degree — consistent with van Prooijen and Douglas's "universal" principle. Second, and this will matter on day 3 and again on day 6, information received through *interpersonal ties* (family, friends, coworkers) was a stronger and more consistent predictor of conspiracy belief than exposure through social media feeds — a finding that complicates the popular assumption that platform algorithms are the primary engine of conspiratorial thinking.

Put together, today's three readings suggest a research field that has matured past "who are the conspiracy theorists" and moved to "what conditions make this kind of explanation attractive to nearly everyone, and through what channels does it actually spread." That reframing is the thread the rest of this week will follow: Tuesday and Wednesday dig into the cognitive and social mechanisms behind the "emotional" and "social" principles introduced today; Thursday looks at what we can actually say about real-world consequences; Friday surfaces the field's live disagreements; and Saturday turns to how researchers measure belief and test interventions.

## Questions to think about

1. If conspiracy belief is better described as a widely shared, graded trait than a rare pathology, how should that change the tone of public communication aimed at it?
2. The finding that interpersonal ties predict belief more strongly than social-media exposure cuts against a common narrative about algorithms and misinformation — what would you want to see tested before trusting that finding generally, beyond this one US survey?
3. Van Prooijen and Douglas argue conspiracy beliefs are "universal" across history and cultures — does that make them more or less tractable to address through policy or communication?

## This week's theme

**The psychology of conspiracy beliefs — cognitive and social mechanisms, and what research says about counter-messaging.**

Belief in conspiracy theories is not a fringe curiosity: surveys across dozens of countries find that a majority of people endorse at least one conspiratorial claim, and such beliefs correlate with vaccine refusal, climate-science denial, election-fraud claims, and in rare cases political violence. Psychologists and political scientists have spent the last two decades trying to understand why the human mind is so receptive to these narratives — and the picture that emerges is more nuanced than "irrational people believe silly things." Conspiracy beliefs are now understood as the product of ordinary cognitive processes (pattern detection, intentionality bias, motivated reasoning) interacting with real social conditions (institutional distrust, group identity threat, feelings of powerlessness). This matters practically because public-health campaigns, election officials, and platforms have spent enormous resources on "debunking" strategies whose effectiveness is contested, while a newer generation of interventions (prebunking / inoculation) has produced some of the most robust experimental results in the misinformation literature.

Tentative outline for the rest of the week: Day 2 — cognitive mechanisms (pattern perception, agency detection, need for closure); Day 3 — social and political drivers (distrust, identity, powerlessness); Day 4 — state of the art on real-world consequences; Day 5 — controversies and open debates (rational response to uncertainty vs. irrationality; measurement problems); Day 6 — methods, and what works in counter-messaging (prebunking vs. debunking); Day 7 — synthesis and open questions. This is a guide, not a contract — it may shift depending on what the literature turns up.
