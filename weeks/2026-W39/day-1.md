# Solar geoengineering — Day 1/7: What is solar geoengineering, and why is it suddenly a serious policy question?

## Context

For most of the twentieth century, the idea of deliberately altering the atmosphere to cool the planet lived at the margins of climate science — a thought experiment invoked mostly to illustrate how desperate the alternative would have to be. That has changed. Global emissions have not fallen fast enough to keep warming comfortably below the targets set in Paris, and a small but growing community of climate scientists, engineers, and philanthropists now argues that "solar geoengineering" — deliberately reflecting a fraction of incoming sunlight back to space — deserves serious research, even if it is never deployed. Funding for this research reportedly nearly tripled in 2025, and a handful of small outdoor experiments have already been attempted or proposed, some of which were cancelled after public pushback.

This week's reading traces solar geoengineering from first principles: what it is, how it might work, what is and is not known about its effects, and the genuinely unresolved political and ethical questions around who — if anyone — should get to decide to use it. Today's panorama sets the terms: the three leading proposed methods, the basic physics that makes them plausible, and why the idea provokes such strong disagreement even among people who agree that climate change is an emergency. Later in the week we will go deeper into the atmospheric mechanisms (Day 2), what climate models project about benefits and side effects (Day 3), the current state of real-world experiments and funding (Day 4), the central controversies (Day 5), the methodological limits of how scientists study something that has (mostly) never been done (Day 6), and a synthesis of the governance debate (Day 7).

## Today's readings

**Solar Geoengineering: History, Methods, Governance, Prospects** (Parson & Keith, *Annual Review of Environment and Resources*, 2024) — https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-environ-112321-081911 — ~10 min (executive summary and introduction). A comprehensive review by two of the field's most-cited researchers (Edward Parson, UCLA; David Keith, University of Chicago), covering the history of the idea, the physical methods proposed, the state of governance thinking, and near-term prospects. This is the single best "state of the field" reference for solar geoengineering as of 2024, published in a well-established peer-reviewed review journal.

**Reflecting Sunlight: Recommendations for Solar Geoengineering Research and Research Governance** (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2021) — https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/1 — ~7 min (read the report highlights / summary chapter). A consensus study report from the US National Academies — the closest thing to an authoritative, independent scientific verdict on the state of the field circa 2021. It lays out the three leading methods, what was and was not understood about each, and recommends a US research program under formal governance rather than unregulated experimentation. Freely readable online.

**One Atmosphere: An Independent Expert Review on Solar Radiation Modification Research and Deployment** (UN Environment Programme, 2023) — https://www.unep.org/resources/report/Solar-Radiation-Modification-research-deployment — ~5 min (summary / key findings). Commissioned by UNEP and produced by an international panel of independent scientists, this report is the closest thing to a global, intergovernmental-adjacent assessment of the field. It concludes large-scale deployment is not currently justified given the state of knowledge, and calls for a global scientific assessment process and exploration of multilateral governance — the clearest institutional statement yet of where international opinion stands.

## Guided summary

Solar geoengineering (also called "solar radiation modification," SRM, or sometimes "solar radiation management") is not one technology but a family of proposals that share a single idea: reflect a small amount of incoming sunlight — on the order of 1% — back into space before it warms the Earth's surface. Unlike carbon dioxide removal, which addresses the cause of warming by pulling greenhouse gases out of the atmosphere, solar geoengineering treats a symptom: it could lower global average temperature relatively quickly without touching atmospheric CO₂ concentrations, and therefore would do nothing to slow ocean acidification, one of the other major consequences of rising CO₂.

Three methods dominate the serious scientific literature. The most studied is **stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI)**: releasing reflective particles — most commonly modeled as sulfate aerosols, the same compounds volcanoes inject during large eruptions — into the stratosphere, roughly 15–25 km up, where they would linger for one to two years and scatter sunlight globally. The physical plausibility of this approach rests heavily on observed nature: large volcanic eruptions (Mount Pinatubo in 1991 is the classic case) measurably cooled the planet for a year or two afterward, giving scientists real-world data to calibrate their models against. The second method, **marine cloud brightening (MCB)**, would spray fine sea-salt particles into low-lying marine clouds to make them reflect more sunlight — an effect that is already observed, inadvertently, in the tracks left by ship exhaust over the ocean ("ship tracks"). The third, **cirrus cloud thinning**, works in the opposite direction: thinning high, wispy ice clouds that currently trap outgoing heat, so more heat escapes to space — this is less like the other two, which reflect incoming sunlight, and more like reducing a different kind of greenhouse effect.

Of the three, SAI has received by far the most attention because its physics is best understood (thanks to the volcanic analogy) and it could plausibly be deployed at global scale using a fleet of high-altitude aircraft — the National Academies report and the Parson & Keith review both treat it as the leading candidate for near-term research and, eventually, possible deployment. But "best understood" is relative: both reviews stress that major uncertainties remain about regional effects — how injected aerosols would shift rainfall patterns, monsoons, and stratospheric ozone chemistry — that are much harder to pin down than the global average cooling effect.

Why has this moved from academic curiosity to something research institutions and even a UN body are formally assessing? Both the 2021 US National Academies report and the 2023 UNEP-commissioned review make the same basic argument: given that emissions cuts are proceeding too slowly to avoid significant overshoot of climate targets, understanding solar geoengineering's risks and limits now — under careful, transparent, governed research — is preferable to a world where a country, company, or even wealthy individual attempts unilateral deployment later, under climate duress, with no prior research base to evaluate the consequences. Neither report endorses deployment; both explicitly say the technology is not ready and that emissions reductions must remain the priority. What they endorse is research, under governance frameworks that mostly do not yet exist.

That gap — real scientific momentum outpacing the international rules that would govern it — is the throughline of this week. There is no treaty, UN body, or international scientific consensus process that currently has authority over solar geoengineering research or deployment decisions. The 2023 UNEP report explicitly calls for building one. In the meantime, research funding has grown quickly, a few small outdoor experiments have already been attempted (and, in at least one high-profile US case, cancelled after public objection), and the scientific and governance conversations are, by most accounts, not moving at the same speed.

## Questions to think about

1. If solar geoengineering could meaningfully lower global temperatures within a decade, while emissions cuts take much longer to show effect, how should that speed advantage be weighed against the regional side-effect uncertainties described above?
2. The National Academies and UNEP reports both call for governed research rather than either an outright ban or unrestricted experimentation — what would need to be true of a governance framework for you to consider that middle path trustworthy?
3. Solar geoengineering does nothing about ocean acidification, since it doesn't lower CO₂ concentrations. How much should that limitation weigh in the overall case for or against researching it?

## This week's theme

**Solar geoengineering — the science, risks, and governance debates around deliberately cooling the planet.**

As greenhouse-gas emissions keep rising faster than they are being cut, a once-fringe idea has moved into mainstream scientific and policy discussion: deliberately reflecting a fraction of incoming sunlight back to space to cool the Earth. Proposals range from injecting reflective aerosols into the stratosphere to brightening marine clouds, and funding for research — and for a handful of small outdoor experiments — has surged since 2023. The idea is scientifically serious (backed by volcanic-eruption analogues and detailed climate models) and deeply contested: it does nothing about ocean acidification or the root cause of warming, its side effects on regional rainfall and ozone are still poorly constrained, and no international body currently governs who could deploy it, at what scale, or how it could be stopped. This week traces the physics, the evidence base, the live controversies, and the emerging governance debate around a technology that could plausibly be tested at meaningful scale within this decade.
