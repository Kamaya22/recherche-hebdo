# Solar geoengineering — Day 6/7: How do we actually know? The limits of models and small-scale experiments, and what real monitoring would take

## Context

This week has built its picture of solar geoengineering almost entirely from two sources: computer models (Day 3's GeoMIP intercomparisons of cooling, precipitation, and ozone effects) and a handful of small, carefully governed outdoor experiments that either ran partially or never flew at all (Day 4's account of ARIA's balloon-material platform and Harvard's cancelled SCoPEx). Day 5 then showed how much hinges on getting this right — termination shock, mitigation deterrence, and questions of who controls the technology are all, in the end, arguments about a system nobody has ever watched operate at scale. Today's reading steps back from *what* the models and experiments project and asks a more basic question: *how much should we trust what we think we know*, and what would it even take to find out for real if stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI) were ever deployed?

The honest answer, as today's three readings show, is that solar geoengineering research faces a structural epistemic problem that most climate science does not: the intervention being studied cannot be run at the scale that matters without first deciding to do the thing itself. Ordinary climate science can, in principle, wait for enough time to pass and enough data to accumulate. SAI research has to reason about a large-scale planetary intervention using only computer models (whose structural assumptions cannot be fully checked against reality), tiny outdoor experiments (which by design cannot approach deployment scale), and, if it were ever deployed, a monitoring effort that would face the same noisy climate system that makes any climate signal slow to confirm. Today's readings take each piece of that chain in turn: what kind of uncertainty limits the models (Kravitz & MacMartin), what a small-scale outdoor experiment can and cannot actually measure (Golja et al.'s study of the SCoPEx balloon plume), and how long it would take to detect an SAI signal in real-world data even after deployment (Lo et al.). Tomorrow's synthesis will ask what this means for whether and how the technology should be governed; today is about the evidentiary floor beneath that question.

## Today's readings

**Uncertainty and the basis for confidence in solar geoengineering research** (Kravitz & MacMartin, *Nature Reviews Earth & Environment*, 2020) — https://www.nature.com/articles/s43017-019-0004-7 — ~9 min (introduction and the typology of uncertainty sources). A peer-reviewed Perspective in a Nature Portfolio review journal (the same venue Day 4 referenced for the ARIA funding thread), by two of the field's most-cited modelers — Ben Kravitz (Indiana University / Pacific Northwest National Laboratory) and Douglas MacMartin (Cornell University), both long-standing GeoMIP contributors. It is the standard reference for how the field itself categorizes what is and is not known.

**Aerosol Dynamics in the Near Field of the SCoPEx Stratospheric Balloon Experiment** (Golja, Chew, Dykema & Keith, *Journal of Geophysical Research: Atmospheres*, 2021) — https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2020JD033438 — ~8 min (abstract, introduction, and conclusions). A peer-reviewed primary study in AGU's flagship atmospheric-science journal, by the Harvard team that designed SCoPEx (introduced on Day 4 as the outdoor SAI experiment cancelled in 2024 without ever flying). It models, in fine mechanistic detail, exactly what the experiment's balloon-released plume would and would not have been able to measure — useful precisely because it lets us evaluate the experiment's scientific reach independent of the governance debate that ultimately stopped it.

**Detecting sulphate aerosol geoengineering with different methods** (Lo, Charlton-Perez, Lott & Highwood, *Scientific Reports*, 2016) — https://www.nature.com/articles/srep39169 — ~7 min (abstract, introduction, and results summary). A peer-reviewed, fully open-access study in Nature's multidisciplinary megajournal, by atmospheric scientists at the University of Reading and the UK Met Office, comparing five climate models to ask how many years of observational data it would take to statistically detect that SAI was actually changing global temperature, under different detection methods.

## Guided summary

Start with Kravitz and MacMartin's typology, because it gives the vocabulary the other two readings implicitly use. They split uncertainty about SAI's climate effects into three distinct sources. *Scenario uncertainty* is about choices nobody has made yet — how much aerosol, injected where, in what pattern, to achieve what temperature target — and is not really uncertainty about the world so much as about a decision that has not been taken. *Model structural uncertainty* is the disagreement between different climate models simulating the identical scenario, rooted in different ways of representing aerosol microphysics, cloud interactions, and atmospheric circulation — this is exactly what produced the inter-model spread Day 3's GeoMIP reading quantified for cooling efficiency and tropical precipitation. *Internal variability* is the climate system's own natural noise — El Niño cycles, volcanic eruptions, decade-to-decade wobbles — which exists whether or not SAI is deployed and which can mask or mimic a forced signal. Kravitz and MacMartin's central point is that these three sources respond differently to more research: scenario uncertainty shrinks the moment a deployment decision is actually made; model structural uncertainty can shrink with better physics and more observational data to constrain parameterizations (which is exactly why the volcanic analogy from Day 2 matters so much — it is close to the only real-world validation data models have); but internal variability is a property of the climate system itself and does not go away no matter how good the models get. Confidence in SAI research, in their framing, is not a single number but depends on which of these three you are trying to reduce.

Golja and colleagues' study puts flesh on the "model structural uncertainty" problem at its smallest, most tractable scale — the resolution at which experiments like SCoPEx were meant to help. Their paper simulates, in detailed 3D fluid dynamics, what actually happens in the few kilometers immediately behind a balloon releasing calcite or sulfate aerosol into the stratosphere: a turbulent wake in which particles nucleate, grow, and coagulate before spreading into the ambient air. This near-field plume physics — how big the particles end up, how quickly they disperse — is precisely one of the "aerosol microphysics" parameters that Day 2's mechanistic review flagged as poorly constrained, and that global climate models like GeoMIP's must currently assume rather than measure. The paper's finding that injection method changes particle size outcomes substantially (a monodisperse plume from solid calcite versus a broader size distribution from condensable sulfate reaching roughly 0.1 micron average radius a few kilometers downstream) illustrates the value SCoPEx-type experiments were meant to add: real measurements of a process current models have to parameterize by assumption. But it also illustrates the sharp limit on that value. A balloon plume a few kilometers long, sampled over hours, can validate microphysics happening at that scale — it cannot, even in principle, tell you anything directly about whether continuous injection over years at a scale meant to offset a meaningful fraction of global warming would produce the cooling, precipitation, or ozone effects Day 3's models project. That gap between "we measured this specific microphysical process accurately" and "we therefore know how planetary-scale deployment behaves" cannot be closed by more or larger small-scale experiments alone — bridging it still requires the very climate models the experiment was meant to improve, which brings the argument back to Kravitz and MacMartin's second and third categories.

Lo and colleagues take the question in a different direction entirely: suppose SAI actually were deployed at scale — how would anyone know, from real observations, that it was working as intended, or producing a specific side effect, rather than what would have happened anyway? Using five climate models and a simulated scenario injecting 5 teragrams of sulfur dioxide per year, they tested several statistical detection methods against the same problem underlying Kravitz and MacMartin's "internal variability" category: separating an SAI-driven temperature signal from the climate system's own noise and from other forcings (greenhouse gases, volcanic eruptions, aerosols from ordinary air pollution) happening simultaneously. Their headline result is that the answer depends heavily on method and model: using the same multi-variate statistical approach traditionally used for detecting and attributing human-caused climate change, roughly two-thirds of their model comparisons needed 25 years or more of data before the SAI signal became statistically distinguishable from noise; a trend-based filtering approach did much better, with two-thirds needing fewer than 10 years. That range — a decade to a quarter-century, depending on which of several equally defensible statistical choices you make — is itself the finding. It means real-world monitoring of a deployed SAI program would face exactly the same noise floor that Kravitz and MacMartin identify as the one uncertainty source research cannot fully engineer away, and that a government or coalition deciding whether SAI is "working" might have to wait years to over a decade for a clear answer, all while the intervention continued.

Put the three readings together and a chain emerges that did not exist in this week's earlier, more optimistic-sounding accounts of "the models say" and "the experiment would measure." Small-scale outdoor experiments like SCoPEx can, at best, validate narrow physical processes — Golja's plume microphysics — that feed into climate models as better parameters. Climate models, even with those better parameters, retain structural disagreement between modeling centers that has not been fully resolved and, per Kravitz and MacMartin, may never be fully resolved for scenario and internal-variability reasons that have nothing to do with model quality. And even a real deployment, generating real observational data unavailable to any model or experiment, would not resolve the question quickly — Lo and colleagues' decade-plus detection timelines mean the feedback loop between "we deployed this" and "we know what it did" is far slower than the political and diplomatic decision cycles Day 5's controversies operate on. This is the sense in which SAI's evidence base differs qualitatively from, say, vaccine trials or renewable-energy rollouts, where real-world data accumulates quickly and cheaply relative to the decision at hand: here, every rung of the evidentiary ladder — model, small experiment, real deployment — is separately and irreducibly slow.

## Questions to think about

1. Kravitz and MacMartin argue that only two of their three uncertainty categories (model structural uncertainty and, indirectly, scenario uncertainty) can be shrunk by more research, while internal variability is close to irreducible. If a research program cannot ever fully resolve the third category, what would count as "enough" confidence to justify a deployment decision?
2. Golja and colleagues' study shows that SCoPEx-scale experiments can validate specific microphysical parameters but cannot, even in principle, test planetary-scale climate response. Given that limit, does a small outdoor experiment still seem like a necessary step before larger-scale field trials, or could its parameter-validation role be served just as well by laboratory chamber studies and further volcanic-eruption analysis?
3. Lo and colleagues find that detecting an SAI signal in real temperature data could take anywhere from under a decade to over 25 years depending on statistical method. If you were advising a government on whether to deploy SAI, how would you propose it commit, in advance, to which detection method and what evidence would count as grounds to stop?
