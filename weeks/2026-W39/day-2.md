# Solar geoengineering — Day 2/7: How would stratospheric aerosol injection actually work?

## Context

Yesterday's panorama named stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI) as the leading candidate among solar geoengineering methods, precisely because its physics is the best constrained of the three — thanks largely to a natural experiment nobody designed: large volcanic eruptions. Today we slow down and walk through that physics in more detail: what actually has to happen, chemically and physically, between "release a gas from an aircraft" and "a measurable fraction of sunlight is reflected back to space." This is the mechanistic backbone that every later day in this week — model projections (Day 3), field experiments (Day 4), controversies (Day 5), methodological limits (Day 6), governance (Day 7) — will keep referring back to.

The short version is that SAI is not simply "add reflective dust to the sky." It requires a specific chemical conversion, a particle-growth process that determines how efficiently the resulting aerosol scatters light, and transport by a specific, slow-moving current of stratospheric air that determines how long the effect lasts and how evenly it spreads. Each of these steps is also where the side effects flagged yesterday — regional rainfall shifts, ozone interference — actually originate, so understanding the mechanism is a prerequisite for the rest of the week, not a detour from it.

Volcanoes remain the central reference point today, but it is worth being precise about what kind of reference they are: a real-world, one-off, uncontrolled test of the same basic physics that a deliberate, continuous, tunable injection program would try to engineer more carefully. Today's readings pair a technical review of that physics with an institutional source that examines directly how good — and how limited — the volcanic analogy really is.

A short, plain-language piece from a federal earth-science agency, written by volcanologists who study eruptions for a living, directly addressing where the volcanic analogy holds and where it breaks down. Useful as a check against over-reading the analogy, from scientists with no stake in the geoengineering debate itself.

**For Stratospheric Aerosol Injection, All Strategies Are Not Created Equal** (NOAA Chemical Sciences Laboratory, 2023 news release) — https://csl.noaa.gov/news/2023/390_1107.html — ~5 min. It moves the discussion from "does the mechanism work" to "how sensitive is the mechanism to engineering choices" — a bridge to Wednesday's discussion of model projections.

## Guided summary

Stratospheric aerosol injection does not begin with aerosol at all — it begins with a precursor gas, almost always modeled as sulfur dioxide (SO₂), released from aircraft into the lower stratosphere (roughly 15–25 km up, above where weather and rain occur). SO₂ itself barely scatters sunlight. The first step is chemical: over a period the literature puts at roughly three to six weeks, atmospheric hydroxyl radicals oxidize SO₂ into gaseous sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄). That gas-phase acid is what actually becomes the reflective aerosol — but only after a second step, aerosol microphysics, converts it into liquid droplets. Some H₂SO₄ molecules nucleate entirely new droplets with ambient water vapor; the rest condense onto particles that already exist. Those droplets then grow further through condensation and through coagulation (droplets colliding and merging as they drift).

This growth stage is where engineering choices matter most, because particle size determines how efficiently the resulting aerosol layer reflects sunlight per unit of sulfur injected. Droplets with an effective radius in the sub-micron range — roughly comparable to the wavelength of visible light, a few tenths of a micrometer — scatter sunlight most efficiently for their mass (a regime governed by Mie scattering physics). Grow the particles larger than that and each unit of sulfur delivers less cooling: bigger droplets scatter less efficiently per unit mass and, just as importantly, fall out of the stratosphere faster under gravity, shortening the time they spend doing any reflecting at all. This is precisely the trade-off the NOAA CSL summary describes at the strategy level: modeled scenarios that inject at mid-latitudes (around 30°N and 30°S) rather than at the equator or poles achieve the same global cooling target with meaningfully less sulfur, in large part because the resulting particle size distribution and circulation pathway are more favorable. Where and when you inject is not a logistical afterthought — it is a lever on the microphysics itself.

Once formed, the aerosol has to go somewhere, and that "somewhere" is set by the Brewer–Dobson circulation: a slow, global overturning of stratospheric air that rises in the tropics, moves poleward, and sinks at high latitudes over the course of roughly one to two years. This circulation is why the stratosphere is the target layer in the first place, rather than the more accessible troposphere just below it: tropospheric aerosols are washed out by rain within days to weeks, while stratospheric aerosols, lofted above the weather, can persist and spread for one to two years before descending back into the troposphere and being rained out. Injection latitude therefore also determines geographic reach — equatorial injections tend to spread more evenly across both hemispheres, while injections at a single high latitude stay more confined to that hemisphere.

This is where the volcanic analogy earns its keep, and also where its limits show. Mount Pinatubo's 1991 eruption injected an estimated 15–20 megatons of SO₂ to an altitude of roughly 35–40 km; the resulting aerosol cloud encircled the globe within about three weeks and reached near-global coverage within a year, producing a measured global cooling of roughly 0.5°C that peaked about 18 months after the eruption and faded over the following one to two years as the aerosol settled out — a real-world demonstration of the entire chemical-microphysical-transport chain described above, and the reason scientists trust that the basic mechanism works at all. But as the USGS piece stresses, a volcanic eruption is a single, massive, uncontrolled pulse at one location, one altitude, and one moment, with a particle size distribution set by chance rather than design. A deliberately engineered SAI program, by contrast, would be continuous rather than pulsed, and — at least in principle — tunable in latitude, season, altitude, and injection rate to try to shape the resulting particle size and circulation pathway more favorably than an eruption does. Whether that tunability can actually be achieved with real aircraft and real atmospheric variability, rather than only in models, remains an open engineering question that later days will return to.

The same mechanism that produces cooling also produces the side effects flagged on Day 1. Sulfate aerosol does not only scatter incoming visible sunlight; it also absorbs some outgoing infrared radiation, which locally warms the stratosphere itself — observed clearly after Pinatubo — and that localized heating alters stratospheric circulation patterns, including the polar vortex. Sulfate droplet surfaces also host heterogeneous chemical reactions that activate ozone-destroying chlorine compounds, a process well documented as contributing to record Antarctic ozone depletion in the aftermath of Pinatubo. And stratospheric warming can increase stratospheric water vapor — itself a greenhouse gas — which partly offsets the intended cooling. None of these are separate problems bolted onto the reflective effect; they are the same aerosol microphysics and circulation working exactly as expected, just with consequences beyond the one that is wanted.

## Questions to think about

1. If injection strategy (latitude, season, particle size) can be engineered to reduce side effects like ozone depletion or stratospheric warming, how much should that tunability change how the risks discussed on Day 1 are weighed — versus treating it as an unproven promise until tested at scale?
2. The volcanic analogy gives real-world confidence that the basic mechanism works, but a volcanic eruption is a single uncontrolled pulse, not a continuous engineered program. Which of today's mechanistic details (chemical conversion, particle growth, transport) seem most likely to behave differently in a sustained, deliberately tuned deployment than they did after Pinatubo?
3. The same physical process (aerosol scattering incoming light while absorbing outgoing infrared) produces both the intended cooling and unintended stratospheric heating and ozone chemistry. Is it meaningful to call the side effects "avoidable" if they share a mechanism with the effect that is wanted?
