# Solar geoengineering — Day 7/7: Governance proposals and synthesis — what's settled, what's open

## Context

This week has moved from physics to politics. Day 1 named the throughline: solar geoengineering's science is advancing faster than the international rules that would govern it. Day 2 and Day 3 established what is genuinely well-constrained — the chemistry of stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI) and the rough amount of sulfur needed for a given cooling target — versus what is not: regional rainfall and monsoon shifts, and ozone-layer effects, both highly sensitive to engineering choices nobody has settled on. Day 4 showed that gap already shaping who actually gets to experiment (a well-funded, publicly governed UK programme; a rigorously governed academic experiment that never flew; an ungoverned commercial actor that has). Day 5 added three arguments for why SAI might be dangerous or unjust even if the physics works as modeled — termination shock, mitigation deterrence, and a concentration of research power in the Global North. Day 6 showed that even a real deployment would not resolve those questions quickly: models, small experiments, and real-world detection are each separately slow.

Today closes the loop by asking the question every other day deferred: given all of that, what governance actually exists or is being proposed, and does any of it look adequate to the risks this week has documented? The honest answer is that the governance conversation is now genuinely active — busier in 2025-2026 than at any point in the field's history — but it remains fragmented across competing tracks: a scientific-institution track producing assessments and disclosure norms, a legal-academic track arguing for an outright non-use agreement, and a UN-diplomatic track that has so far failed to produce a binding text. Today's readings sample each track, and the guided summary ties them to the substantive risks named on Days 1 and 5.

## Today's readings

**Towards a Non-Use Regime on Solar Geoengineering: Lessons from International Law and Governance** (Gupta, Biermann, van Driel, Bernaz, Jayaram, Kim, Kotzé, Ruddigkeit, VanDeveer & Wewerinke-Singh, *Transnational Environmental Law*, 2024, vol. 13, pp. 368–399) — https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/transnational-environmental-law/article/towards-a-nonuse-regime-on-solar-geoengineering-lessons-from-international-law-and-governance/83A71F8002DC88049D9575790743D3A1 — ~8 min (abstract and conclusions). A peer-reviewed article in a Cambridge University Press international-law journal, by a multi-institution team spanning international-relations and legal scholars (Utrecht University, Wageningen University, and collaborators in India, the US and South Africa among others). It is the fullest academic case for the "non-use agreement" position — the position that over 580 academics have now publicly endorsed — grounded in precedents from other international-law regimes.

**Solar radiation modification: Policy briefing** (The Royal Society, November 2025) — https://royalsociety.org/-/media/policy/projects/solar-radiation-modification/solar-radiation-modification-policy-briefing.pdf — ~6 min (summary and governance section). An independent scientific assessment from the UK's national academy of science — the same tier of institution as the US National Academies report read on Monday — published only weeks before this reading and explicitly evaluating monitoring and governance challenges alongside the physical science, making it the most current authoritative institutional verdict available this week.

**Solar Geoengineering and Climate Change** (Congressional Research Service, R47551, updated 2023) — https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R47551 — ~6 min (sections on international governance and US policy options). A nonpartisan report from the research arm of the US Congress (Library of Congress) — the same institution behind the CRS brief used in an earlier week's synthesis — laying out, for US lawmakers, the absence of any binding domestic or international legal framework specific to solar geoengineering and the policy options under discussion.

## Guided summary

Start with what genuinely changed since Monday's reading. Day 1 described a "gap" between scientific momentum and governance capacity as essentially static — real, acknowledged, but not obviously closing. Today's readings show that gap is now the subject of active, competing efforts to close it, on at least three separate tracks that do not agree with each other about what "closing it" should mean. The clearest illustration is the fate of a formal attempt to act at the UN level: a resolution on solar radiation modification was drafted for the UN Environment Assembly, went through several revisions, and was ultimately withdrawn after parties could not agree even on the narrower goal of commissioning an independent expert report on the technology's risks. That failure is not a footnote — it is the single most concrete piece of evidence this week has produced that intergovernmental consensus, the kind of authority the UNEP "One Atmosphere" report (Day 1) called for, remains genuinely out of reach for now, even for the modest step of formally studying the question.

Into that vacuum, two different kinds of actors have moved with two different remedies. The scientific-institution track, represented today by the Royal Society's November 2025 briefing, does what the US National Academies and UNEP reports did before it: lay out the physics and its uncertainties (largely consistent with Days 2-3's findings) and stop short of either endorsing or banning research, while stressing that SRM "could only mask, not fix" the effects of greenhouse-gas warming and cannot substitute for emissions cuts. This is a consensus-building, incrementalist track — it produces authority through repeated, convergent independent assessment rather than through any binding instrument, and it dovetails with Day 4's account of ARIA building disclosure and ethics requirements directly into its funding programme: govern research first, defer the deployment question.

The legal-academic track, represented by Gupta and colleagues' *Transnational Environmental Law* article, argues that incrementalism is itself the problem. Drawing on precedents from other domains of international law — moratoria and non-use norms that emerged in areas like deep-sea mining, human reproductive cloning, and (echoing Day 2's central analogy in a different register) nuclear weapons testing — the authors argue that a binding international non-use agreement, adopted now, before large-scale deployment capability exists, is more achievable and more protective than trying to build detailed governance rules for a technology already partially deployed. This is precisely the position behind the "International Non-Use Agreement" campaign that has gathered public backing from academics and civil-society organizations across dozens of countries, and it draws direct support from the African Ministerial Conference on the Environment's 2025 reaffirmation of its rejection of SRM — a concrete instance of the Global South political voice whose absence Day 5's justice critique (Stephens & Surprise) identified as a structural problem with the field. Where the scientific-institution track treats governance as something to build alongside research, the non-use track treats the current research trajectory itself as the thing most urgently needing a binding constraint.

The Congressional Research Service report sits between these two tracks and adds a piece neither directly addresses: domestic legal exposure. Recall Day 4's account of the EPA's Section 114 information demand against Make Sunsets — a real regulatory action, but one improvised from ordinary Clean Air Act pollution law rather than any purpose-built authority. The CRS report confirms this is not a gap specific to one company: no US federal statute, and no binding international treaty, currently has solar geoengineering specifically in its scope. Existing frameworks that touch the issue — the Convention on Biological Diversity's de facto moratorium language, the London Convention/Protocol's rules on ocean-based techniques like marine cloud brightening and ocean fertilization, and the Vienna Convention/Montreal Protocol machinery that produced Wednesday's ozone-chapter assessment — each cover a slice of the problem through instruments written for other purposes, exactly the pattern Day 4 identified for Make Sunsets at the domestic level and now visible internationally too.

Put the week together and a structure emerges that none of the individual days stated outright. The parts of solar geoengineering that are scientifically best understood (Days 2-3's cooling mechanism and required sulfur load) are not the parts driving today's governance urgency; the governance urgency is driven by the parts that are least resolved and most contested — regional side effects (Day 3), who controls deployment and termination decisions (Day 5), and how slowly any of it could be verified even after the fact (Day 6). Meanwhile, the actual institutional response to that urgency is itself split three ways, between an incrementalist assessment track, a binding-prohibition track, and a patchwork of adjacent treaties never written with this technology in mind — and the one moment this week when those tracks were tested against each other at the UN level, the attempt to even commission a shared expert report collapsed. That is a strikingly candid picture for a field to have produced about itself in the space of a year: broad agreement that governance is inadequate, and open, unresolved disagreement about what "adequate" would even look like.

## Questions to think about

1. The Royal Society and CRS reports both describe governance gaps without resolving them; the non-use agreement position argues the gap itself is the reason to prohibit further capability-building now. Given Day 4's evidence that governed research (ARIA) and ungoverned deployment (Make Sunsets) are already happening simultaneously, does a non-use agreement seem more likely to close that gap or to formalize a split between compliant and non-compliant actors?
2. The UNEA resolution collapsed over disagreement about commissioning an independent expert *report* — a step short of any binding rule. What does the difficulty of agreeing on that minimal step suggest about the prospects for the more ambitious governance instruments (a non-use treaty, a deployment framework) discussed this week?
3. Having read all seven days, which of this week's open questions — regional precipitation uncertainty (Day 3), termination-shock resilience (Day 5), detection timelines (Day 6), or the concentration of research power (Day 5) — seems most likely to be the one that actually determines whether solar geoengineering is deployed this decade, and why?

## This week's synthesis

Solar geoengineering enters 2026 as a technology whose core physical mechanism is better understood than almost any other unbuilt planetary-scale intervention — thanks to the volcanic-eruption analogue explored on Day 2 — while its regional consequences, its institutional safeguards, and its international legal status remain genuinely unsettled. What is comparatively settled: stratospheric aerosol injection can produce the intended global-mean cooling at a sulfur cost models broadly agree on (Day 3); the chemistry and transport physics behind that cooling are well characterized (Day 2); and no current research programme, however well funded, is anywhere near able to deploy the technology at climate-altering scale (Day 4). What remains genuinely open: how injection strategy trades off regional rainfall and ozone risk (Day 3); whether termination risk and mitigation deterrence can be managed by institutional design or require binding international constraint (Day 5); how long it would take to know, from real data, whether a deployed programme was working as intended (Day 6); and, after this week's reading, which of the competing governance tracks — incrementalist assessment, binding non-use, or continued patchwork — will actually shape what happens next. For anyone wanting to go deeper: the Parson & Keith review (Day 1) remains the best single entry point into the technical literature; the Solar Radiation Management Governance Initiative and the Degrees Initiative both maintain public-facing, continuously updated resources tracking the governance debate specifically; and the Gupta et al. article read today is the fullest single articulation of the case against continuing on the current trajectory at all.
