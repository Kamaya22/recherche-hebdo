# Solar geoengineering — Day 5/7: Termination shock, moral hazard, and who bears the risk

## Context

The first four days of this week moved from physics to practice: what solar geoengineering is (Day 1), how stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI) would mechanistically work (Day 2), what climate models project about its cooling potential and side effects (Day 3), and who is actually funding and running research today — a spectrum from the UK's publicly governed ARIA programme to the ungoverned commercial releases of Make Sunsets (Day 4). Day 4 ended by naming a gap between scientific capability and governance capacity that is "currently determining, in practice, which experiments get built with oversight, which get built without it, and which never get built at all." Today's reading is about what fills that gap with genuine controversy rather than just administrative untidiness: three arguments, each backed by its own peer-reviewed literature, about why solar geoengineering might be dangerous or unjust even if the physics eventually works as modeled.

The three controversies threaded together today — termination shock, moral hazard (more precisely, "mitigation deterrence"), and the justice implications of who controls the technology — are conceptually distinct but mutually reinforcing. Termination shock is a physical risk: what happens if SAI is switched off abruptly. Mitigation deterrence is a political-economy risk: whether the mere existence of SAI as an option changes how hard societies work to cut emissions. The justice critique is about power: who gets to research, propose, and eventually control a technology capable of altering the whole planet's climate, and who bears the consequences if it goes wrong or is switched off. Make Sunsets, introduced yesterday as a company already conducting unilateral stratospheric releases with no governance framework, turns out to be a live test case for all three at once — which is why today's readings keep returning to it.

## Today's readings

**The Risk of Termination Shock From Solar Geoengineering** (Parker & Irvine, *Earth's Future*, 2018) — https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/2017EF000735 — ~8 min (abstract, introduction, and conclusions). A peer-reviewed research article in *Earth's Future*, a journal of the American Geophysical Union, by two researchers then at Harvard's Solar Geoengineering Research Program. It is the first systematic scenario analysis of what could actually cause SAI to be terminated abruptly, and remains the most-cited treatment of the topic.

**Mitigation Deterrence and the "Moral Hazard" of Solar Radiation Management** (McLaren, *Earth's Future*, 2016) — https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/2016EF000445 — ~6 min (abstract, introduction, and the typology of hazards). Also peer-reviewed in *Earth's Future*, by Duncan McLaren (then at Lancaster University), this is the paper that reframed the informal "moral hazard" worry into the more precise concept of "mitigation deterrence" now standard in the field's own vocabulary.

**The Hidden Injustices of Advancing Solar Geoengineering Research** (Stephens & Surprise, *Global Sustainability*, 2020) — https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/global-sustainability/article/hidden-injustices-of-advancing-solar-geoengineering-research/F61C5DCBCA02E18F66CAC7E45CC76C57 — ~6 min (abstract and conclusion). A peer-reviewed article in *Global Sustainability* (Cambridge University Press) by Jennie C. Stephens (Northeastern University) and Kevin Surprise (Williams College), examining who funds and conducts solar geoengineering research and what that concentration implies for who would control eventual deployment decisions.

## Guided summary

Start with termination shock, the physical risk. If SAI were deployed at a scale sufficient to meaningfully offset warming and then stopped suddenly — rather than being wound down gradually — the greenhouse-gas warming it had been masking would reassert itself over just a few years instead of over the decades it took to accumulate. Ecosystems and human systems that might have adapted to gradual warming would instead face a compressed, rapid temperature jump layered on top of whatever warming had continued underneath. Parker and Irvine's contribution was not to model the climate impact of such a jump — earlier modeling studies had already done that — but to ask a question that had been oddly neglected: how likely is termination shock actually to happen, and from what causes? They ran a scenario analysis of plausible "driver events": armed conflict disrupting the physical infrastructure (aircraft fleets, in most SAI designs) needed to sustain injection; a global depression collapsing the funding base; a strengthening of climate science suggesting SAI's side effects (recall the ozone and precipitation findings from Day 3) outweigh its benefits; and, especially relevant given yesterday's reading, unilateral withdrawal by whichever single actor was actually running the injection program, whether a national government or, in a future version of Make Sunsets at scale, a private company. Their headline finding cuts against the more alarmist framing the concept sometimes receives in public debate: most of these drivers, they argue, are not inherently abrupt, and a research and deployment program designed with foresight — geographically distributed infrastructure, financial reserves, a formal wind-down protocol — could be made resilient against all but the most extreme, low-probability catastrophes (a global nuclear war, for instance). Termination shock, in their reading, is a real risk that argues for careful institutional design, not a decisive argument that SAI is inherently unmanageable.

Mitigation deterrence is a different kind of risk — not physical but behavioral and political. The intuitive worry, informally called "moral hazard," is that once people believe a technological fix for warming exists, they will feel less urgency about the harder, more expensive work of cutting emissions. McLaren's 2016 paper is the field's standard reference here because it does not just assert the worry but takes it apart. He distinguishes moral hazard operating at the individual level (a single person emitting more because they believe geoengineering will compensate), the social level (a society's collective political will to decarbonize weakening), and the political level (governments or fossil-fuel interests actively using the prospect of SAI to justify delaying mitigation policy — what he separately terms "regulatory capture" risk, as distinct from simple "technical-dependence" risk). This matters because the empirical evidence for the different levels is genuinely mixed: subsequent behavioral studies have found that simply telling people geoengineering exists does not reliably make them personally reduce their own mitigation effort — the individual-level effect is weak or inconsistent in lab studies. But McLaren's key point is that the political-level version does not require individual psychology to work at all: a government or company only needs to invoke the *possibility* of future geoengineering to justify weaker near-term emissions targets, whether or not deployment ever happens and whether or not the public is individually complacent. That distinction — between a hazard that has to be measured in people's heads and one that operates directly in policy and lobbying — is why "mitigation deterrence" has replaced the vaguer "moral hazard" as the field's preferred term.

The justice critique, from Stephens and Surprise, starts from an empirical observation rather than a hypothetical mechanism: who, concretely, is doing solar geoengineering research today. Their answer, built from mapping funders and lead researchers, is that the field is dominated by a small number of researchers concentrated at elite universities in the Global North, funded disproportionately by a handful of wealthy individuals and their private foundations rather than by broad-based public science budgets. That concentration would matter less if solar geoengineering were a narrow technical question, but by its nature it is not — deploying it, or refusing to, is a decision that would shift climate risk (rainfall patterns, monsoon timing, regional temperature) across the entire globe, with the most climate-vulnerable populations, disproportionately in the Global South, least represented among the people currently deciding whether and how to research it. Stephens and Surprise go further than a diversity critique: they argue the research community's own framing — emphasizing rapid, decisive, top-down deployment scenarios in the face of climate emergency — echoes a "militarized" logic of intervention that tends to sideline slower, more participatory, more genuinely global forms of governance. Read alongside Day 4's account of Make Sunsets — a small, unaccountable, Global-North-based company already unilaterally injecting material into the shared stratosphere and selling the result as a commercial product — their argument stops being abstract: it is a concrete illustration of exactly the concentration-of-control problem they describe, operating with no international governance body positioned to stop it, exactly as the UNEP and National Academies reports (Day 1) warned.

Put the three controversies together and a common structure emerges. Termination shock is what happens if the people running SAI stop, deliberately or not — and that risk gets worse in proportion to how few, how unaccountable, and how narrowly resourced those people are, which is exactly Stephens and Surprise's concern. Mitigation deterrence is a risk that grows the more solar geoengineering is presented, by whoever controls its research agenda, as a viable substitute for emissions cuts rather than a stopgap alongside them — which makes who sets that framing (McLaren's "political" moral hazard) inseparable from who funds and leads the research (Stephens and Surprise's concern again). None of today's three papers concludes that solar geoengineering research should stop; Parker and Irvine argue termination risk is manageable with foresight, and McLaren's point is that the moral-hazard objection, while real, is not automatically a knockout argument against research. But all three converge on the same practical implication that Day 4 already hinted at and that tomorrow's reading on research methods will probe further: the quality of the institutions and the breadth of the people governing this technology may matter as much to the eventual outcome as the atmospheric chemistry itself.

## Questions to think about

1. Parker and Irvine conclude that most termination-shock drivers could be managed with resilient institutional design — but Stephens and Surprise's evidence suggests the people currently designing SAI research programs are a narrow, Global-North-concentrated group. Does that concentration make Parker and Irvine's optimism about manageable termination risk more or less credible?
2. McLaren separates "moral hazard" from "mitigation deterrence" partly because the political-level risk does not require any individual to actually feel complacent — only for a government or company to invoke the possibility of geoengineering to justify weaker climate policy. Can you think of a real climate-policy debate (on any technology) where a similar dynamic has already played out?
3. Make Sunsets appeared in Day 4 as a governance-gap case study; today it doubles as an example of both unilateral termination risk and the justice critique's "who controls this" problem. If a single well-resourced actor — a government, a billionaire, or a company — decided to deploy SAI at climate-altering scale without international agreement, what would the rest of the world's actual options be to respond?
