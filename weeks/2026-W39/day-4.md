# Solar geoengineering — Day 4/7: Who is actually doing solar geoengineering research today?

## Context

The first three days of this week built up the science from first principles: what solar geoengineering is and why it is being taken seriously (Day 1), the chemistry and physics of how stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI) would actually work (Day 2), and what climate models project about its cooling potential and side effects (Day 3). All of that has, so far, been either modeling or laboratory chemistry — deliberately so, since the field has almost no real-world outdoor data beyond the volcanic analogy. Today the question shifts from "what would happen" to "what is actually happening, right now, and who is doing it." That turns out to be a more contested question than the physics, because the answer spans a well-funded, formally governed public research programme in the UK; a small, controversial academic experiment that was designed, debated for five years, and then cancelled without ever flying; and at least one commercial actor releasing material into the stratosphere with no governance framework at all and now facing a US federal regulatory inquiry.

Money is also moving faster than it was even a year or two ago. Public funding for solar radiation modification (SRM) research — cumulative since 2007 — only recently crossed roughly $340 million globally, of which about half came from public sources; 2025 alone saw a comparable jump, with the UK's government-backed innovation agency alone committing tens of millions of pounds to a single new programme. That funding surge, and the shape it is taking, is the throughline of today's reading: who is spending this money, on what kind of research (indoor modeling versus outdoor experiments), and under what oversight — because, as we will see, the answer to "under what oversight" varies enormously between actors, and that variance is itself one of the central open problems the rest of this week (controversies on Day 5, methods on Day 6, governance on Day 7) will keep returning to.

## Guided summary

Start with the largest and most visible actor: ARIA, a UK government-backed research-funding agency modeled loosely on the US's DARPA. In May 2025 it announced £56.8 million across 22 projects under its "Exploring Climate Cooling" programme, spanning atmospheric physics, chemistry, climate modelling, ecology, and governance and ethics — the last two categories a deliberate signal that ARIA is treating oversight as part of the science, not an afterthought. Most of the 22 projects are indoor modelling, laboratory, or desk-based governance work, but five involve small-scale, controlled outdoor experiments: attempts to re-thicken Arctic sea ice by pumping seawater onto its surface (the largest single grant, reportedly around £9.9 million over 42 months, to the University of Cambridge's Centre for Climate Repair), marine-cloud-brightening trials near Australia's Great Barrier Reef and in UK waters, and — closest to this week's central topic of stratospheric aerosol injection — a project using a purpose-built balloon platform to expose small samples of candidate reflective materials to real stratospheric conditions and then safely recover them, to measure how those materials actually age at altitude rather than relying on model assumptions. ARIA has published its own oversight-and-governance framework alongside the funding, including public disclosure requirements for each grantee — an explicit attempt to avoid the pattern described next.

That pattern is what happened to SCoPEx, Harvard's own proposed outdoor SAI experiment, and the *Science* Policy Forum is essentially a post-mortem written by the people who spent five years trying to govern it well. SCoPEx, led by David Keith and Frank Keutsch, was conceived as a small balloon-borne test releasing tiny quantities of material into the stratosphere to measure — not simulate — how a resulting aerosol plume behaves, filling exactly the mechanistic gap Day 2 described between volcanic analogues and deliberately engineered particles. Rather than simply seeking a launch permit, Harvard convened an independent external advisory committee in 2019 specifically to design a governance process for the experiment: public engagement, an independent physical-risk assessment, and local consultation before any launch. That process was, by the standards of the field, unusually careful — and it still took five years, a planned test flight in Sweden was cancelled in 2021 after opposition from Indigenous groups and environmental organizations, and the project was formally abandoned in March 2024 without ever releasing anything into the stratosphere. The Policy Forum's authors do not read this as proof that governance is impossible; they read it as evidence that governance frameworks need to exist *before* a specific project is proposed, rather than being built reactively project-by-project the way SCoPEx's was — a recommendation that anticipates ARIA's own published-in-advance oversight framework, and a thread this week will pick back up on Day 7.

Set against both of those — a well-funded, publicly governed programme, and a rigorously governed but ultimately cancelled academic experiment — is Make Sunsets, which has done more actual stratospheric injection than either. Founded in 2022 by two entrepreneurs, the company releases weather balloons carrying small quantities of sulfur dioxide, timed to burst in the stratosphere, and sells the resulting modeled cooling effect commercially as "cooling credits." It has conducted well over a hundred such releases since 2023 and has already been banned from operating in Mexico. In April 2025, the US EPA's Office of Air and Radiation issued it a formal Section 114 information demand under the Clean Air Act — a legal mechanism ordinarily used to compel industrial polluters to disclose emissions data — seeking information on where the balloons are launched, the source and quantity of the sulfur dioxide released, and whether the company had ever contacted state, local, or federal air regulators before doing so. The EPA's action was framed around conventional air-quality law (sulfur dioxide is a regulated criteria air pollutant, independent of any climate-engineering context) rather than any dedicated geoengineering statute, because no such statute exists in the US or anywhere else — which is precisely the regulatory gap the *Science* Policy Forum and, on Monday's reading, the UNEP and National Academies reports were each, in their own way, warning about.

Put the three actors side by side and a clear asymmetry emerges. The best-funded, most institutionally embedded outdoor research (ARIA's programme) is also the most heavily governed, with public disclosure and an ethics-and-governance workstream built in from the start. The most carefully, deliberately governed single experiment ever proposed (SCoPEx) never actually flew, in part because building consensus-based governance from scratch, project by project, proved slower than the controversy it was meant to manage. And the actor that has actually put the most sulfur into the stratosphere to date operated with essentially no external governance at all until a national environmental regulator intervened under a law never written with geoengineering in mind. None of this tells us whether SAI research should continue, or at what pace — that normative question is squarely Friday's territory. What today's reading establishes is more basic: the gap between scientific capability and governance capacity that Day 1 flagged as this week's throughline is not hypothetical. It is currently determining, in practice, which experiments get built with oversight, which get built without it, and which never get built at all.

## Questions to think about

1. ARIA funded governance and ethics work as part of the same programme as the physical experiments, while SCoPEx tried to build a governance process around one already-proposed experiment. Based on today's reading, does funding governance work *alongside* research seem to solve the problem the SCoPEx advisory committee identified, or does it just relocate where the slowdown happens?
2. The EPA's intervention against Make Sunsets used ordinary air-pollution law, not any geoengineering-specific authority. What would a purpose-built regulatory framework need to cover that Section 114 of the Clean Air Act cannot?
3. SCoPEx spent five years on governance and never flew; Make Sunsets has conducted well over a hundred releases with none. If you were designing a policy response, would you prioritize making careful governance faster, or making unauthorized deployment harder — and would those two priorities ever conflict?
