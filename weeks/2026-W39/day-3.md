# Solar geoengineering — Day 3/7: What do climate models actually project — cooling, precipitation, and ozone?

## Context

Yesterday walked through the physical mechanism of stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI): how a sulfur-dioxide precursor becomes reflective sulfate aerosol, how particle size and injection strategy shape its efficiency, and how the same microphysics that produces cooling also produces stratospheric heating and ozone chemistry side effects. Today we ask a narrower, more practical question: when climate scientists actually run global climate models with this mechanism switched on, what do they project — does it cool the planet as intended, and how large, and how uncertain, are the side effects?

The standard way researchers answer this is the Geoengineering Model Intercomparison Project (GeoMIP), a coordinated experiment design in which multiple independent climate modeling centers run the same SAI scenario through their own Earth system models and compare results. Its current phase, GeoMIP6, asks each model to inject enough sulfate (in experiment "G6sulfur") to bring a high-warming future (the SSP5-8.5 scenario) down to the surface-temperature trajectory of a medium-warming future (SSP2-4.5) — and runs a parallel experiment, "G6solar," that achieves the same temperature target by simply dimming the sun in the model, to isolate what is specific to aerosols versus what is a generic consequence of cooling the planet by any means. Separately, because stratospheric ozone chemistry is governed by an international treaty, the Montreal Protocol's own scientific panel commissioned a dedicated assessment of SAI's ozone effects — the closest thing to an authoritative, treaty-linked verdict on that specific side effect.

Today's readings cover both threads: the multi-model GeoMIP results on cooling efficiency and precipitation, and the ozone-specific assessment. Both matter for the rest of the week — Thursday's look at current field experiments and funding, and Friday's controversies, both turn on how confident (or not) modelers are in these projections.

## Today's readings

**Identifying the sources of uncertainty in climate model simulations of solar radiation modification with the G6sulfur and G6solar Geoengineering Model Intercomparison Project (GeoMIP) simulations** (Visioni, MacMartin, Kravitz, Boucher, Jones, Lurton, Michou, Mills, Nabat, Niemeier, Séférian & Tilmes, *Atmospheric Chemistry and Physics*, 2021) — https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/21/10039/2021/ — ~9 min (abstract, and sections on injection rates and inter-model spread). A peer-reviewed, fully open-access primary study in a Copernicus/European Geosciences Union journal (the same venue as Tuesday's reading on injection strategy), reporting results from six Earth system models running the standardized GeoMIP6 experiments. It is the closest thing to a "how much sulfur, and how confidently do models agree" reference for SAI as currently studied.

**Chapter 6 — Stratospheric Aerosol Injection and its Potential Effect on the Stratospheric Ozone Layer**, in *Scientific Assessment of Ozone Depletion: 2022* (Haywood & Tilmes, lead authors; GAW Report No. 278, World Meteorological Organization, Geneva, 2022) — https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/downloads/Chapter6_2022OzoneAssessment.pdf — ~8 min (chapter summary). Commissioned by the Montreal Protocol's Scientific Assessment Panel — the same expert process that has tracked ozone-layer recovery for decades — this was the first time that assessment series devoted a full chapter to SAI, specifically because treaty parties asked for it. Freely available from NOAA's Chemical Sciences Laboratory, which co-produces the assessment.

**Energetic constraints on tropical precipitation changes under stratospheric aerosol geoengineering: a topical review** (Xavier, Bala & Kallihosur, *Environmental Research Letters*, 2026) — https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/ae6714 — ~8 min (introduction and synthesis sections). A peer-reviewed, fully open-access topical review in a well-established IOP Publishing journal, synthesizing the now sizeable literature on why and how stratospheric aerosol geoengineering changes tropical rainfall, organized around the physical energy-budget constraints that drive those changes.

## Guided summary

Start with the headline result that is, relatively speaking, the least controversial: models broadly agree that SAI can produce the intended global-mean cooling, and they broadly agree on the order of magnitude of sulfur required to do it. In the GeoMIP6 G6sulfur experiment, the six participating Earth system models needed, on average, about 29 ± 9 teragrams of SO₂ per year by the 2080s–2090s to hold global temperature at the SSP2-4.5 trajectory instead of letting it follow the more severe SSP5-8.5 path — roughly one to two Pinatubo-sized eruptions' worth of sulfur, injected continuously, every year, by the end of the century. The ± 9 spread is itself informative: different models need different amounts of sulfur to achieve the same cooling because they represent aerosol microphysics (the particle-growth process described on Day 2) somewhat differently, so the same injection produces aerosol layers of different reflective efficiency in different models. That is real scientific uncertainty, but it is uncertainty about a dial setting, not about whether the mechanism works at all.

The picture gets far less settled once the question shifts from "does it cool the planet" to "what else does it do." The Xavier, Bala & Kallihosur review lays out why global average temperature and global average precipitation are not simply linked: precipitation responds to the atmosphere's overall energy budget, not just to surface temperature, and that budget has two distinct components with different timescales. A "fast" component kicks in almost immediately: the aerosol layer itself absorbs and scatters radiation within the atmosphere, which changes atmospheric heating independently of any change in surface temperature, and this directly suppresses convection and rainfall. A "slow" component operates the way most people intuitively expect climate change to work: as the surface cools, evaporation drops, and the hydrological cycle weakens in step with temperature. Because SAI's whole purpose is to produce surface cooling without touching the atmospheric aerosol load the way, say, simply having fewer greenhouse gases would, both components operate at once — and an earlier, now-classic study (Ferraro, Highwood & Charlton-Perez, *Environmental Research Letters*, 2014) showed that this fast component is specific to aerosol-based cooling: when the same temperature target is instead achieved in a model by turning down the sun ("solar dimming," the G6solar comparison case), the fast adjustment and its associated circulation weakening largely disappear. In other words, the precipitation side effect is not a generic consequence of geoengineered cooling — it is specifically a consequence of doing that cooling with stratospheric aerosols rather than by some other hypothetical method, which is one reason the field insists on modeling SAI directly rather than treating simple solar-dimming experiments as an adequate stand-in.

The second energetic constraint the review discusses concerns not the global total but its geographic distribution: interhemispheric asymmetries in the aerosol layer shift the energy balance between the Northern and Southern Hemispheres, which in turn shifts the position of the tropical rain belt (the intertropical convergence zone, ITCZ) — the band of heaviest tropical rainfall, and the climatological anchor for monsoon systems. Because injection latitude and seasonal timing determine how aerosol is distributed between hemispheres (the same engineering levers discussed on Day 2), the same total sulfur load can shift the ITCZ, and therefore monsoon rainfall, in different directions depending on how it is deployed. This is precisely the mechanism at work in modeling studies of the South Asian summer monsoon under SAI (reported, for instance, in *npj Climate and Atmospheric Science*, 2024): monsoon rainfall responses show roughly four times more disagreement between models than an equivalent solar-dimming experiment does, and a large share of that disagreement traces back to how each model's aerosol scheme distributes the injected sulfate between the equator and the monsoon region — less aerosol loading directly over South Asia, relative to the equator, tends to favor more insolation and more rainfall there, and vice versa. The takeaway echoes yesterday's theme: injection strategy is not a peripheral engineering detail layered on top of settled science — it is a direct lever on which regions get more or less rain.

Stratospheric ozone is the side effect furthest along in formal assessment, precisely because it falls under an existing international science process. The WMO/UNEP ozone assessment's new SAI chapter concludes that even a gradual, phased-in sulfur injection program produces an abrupt deepening of the Antarctic ozone hole within its first decade, reducing total column ozone there by roughly 8–20% relative to a no-SAI baseline, with the exact figure depending heavily on injection strategy (latitude, altitude, and rate). The mechanism is the same heterogeneous chemistry flagged on Day 2 — sulfate droplet surfaces host reactions that activate ozone-destroying chlorine compounds, a process already well documented after Pinatubo — but sustained, continuous injection means that chemistry operates continuously rather than fading out over a year or two, which is why the assessment also flags a plausible delay in the ozone layer's ongoing recovery under the Montreal Protocol, a recovery that is otherwise one of the qualified success stories of international environmental governance. The chapter is explicit, though, that this is assessed with real knowledge gaps: how ozone chemistry responds depends sensitively on injection strategy choices that are themselves still being explored in models, and the panel stops short of a single confident number for the "true" ozone cost of SAI.

Put together, today's readings sharpen yesterday's picture rather than overturning it. The part of SAI that is best constrained — global-mean cooling, and the rough quantity of sulfur needed to achieve it — is also the part that is least controversial among modelers. The parts that are least constrained — regional precipitation and monsoon shifts, and ozone-layer effects — are also the parts most sensitive to exactly how (where, when, how much) an injection program is engineered, meaning the uncertainty is not simply a matter of running bigger or better models. It is baked into the fact that "SAI" is not one scenario but a large design space, and different points in that space plausibly do meaningfully different things to different regions.

## Questions to think about

1. Models agree reasonably well on how much sulfur is needed for a given global cooling target, but disagree much more on regional precipitation and monsoon outcomes. If you were deciding whether SAI research should continue, how much weight should that gap between "well-constrained global effect" and "poorly-constrained regional effects" carry?
2. The Ferraro et al. finding that aerosol-specific cooling behaves differently from simple solar dimming suggests that some geoengineering research using simplified model setups may not transfer cleanly to a real SAI scenario. What does that imply about how much confidence to place in older or simpler modeling studies relative to the full aerosol-microphysics GeoMIP experiments?
3. The WMO/UNEP ozone assessment ties a specific, quantified risk (8–20% Antarctic column ozone loss) to a program already operating under the same international framework that oversaw the ozone layer's recovery. Does the existence of that framework make you more or less confident that ozone risk, specifically, could be managed responsibly compared to the precipitation and monsoon risks, which have no comparable treaty body?
