# Lithium-ion battery science and the energy storage transition — Day 4/7: Supply chains and critical minerals

## Context

Days 1–3 treated a lithium-ion cell mostly as a chemistry and engineering problem: what it's made of, how it charges and ages, and how it fails. But Day 1 already flagged the detail that reframes everything today — the cathode choice is not just a performance trade-off, it is a *minerals* trade-off. NMC and NCA chemistries, favored for their higher energy density, depend on nickel and cobalt. LFP, favored for cost and safety (confirmed experimentally on Day 3), needs neither. That single design choice ripples all the way down into which countries' mines, and which companies' refineries, end up supplying a given battery pack.

Today looks at where those minerals actually come from, how concentrated that supply is, and what the human and environmental costs of extraction look like on the ground — using cobalt mining in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) and nickel mining in Indonesia as the two best-documented case studies. It also introduces a distinction that the mining headlines often blur: *where ore is mined* and *where it is refined into battery-grade chemicals* are frequently two different countries, with different geopolitical implications.

This matters because mineral concentration, unlike a chemistry limitation, is not fixable by better engineering alone — it is a function of geology, decades of industrial investment, and in some cases deliberate policy. That makes it one of the harder parts of the energy transition to solve quickly, and sets up Day 5 (recycling, which reduces primary-mineral demand) and Day 6 (alternative chemistries, which reduce dependence on the most concentrated minerals specifically).

## Today's readings (~23 min)

**"Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2025"** (International Energy Agency, 2025) — https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2025 — ~8 min (executive summary). The IEA's institutional annual assessment of supply, demand, and investment across copper, lithium, nickel, cobalt, graphite, and rare earths, with a specific focus this year on how concentrated supply chains remain despite diversification efforts. Like the IEA report used on Day 1, treat it as the IEA's own policy-oriented analysis rather than a neutral academic consensus — but it is currently the most comprehensive, regularly updated public dataset on this exact question, which is why it anchors today's reading.

**"Assessing impacts of responsible sourcing initiatives for cobalt: Insights from a case study"** (Lucia Mancini, Nicolas A. Eslava, Marzia Traverso & Fabrice Mathieux, *Resources Policy*, vol. 71, article 102015, 2021, open access, CC BY) — https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resourpol.2021.102015 — ~9 min. A peer-reviewed study by researchers at the European Commission's Joint Research Centre and RWTH Aachen University, combining primary field data with Social Life Cycle Assessment methodology to evaluate two pilot "responsible sourcing" programs for artisanal cobalt mining in the DRC's Lualaba province. Trustworthy because it reports primary stakeholder data rather than secondary claims, and is candid about where the programs it studies fall short.

**"Nickel mining reduced forest cover in Indonesia, but had mixed outcomes for well-being"** (Lo, Santika, Mumbunan, Davies et al., *One Earth*, vol. 7, issue 11, pp. 2019–2033, 2024, open access, CC BY 4.0) — https://doi.org/10.1016/j.oneear.2024.10.010 — ~6 min. A peer-reviewed counterfactual study (Cell Press journal) comparing 7,721 Sulawesi villages between 2011 and 2018 to isolate nickel mining's specific effect on deforestation and local well-being, rather than just reporting correlations. Trustworthy for the same reason the Day 3 ARC calorimetry study was: it reports a controlled empirical comparison, not a modeled estimate or an anecdote.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with where the minerals that matter most for lithium-ion batteries actually come from, because the geography is strikingly lopsided. Cobalt — needed for NMC and NCA cathodes — is overwhelmingly a Congolese story: the Democratic Republic of the Congo supplies roughly three-quarters of the world's mined cobalt, a share that has been stable or rising for years because no other known deposit comes close in scale. Nickel — also needed for NMC/NCA — has become an Indonesian story even more recently and even more dramatically: Indonesia's share of global mine production rose from a minority share a decade ago to over 60% by 2024, almost entirely on the back of a government-driven push to build domestic processing capacity rather than export raw ore. Lithium, by contrast, is somewhat more geographically spread (Australia, Chile, China, and a growing Argentine supply), which is one reason LFP's lithium-only mineral footprint, alongside its safety and cost advantages from Day 3, has made it increasingly attractive for grid storage and now many EVs — it opts out of the two most concentrated mineral dependencies entirely.

Mining concentration is only half the picture, though, and the IEA's 2025 Global Critical Minerals Outlook makes the case that *refining* concentration is in some ways the more acute vulnerability. Turning mined ore into battery-grade chemicals (lithium carbonate or hydroxide, cobalt sulfate, nickel sulfate) is a separate, capital-intensive industrial step, and China has come to dominate it regardless of where the ore itself is dug up — including a large share of the refining capacity for Indonesian nickel and Congolese cobalt, built through Chinese corporate investment in those countries' processing plants. The IEA's report finds that the average share of key energy-transition minerals refined by just the top three producing countries rose from about 82% in 2020 to 86% in 2024, and projects it will fall only slightly, to around 82%, by 2035 even after accounting for diversification efforts currently underway — essentially back to where it started. The report frames the risk less as "running out" of these minerals and more as the possibility of sudden, geopolitically-driven supply disruptions (export restrictions, trade disputes) hitting an industry with very few alternative suppliers to pivot to on short notice.

The Mancini et al. study takes us down from that macro picture into what mining the cobalt actually looks like for the people doing it. A substantial share of DRC cobalt, by some industry estimates roughly a quarter, comes from artisanal and small-scale mining (ASM) — informal, often unmechanized digging, frequently criticized for child labor, unsafe working conditions, and toxic exposure to cobalt dust and heavy metals. The study's core contribution is evaluating, with primary field data rather than assumption, whether "responsible sourcing" pilot programs (which certify and monitor specific artisanal sites so that downstream battery and EV manufacturers can buy from them with fewer human-rights concerns) actually work. The honest answer is: partially. The programs studied were reasonably effective at addressing the most visible, severe harms — child labor, life-threatening working conditions, outright corruption — which is a genuine achievement given how entrenched those problems have historically been. But the authors found the programs were designed around the risk categories that matter most to downstream *buyers* (reputational and legal risk), which meant they systematically underweighted what miners themselves prioritized: fair income and gender-specific working conditions. In other words, a mine can become "responsibly sourced" by the metrics that satisfy a battery manufacturer's audit while the miners' own economic and gender-equity concerns go largely unaddressed — a gap between compliance and genuine improvement worth holding onto whenever "responsibly sourced" minerals are mentioned in a corporate sustainability claim.

The Lo et al. study does the equivalent work for Indonesian nickel, and its counterfactual design (comparing mining villages to carefully matched non-mining villages, rather than just observing mining villages alone) lets it isolate causal effects rather than correlations. The headline finding is that deforestation rates in villages with nickel mining roughly doubled relative to similar non-mining villages between 2011 and 2018 — a direct environmental cost of the push to dominate global nickel refining described above. But the well-being findings are genuinely mixed, which is worth taking seriously rather than flattening into a simple "mining is bad" story: health outcomes, infrastructure, and social relations in mining villages actually *improved* relative to the comparison villages, plausibly through new jobs, roads, and local investment. What worsened were environmental well-being, living standards broadly, and education — and the environmental decline specifically got worse over time, concentrated in villages that were already poorer before mining arrived. That pattern — some real local benefits, concentrated environmental costs that compound with poverty — is a more complicated and more policy-relevant picture than either "resource curse" or "development opportunity" framings alone capture.

Put together, these three readings suggest that "critical mineral supply chain risk" is really at least three distinct problems wearing one label: a *geopolitical* problem (mining and especially refining concentrated in few countries, explored by the IEA), a *labor and human-rights* problem (specific to how artisanal cobalt mining is organized in the DRC, explored by Mancini et al.), and a *local environmental and development* problem (specific to how industrial nickel mining reshapes Indonesian forest landscapes and village economies, explored by Lo et al.). Policy responses that work for one — say, certifying supply chains to address labor risk — do very little for the other two, which is part of why this remains such a persistently hard problem for an industry growing as fast as the one Day 1's IEA battery-deployment figures described.

## Questions to think about

- The IEA frames critical-mineral concentration mainly as an energy-security and geopolitical risk. The Mancini et al. and Lo et al. studies suggest the more immediate costs are borne by miners and local communities rather than by the countries buying the batteries. How should responsibility and the cost of fixing these problems be divided between mineral-producing and battery-consuming countries?
- Mancini et al. found that "responsible sourcing" programs address the risks that matter most to downstream buyers, while underweighting miners' own priorities (income, gender equity). What would a certification scheme designed primarily around miners' stated priorities look like, and would buyers still trust it?
- Lo et al. found real local improvements in health and infrastructure alongside worsening environmental well-being, especially in already-poor villages. If you were an Indonesian policymaker, what single intervention would you prioritize to change that trade-off — and would it need to come from the mining companies, the national government, or local government?
