# Lithium-ion battery science and the energy storage transition — Day 5/7: Recycling and the circular economy

## Context

Day 4 left off on a genuinely hard problem: cobalt, nickel, and to a lesser extent lithium are mined and refined in a handful of places, under conditions that carry real human and environmental costs, and that concentration cannot be fixed by better engineering alone. Today looks at the other lever available to reduce that pressure — not digging up less, but putting back into the loop what has already been dug up once. Every lithium-ion cell that reaches end of life is, in principle, a small deposit of lithium, cobalt, nickel, copper, and graphite that a mine does not need to produce from scratch. Whether that principle becomes practice at scale is this week's open question.

It is also a natural place to return to the chemistry from Day 2 and the failure modes from Day 3, but viewed in reverse. Day 2 described how the solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) thickens, how graphite particles crack, and how cathode structure degrades cycle after cycle; recycling is, in effect, the industrial-scale attempt to either reverse that degradation (repair the structure and reuse it) or dismantle it back down to raw elements and start over. Day 3's cathode-chemistry comparisons (NMC/NCA versus LFP) also resurface here, because what a cell is made of determines both how valuable it is to recycle and how hard it is to do so — cobalt- and nickel-rich cathodes are economically attractive to recover, while cobalt-free LFP, now dominant in grid storage and an increasing share of EVs, is currently far less profitable to recycle, which is itself becoming a policy problem.

Today's readings cover three distinct technology families for getting materials back out of a dead battery, an attempt to put numbers on how far the field has actually come, and the regulatory push — principally from the European Union — trying to force the industry to close the loop faster than market economics alone would. This sets up Day 6 (alternative chemistries, several of which will need their own, not-yet-mature recycling pathways) and Day 7's synthesis of what is settled versus open across the whole week.

## Today's readings (~21 min)

**"Lithium-Ion Battery Recycling in the Circular Economy: A Review"** (Md Tasbirul Islam & Usha Iyer-Raniga, *Recycling*, 7(3), article 33, 2022, open access, MDPI) — https://doi.org/10.3390/recycling7030033 — ~9 min. A peer-reviewed systematic review synthesizing 93 articles (66 original research papers and 27 reviews) drawn from the Web of Science core collection, mapping the state of lithium-ion battery recycling research against circular-economy principles. Trustworthy as a broad orientation because it is a large-sample systematic review rather than a single lab's perspective, and it is unusually candid about where the research literature itself is thin (policy and economics) rather than only reporting what is well studied (recovery chemistry).

**"Advancing hydrometallurgical recycling of spent lithium-ion batteries: an AI-based readiness and sustainability assessment"** (Dilshan Sandaruwan Premathilake, Lucas Fonseca Guimarães, Denise Crocce Romano Espinosa, Jorge Alberto Soares Tenório, Mentore Vaccari & Alexandre Botelho Junior, *RSC Sustainability*, vol. 3, pp. 4975–4991, 2025, open access, CC BY) — https://doi.org/10.1039/D5SU00417A — ~8 min. A peer-reviewed critical review from a Royal Society of Chemistry journal, authored by researchers across Brazilian and European institutions, that scores the technology readiness and sustainability of recycling processes using an AI-assisted method on literature drawn from Scopus, explicitly to reduce subjective judgment calls in comparing technologies. Trustworthy because it is transparent about its scoring methodology and published in a peer-reviewed, open-access venue.

**"Sustainability rules for batteries and waste batteries"** (summary of Regulation (EU) 2023/1542, EUR-Lex, Publications Office of the European Union) — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/EN/legal-content/summary/sustainability-rules-for-batteries-and-waste-batteries.html — ~5 min. The official EU institutional summary of the binding regulation that sets recycling-efficiency targets, minimum recycled-content thresholds, collection obligations, and battery-passport/labelling requirements for batteries sold in the EU. Not peer-reviewed, but it is the primary legal-institutional source for what is, by scale, currently the world's most concrete regulatory push on battery circularity.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Three distinct technology families compete to recover value from a dead lithium-ion cell, and they trade off against each other in ways that mirror the energy-density-versus-safety-versus-cost trade-off from Day 1 and Day 3. Pyrometallurgy — smelting spent cells and modules at high temperature — is the oldest and most industrially mature route, borrowed largely from existing metal-smelting infrastructure; it reliably recovers cobalt, nickel, and copper as an alloy, but it burns off the lithium and graphite as slag or emissions, meaning the two materials this week's supply-chain story (Day 4) flagged as increasingly strategic are the ones pyrometallurgy is worst at recovering. Hydrometallurgy — dissolving battery material in acid or other leaching agents, then chemically separating and precipitating out each metal — is the route the Premathilake et al. 2025 review focuses on, and their assessment finds it offers real advantages over pyrometallurgy specifically because it can recover lithium as well as cobalt and nickel, produces purer end products suitable for making new cathode material directly, and tolerates the messy, mixed feedstock of real-world collected batteries (different chemistries, different states of degradation) better than more delicate alternatives. The cost is a more complex, multi-step chemical process and its own waste stream (spent leaching solutions) to manage.

The third family, direct recycling, aims lower in ambition but potentially higher in efficiency: rather than breaking the cathode back down into separate elements and resynthesizing new material from scratch (as both pyro- and hydrometallurgy effectively do), it tries to repair the existing crystal structure of a degraded cathode and put it back into service. A 2024 review in *Nano-Micro Letters* (Huang et al.) lays out why this is harder than it sounds and surveys five distinct ways researchers are attempting it — solid-state sintering, hydrothermal treatment, eutectic molten-salt treatment, electrochemical relithiation, and chemical lithiation — each aimed at reversing the specific degradation damage Day 2 described (lithium loss from the cathode structure, cracking, and loss of crystalline order) rather than discarding the structure entirely. Because the right repair recipe depends on exactly how a given cathode degraded, and because collected batteries arrive as an unsorted mix of chemistries, ages, and failure histories, this route remains mostly confined to laboratory and pilot-scale demonstrations rather than high-volume industrial practice — the Islam & Iyer-Raniga review reaches the same conclusion from its broader survey, flagging direct recycling as the most promising long-term route precisely because it is the least energy- and chemically-intensive, while also being the least mature.

Both of today's technical reviews converge on the same underlying message: the chemistry and engineering side of battery recycling has made genuine, documented progress, with multiple credible and partly commercialized pathways to recover the bulk of a cell's valuable metals. What the Islam & Iyer-Raniga systematic review adds, from its vantage point over the whole literature rather than one process family, is a pointed observation about where research effort has not gone: the 93 articles they reviewed are heavily weighted toward recovery chemistry and process engineering, and comparatively thin on economics, lifecycle assessment, and policy — exactly the questions that determine whether a technically feasible process actually gets built, used, and scaled. A recycling technology that works in a lab is not the same thing as a profitable recycling industry; a battery also has to physically reach a recycler in the first place, which depends on collection infrastructure and incentives that sit outside the chemistry entirely, and which the review identifies as comparatively under-studied relative to the recovery processes themselves.

This is where the EU's 2023/1542 Battery Regulation becomes relevant as more than a side note — it is a direct policy response to that gap between "technically possible" and "actually happening." The regulation sets binding recycling-efficiency targets (65% for lithium-based batteries by the end of 2025, rising to 70% by 2030), requires minimum shares of recycled cobalt, lead, lithium, and nickel in new batteries from 2031 (with higher thresholds following from 2036), and introduces extended producer responsibility obligations making manufacturers financially responsible for collection and recycling, alongside a "battery passport" meant to make a battery's material composition and history traceable across its life. Reading this alongside today's technical reviews reframes the regulation's ambition: hitting rising recycled-content minimums over the next decade will require not just more recycling capacity in general, but specifically more deployment of the higher-yield, higher-purity routes (hydrometallurgy, eventually direct recycling) that today's reviews describe as technically promising but not yet running at the scale the mandates will eventually demand.

Put together, recycling looks less like a single finished technology waiting to be deployed and more like three technology families at different stages of maturity, being pulled forward by regulation faster than market economics alone would move them — a genuinely open race between policy ambition and industrial readiness. It is also a direct, if partial, answer to Day 4's supply-chain concentration problem: every tonne of cobalt or lithium recovered from a dead battery is a tonne that does not need to come from a Congolese or Indonesian mine, with that case study's attendant human and environmental costs. But "partial" is the operative word — recycled supply can only ever offset primary mining, not replace it, for as long as the global battery fleet is still growing as fast as Day 1's IEA deployment figures describe, since a growing fleet means far more batteries are currently in use than are reaching end of life in any given year.

## Questions to think about

- The Islam & Iyer-Raniga review found recovery chemistry far better studied than economics and policy, yet getting batteries to a recycler in the first place depends on collection logistics and incentives, not chemistry. If you had to allocate new research funding, would you put it into better recovery processes or into collection-and-economics research — and why?
- The EU's recycled-content mandates (rising through 2036) assume hydrometallurgical and eventually direct-recycling capacity will scale up to match. If that capacity does not materialize fast enough, what should regulators do: relax the targets, subsidize the technology directly, or let manufacturers face penalties for missing them?
- LFP batteries (Day 1, Day 3) are cheaper and safer but currently far less profitable to recycle than cobalt- and nickel-rich chemistries, because they contain no cobalt and little recoverable value beyond lithium and iron. As LFP's market share keeps growing, should recycling economics for low-value chemistries be addressed through regulation (mandated recycling regardless of profitability) or through better direct-recycling technology that makes recovering LFP cheaper in the first place?
