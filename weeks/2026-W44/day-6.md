# Lithium-ion battery science and the energy storage transition — Day 6/7: Beyond lithium-ion — sodium-ion, solid-state, and LFP's rise

## Context

This week has so far treated "lithium-ion" as a single, if internally varied, technology: a chemistry (Day 2), a safety profile (Day 3), a mineral footprint (Day 4), and a recycling problem (Day 5). Today reframes the question. "Beyond lithium-ion" is not one challenger technology waiting to dethrone lithium-ion — it is at least three distinct competitive bets unfolding at different speeds, and one of them is not really "beyond" lithium-ion at all.

The first bet has already won a large part of the market: lithium iron phosphate (LFP), introduced on Day 1 as the safety- and cost-optimized alternative to nickel- and cobalt-based cathodes, confirmed by Day 3's thermal-runaway data to be genuinely safer, and shown on Day 4 to sidestep the two most geopolitically concentrated minerals (cobalt, nickel) entirely. LFP is still lithium-ion chemistry, but its rise to roughly half of global EV battery deployment is itself reshaping what "the default battery" means — including, as Day 5 noted, making recycling economics harder, since LFP has no valuable cobalt or nickel to recover. The second bet, sodium-ion, is a genuinely different ion chemistry built on the same "rocking-chair" intercalation principle from Day 2, but swapping lithium for an element so abundant it is essentially free, at the cost of lower energy density. The third bet, solid-state batteries, keeps lithium but replaces the flammable liquid electrolyte with a solid one — directly targeting the SEI degradation chemistry from Day 2 and the dendrite-driven short circuits from Day 3, at a manufacturing-cost and -complexity price that has made commercialization genuinely difficult to time.

A broader 2021 review in *Chemical Reviews*, "Promises and Challenges of Next-Generation 'Beyond Li-ion' Batteries for Electric Vehicles and Grid Decarbonization" (Tian, Zeng, Chen, Ceder et al.), groups sodium-ion and solid-state together with two chemistries this week does not cover in depth — potassium-ion and multivalent (magnesium, calcium, zinc) batteries — as the four main academic candidates for life after lithium-ion; it is a useful map of the wider field even though today focuses on the two candidates closest to real deployment. Today's readings cover these three bets roughly in order of how close they are to today's market: LFP's data-documented rise, sodium-ion's early but real commercial deployment, and solid-state's decade-long, still-unresolved path to scale.

## Today's readings (~28 min)

**"Comparative Issues of Metal-Ion Batteries toward Sustainable Energy Storage: Lithium vs. Sodium"** (*Batteries*, MDPI, vol. 10, issue 8, article 279, 2024, open access, CC BY) — https://www.mdpi.com/2313-0105/10/8/279 — ~10 min. A peer-reviewed review comparing lithium-ion (including LFP) and sodium-ion systems across energy density, raw-material cost, environmental footprint, and safety, aimed specifically at assessing how realistic sodium-ion is as a sustainable complement to lithium-ion rather than a wholesale replacement. Trustworthy as a review article synthesizing the comparative literature rather than promoting a single lab's results; MDPI's *Batteries* is a specialist, peer-reviewed venue already used on Day 5 for recycling literature.

**"Challenges in speeding up solid-state battery development"** (Jürgen Janek & Wolfgang G. Zeier, *Nature Energy*, vol. 8, pp. 230–240, 2023) — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41560-023-01208-9 — ~10 min. A review by two leading solid-state-battery researchers (Justus Liebig University Giessen / Helmholtz Institute Münster), candid about why solid-state batteries have taken far longer to commercialize than early-2010s predictions suggested: electrolyte conductivity, anode-interface stability, and manufacturing economics all remain open problems simultaneously. Trustworthy specifically because it argues against the premature-certainty narrative common in industry press coverage of the same technology.

**"Global EV Outlook 2025 — Electric vehicle batteries"** (International Energy Agency, 2025, open access) — https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2025/electric-vehicle-batteries — ~8 min. The IEA's annual institutional data chapter on battery chemistry deployment, tracking the real-world market shift toward LFP in EVs and energy-storage systems. As on Days 1 and 4, treat this as the IEA's own policy-oriented analysis rather than an independent academic source, but it is the most current public dataset on which chemistries are actually being deployed at scale, as opposed to which are being researched.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with the bet that has already paid off commercially: LFP. The IEA's 2025 data shows LFP accounting for roughly half of global EV battery deployment by 2024, with China's domestic market alone above 70%, and — a figure worth sitting with — more than 90% of batteries used in grid-scale energy-storage systems worldwide. That last number matters because it shows LFP's advantages (cost, safety, long cycle life) matter even more when energy density is a secondary concern, as it is for a stationary grid battery that does not need to fit inside a car. Outside China, nickel-based chemistries (NMC/NCA) still hold roughly 80% of the EV market, mostly because LFP cell and pack manufacturing capacity remains heavily concentrated in Chinese companies — a reminder that "which chemistry wins" is as much an industrial-capacity question as a materials-science one. This is the piece of this week's supply-chain story (Day 4) that resolves fastest in practice: LFP's lithium-only mineral footprint is already shifting demand away from cobalt and nickel, years before any genuinely new chemistry reaches meaningful scale.

Sodium-ion is the newest chemistry to move from lab to early factory. Mechanically it is close kin to lithium-ion — ions still shuttle between a cathode and an anode through an electrolyte, the same intercalation principle from Day 2 — but sodium is roughly a thousand times more abundant in the Earth's crust than lithium and needs no cobalt or nickel, which in principle removes Day 4's mineral-concentration problem rather than just reducing it, as LFP does. The MDPI review's honest assessment is that sodium-ion currently trails lithium-ion (and especially LFP) on energy density and, for now, on per-kWh cost, because production scale is still small and electrode/electrolyte materials are less mature — the interfacial stability and low-temperature performance issues are active, unresolved research areas, directly analogous to the SEI chemistry Day 2 covered for graphite anodes, just for a different ion. That said, this is no longer purely theoretical: Chinese manufacturers including CATL have begun early commercial-scale production of sodium-ion cells since 2025, targeting stationary storage and lower-range vehicles first — exactly where LFP's grid-storage dominance suggests the real near-term opening is, rather than in long-range EVs where energy density still rules.

Solid-state batteries are the highest-ceiling, longest-horizon bet, and Janek and Zeier's review is useful precisely because it pushes back against hype. The appeal is twofold: a solid electrolyte cannot combust the way the liquid electrolytes behind Day 3's thermal-runaway chemistry can, and it can in principle allow a pure lithium-metal anode (much higher energy density than graphite) without the dendrite formation that, on Day 2 and Day 3, caused lithium plating to short-circuit and ignite liquid-electrolyte cells. The problem is that solving both at once, at automotive manufacturing scale and cost, has proven much harder than a decade of industry announcements suggested. Janek and Zeier identify three simultaneous open problems rather than one: solid electrolytes fast enough to match liquid-electrolyte performance in real composite cathodes, anode interfaces stable enough for long cycle life, and manufacturing processes cheap enough to compete with an incumbent (lithium-ion) that keeps getting cheaper on its own. Their central methodological point — that the field needs continued diversity of materials and approaches rather than a premature bet on one "winning" design — is itself a useful lens for reading future solid-state announcements skeptically: a single company's breakthrough claim does not resolve problems that, per this review, are still open across the field as a whole.

Taken together, these three bets map onto different parts of the market rather than competing head-to-head for the same one. LFP has already won the cost- and safety-sensitive segment (grid storage, mass-market EVs) it was built for. Sodium-ion is entering the same segment from a different angle — no lithium at all — starting with grid storage and cheap vehicles, where its lower energy density matters least. Solid-state, if it clears Janek and Zeier's open problems, is aimed instead at the segment where energy density is the binding constraint: long-range EVs and aviation-adjacent applications that LFP and sodium-ion cannot reach. None of this displaces the Day 4 and Day 5 conclusions so much as redistributes them: a genuinely multi-chemistry battery industry would need different mineral supply chains and different recycling processes for each, not one unified answer to either problem.

## Questions to think about

- LFP's dominance in grid storage (>90% per the IEA) and sodium-ion's early focus on the same market suggest energy density matters far less for stationary storage than for EVs. If that is right, why does so much battery R&D funding and media attention still focus on EV-range-oriented chemistries like solid-state rather than the chemistries best suited to grid storage specifically?
- Janek and Zeier argue that continued diversity of materials and research approaches, rather than early convergence on one "winning" solid-state design, is the field's best path forward. Is that a general principle for emerging technologies, or does it depend on something specific about where solid-state batteries currently are in their development?
- Sodium-ion removes the cobalt and nickel dependency this week's Day 4 reading described, but it would still need its own mining and refining supply chains at scale (for sodium, iron, manganese, and other electrode materials). What would need to be true for sodium-ion to avoid simply recreating a smaller version of the same concentration problem?
