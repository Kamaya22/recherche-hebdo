# Lithium-ion battery science and the energy storage transition — Day 3/7: Safety — thermal runaway, failure modes, and the engineering that tamed them

## Context

Day 2 ended on a warning sign: under fast charging, low temperatures, or in an aged cell with a thickened solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI), lithium ions can stop intercalating into graphite and instead deposit as metallic lithium on the anode surface. That "lithium plating" can grow needle-like dendrites that pierce the separator and cause an internal short circuit — one of the main triggers for the failure mode this week has been building toward: thermal runaway. Today looks at what thermal runaway actually is, chemically and thermally, why some battery chemistries are much more prone to it than others, and the layered set of chemistry and engineering defenses that have made it a comparatively rare event despite the explosive growth in deployed cells that Day 1's IEA data described.

This is also a good moment to notice how tightly this week's sub-topics are linked rather than separate boxes. Thermal runaway is not a different phenomenon from the degradation chemistry of Day 2 — it is what happens when the same side reactions (SEI breakdown, electrolyte decomposition) that slowly age a healthy cell instead run away with themselves inside seconds to minutes. And the LFP-versus-NMC trade-off introduced on Day 1 (energy density versus cost and safety) turns out to have hard experimental numbers behind it, which today's readings supply.

Why this matters beyond the lab: as EVs and grid-scale battery installations scale into the millions of packs, the probability that *some* cell, somewhere, experiences an abuse condition is no longer negligible — which is exactly why cell-, pack-, and system-level safety engineering, not just cell chemistry, has become its own substantial field.

## Today's readings (~21 min)

**"Mitigation Strategies for Li-Ion Battery Thermal Runaway: A Review"** (Bor-Rong Xu, Juyoung Lee, Donguk Kwon, Lingxi Kong & Michael Pecht, *Renewable and Sustainable Energy Reviews*, vol. 150, article 111437, 2021) — https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rser.2021.111437 — ~9 min. A peer-reviewed review from a leading energy-technology journal (Elsevier) that systematically covers the layered defenses built into commercial cells and packs: positive-temperature-coefficient (PTC) thermistors and electrodes, current-interrupt devices (CID), safety vents, shutdown separators, electrolyte additives, and battery management systems (BMS). The authors are long-time battery-reliability researchers at the University of Maryland's CALCE center. Published in a respected peer-reviewed journal; note it sits behind a paywall.

**"Thermal and Mechanical Safety Assessment of Type 21700 Lithium-Ion Batteries with NMC, NCA and LFP Cathodes — Investigation of Cell Abuse by Means of Accelerating Rate Calorimetry (ARC)"** (Sebastian Ohneseit et al., Karlsruhe Institute of Technology and Queen's University Belfast, *Batteries*, 9(5), article 237, 2023, open access, CC BY) — https://doi.org/10.3390/batteries9050237 — ~7 min. A peer-reviewed experimental study that directly compares how commercial 21700-format cells with three different cathode chemistries (NMC, NCA, LFP) behave under controlled thermal and mechanical abuse in an accelerating rate calorimeter. Trustworthy because it reports primary measured data (onset temperatures, nail-penetration outcomes) rather than modeled estimates, in an open-access, peer-reviewed specialist battery journal.

**"Advances in Thermal Management of Lithium-Ion Batteries: Causes of Thermal Runaway and Mitigation Strategies"** (Tiansi Wang, Haoran Liu, Wanlin Wang, Weiran Jiang, Yixiang Xu, Simeng Zhu & Qingliang Sheng, *Processes*, 13(8), article 2499, 2025, open access) — https://doi.org/10.3390/pr13082499 — ~5 min. A recent peer-reviewed review that classifies thermal-runaway triggers into mechanical, thermal, and electrical abuse, and compares passive thermal-management strategies (phase-change materials, heat pipes, fins) against active ones (liquid/air cooling), with their respective trade-offs. Useful as the most up-to-date synthesis of where thermal-management engineering stands.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Thermal runaway is best understood as a self-accelerating chain of exothermic (heat-releasing) side reactions, each of which raises the cell's internal temperature enough to trigger the next, faster reaction — a positive feedback loop rather than a single event. It can be set off by any of three "abuse" categories: mechanical abuse (crushing, a nail penetrating the cell, a manufacturing defect), electrical abuse (overcharging, an external short circuit, or the lithium-plating/dendrite pathway described on Day 2), or thermal abuse (external heating, a cooling-system failure, or heat arriving from a neighboring cell that has already failed). Whatever the trigger, the underlying chemistry is broadly shared: the SEI that Day 2 described as a stabilizing passivation layer begins to decompose once the cell passes roughly 90–120°C, releasing heat and gas; around 130–150°C the polymer separator that physically keeps the electrodes apart starts to melt and shrink, and once it fails locally the electrodes can touch directly, causing a large internal short circuit; from there, electrolyte decomposition and, in nickel- and cobalt-rich cathodes, the release of oxygen from the cathode's crystal structure itself feed a runaway heating process that can push the cell well past 200–300°C, culminating in violent gas venting, fire, or, in confined conditions, explosion.

The Ohneseit et al. accelerating-rate-calorimetry study gives this abstract sequence hard numbers, and it closes the loop on Day 1's LFP-versus-NMC/NCA trade-off with direct experimental evidence rather than just citing energy-density figures. Testing commercial 21700-format cells with NMC, NCA, and LFP cathodes under both thermal abuse (controlled heating) and mechanical abuse (nail penetration), the authors found that NMC cells began reacting first as temperature rose, but NCA cells actually reached full thermal runaway slightly sooner once triggered; LFP cells, by contrast, reacted far more gradually and only at significantly higher temperatures — and under nail penetration, the LFP cells did not go into thermal runaway at all. This is the mechanistic reason behind LFP's reputation as the "safer" chemistry that Day 1 flagged: LFP's cathode releases much less oxygen when stressed and is intrinsically more thermally stable than nickel-cobalt chemistries, independent of any added engineering protection. It is also a reminder that "safer" is a matter of degree and test conditions, not an absolute guarantee — LFP cells can still fail under sufficiently severe abuse, just at a higher threshold.

Because cell chemistry alone cannot make failure impossible, the battery industry has built multiple independent, layered defenses on top of it, which the Xu et al. review maps systematically. At the cell level: positive-temperature-coefficient (PTC) materials sharply increase electrical resistance once they get hot, throttling current flow before things escalate; current-interrupt devices (CID) permanently break the circuit if internal pressure rises past a threshold (an early sign of gas-generating decomposition); and "shutdown separators" are engineered to melt and seal their own pores at a moderate temperature, blocking further ion transport — deliberately killing the cell's function to stop a thermal event, before the separator's structural failure (the 130–150°C event described above) can occur. Electrolyte additives and flame-retardant formulations aim to slow decomposition chemistry itself. At the pack level, safety vents direct any gas release away from other cells, and physical spacing or thermal-barrier materials between cells are designed to stop a single failed cell from triggering its neighbors — propagation being the scenario that turns one cell's failure into a pack-wide fire. At the system level, the battery management system (BMS) continuously monitors voltage, current, and temperature precisely to keep every cell inside the safe operating window and prevent abuse conditions from arising in the first place, rather than just containing them once they start.

The Wang et al. 2025 review adds the thermal-management layer that sits alongside — and overlaps with — these protection devices: keeping cells within a safe temperature band during ordinary operation, not just during a fault. Passive approaches (phase-change materials that absorb heat as they melt, heat pipes, cooling fins) require no extra power and add little complexity, but their heat-dissipation capacity is limited and can be overwhelmed under sustained high-power use (fast charging, hard acceleration, hot climates) — exactly the conditions Day 2 identified as accelerating SEI growth and lithium plating. Active approaches (liquid or forced-air cooling loops) dissipate far more heat and are now standard in most EV packs, but add cost, energy consumption, and additional components that can themselves fail. The throughline across both of today's reviews is that no single defense is relied upon alone: chemistry (cathode choice), passivation engineering (separators, additives), electronic protection (PTC, CID, BMS), and thermal management (passive/active cooling) are stacked redundantly, which is precisely why, despite the enormous scale-up in deployed lithium-ion cells that Day 1's IEA figures described, severe thermal-runaway incidents remain statistically rare events rather than a routine risk.

That said, "rare" is not "solved," and today's readings point to where the remaining risk concentrates. Aging is the connective tissue back to Day 2: a cell that has accumulated cracked graphite particles, plated lithium, and a thickened, uneven SEI has more latent weak points and a lower margin before an abuse condition tips into runaway than a fresh cell does — meaning a battery's safety margin is not fixed over its lifetime, even though most safety standards and consumer-facing specifications are tested on new cells. And as Day 1 noted that lithium-ion energy density is approaching a practical ceiling, the industry's response of packing more cells together at higher density to chase range or storage capacity makes propagation-prevention (stopping one failed cell from triggering its neighbors) an increasingly central — and increasingly difficult — engineering problem, particularly for large-format EV and grid-scale packs.

## Questions to think about

- The Ohneseit et al. data show LFP did not go into thermal runaway at all under nail penetration, while NMC and NCA did. How should that safety margin be weighed against NMC/NCA's higher energy density when choosing a chemistry for different applications — say, a long-range EV versus a stationary grid battery where weight barely matters?
- Many of the defenses the Xu et al. review describes (CID, shutdown separators, safety vents) are "last line" responses that activate once something has already started going wrong, whereas the BMS is meant to prevent abuse conditions from occurring at all. If you were allocating a fixed R&D budget, would you prioritize better prevention (BMS, sensing) or better containment (separators, propagation barriers) — and why?
- Day 2 established that all lithium-ion cells degrade in ways that reduce their safety margin over time, yet safety testing and ratings are typically done on new cells. What would a safety standard that accounted for a battery's full lifecycle — including after years of use or a stint on the secondary (resale/reuse) market — need to measure differently?
