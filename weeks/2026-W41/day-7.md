# Gravitational-wave astronomy — Day 7/7: Testing gravity itself, and the next generation of detectors

## Context

This week moved from a single chirp (Day 1) to the machinery that extracts it from noise (Day 2), to the hundreds-strong catalogue that machinery has produced (Day 3), to one extraordinary multi-messenger event within that catalogue (Day 4), to the live debates the catalogue cannot yet settle — formation channels, the stochastic background, the Hubble tension (Day 5) — and finally to the statistical and machine-learning infrastructure that makes every single detection trustworthy in the first place (Day 6). Today closes the loop on two threads that have been running underneath all of it without ever taking center stage: every merger is also, implicitly, a test of general relativity in a regime no laboratory can reach, and the entire field is about to be rebuilt around instruments far more sensitive than the ones that produced everything described so far.

Both threads point in the same direction. If general relativity is eventually caught failing a gravitational-wave test, it will most likely be because a new detector — more sensitive, covering a different frequency band — finally had the statistical power to see the failure. So today's reading pairs the collaboration's own account of just how hard they have tried to break Einstein's theory with hundreds of real events, with the design studies for the three instruments meant to take that search a factor of ten (or more) further: the European Einstein Telescope, the US-led Cosmic Explorer, and the space-based LISA mission.

The collaboration's own peer-reviewed compilation of every test of general relativity performed on its most confident detections through the third observing run, published in a flagship American Physical Society journal — the most comprehensive and current single source on how, and how hard, gravitational-wave astronomy has tried to catch Einstein's theory failing.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Every merger analyzed this week was, without saying so explicitly, also a test of general relativity, because the whole detection and parameter-estimation pipeline (Day 6) assumes the theory's predicted waveform is correct. The GWTC-3-era test paper makes that assumption falsifiable by running nine distinct checks on the loudest, cleanest events. It looks for leftover power in the data once the best-fit general-relativity waveform has been subtracted (there should be none, and there isn't, within the detectors' noise). It compares the signal's early inspiral against alternative "parametrized" waveform models that let each post-Newtonian term in the equations of motion float freely, rather than being fixed by the theory — general relativity's predicted values for those terms, including the term closest to being poorly constrained before this analysis, continue to fit. It checks whether gravitational waves disperse in transit the way a massive particle would (they do not, within measurement precision, which translates into an upper bound on a hypothetical graviton's mass at a level of roughly 10⁻²³ eV/c² — a number so far from zero it is best read as "indistinguishable from exactly massless" rather than as evidence of a small but nonzero mass). It searches for extra polarization states beyond general relativity's two, for echoes that would appear if merged objects had a reflecting surface instead of a true event horizon, and for inconsistency between the inspiral and the post-merger "ringdown" — the final black hole settling down by shedding distortions as a characteristic set of damped-oscillation frequencies, much as a struck bell rings at specific tones determined only by its size and material. Under general relativity's "no-hair" theorem, a black hole's ringdown frequencies are set entirely by its final mass and spin; measuring more than one such frequency in the same event, as has now been done for a handful of the loudest mergers, lets physicists check that the two numbers it implies agree with the mass and spin inferred from the earlier inspiral. Across every one of these nine tests, applied to the strongest signals detected through the end of the third observing run, the data stayed fully consistent with Einstein's century-old theory, extending the previous bounds and finding no statistically significant deviation anywhere.

That is a genuinely remarkable, if slightly unsatisfying, scientific result: general relativity has now been stress-tested in the strong-field, highly dynamical regime of merging black holes and neutron stars, across nine independent angles of attack, and has not budged. It is unsatisfying only in the sense that many physicists expect general relativity to break down somewhere — reconciling it with quantum mechanics is one of the biggest open problems in physics, and several dark-energy and dark-matter alternatives to the standard cosmological model predict exactly the kind of modified-gravity signatures these tests are built to catch. So far, none of those alternatives has found support. The honest takeaway, which the rest of today's reading explains how to improve on, is that current detectors may simply not yet be sensitive enough, or may not yet have seen a loud enough, close enough, or exotic enough event, to expose whatever deviation (if any) is actually there.

This is exactly the gap next-generation detectors are designed to close. The European Einstein Telescope's science case (this week's second reading) proposes a fundamentally different geometry from LIGO: three nested interferometers arranged in an equilateral triangle, each arm 10 kilometers long, built underground (to cut seismic noise far below what any surface detector achieves) and split between a "cold," low-power configuration optimized for low frequencies and a "hot," high-power configuration optimized for high frequencies — a design choice meant to extend the observable band down to around 2 Hz, a full order of magnitude below the roughly 20 Hz floor of Advanced LIGO, while improving sensitivity by up to a factor of eight across the band current detectors already cover. Cosmic Explorer, the parallel US-led proposal (previewed in the Bailes et al. roadmap and developed in the Cosmic Explorer collaboration's own 2021 technical design study), takes a different route to a similar order-of-magnitude leap: surface-based, L-shaped interferometers with arms up to 40 kilometers long — ten times LIGO's — trading underground construction for sheer baseline length. Operating together as a global "third-generation" network, Einstein Telescope and Cosmic Explorer are expected to detect binary-black-hole mergers out to such extreme distances that essentially every stellar-mass black-hole merger in the observable universe back to its earliest star-forming epochs would register, turning today's growing-but-still-incomplete catalogue (Day 3) into something closer to a full cosmic census — and giving every one of today's general-relativity tests, the Hubble-tension standard-siren method (Day 5), and the formation-channel debate (Day 5) vastly more statistical power than they have now.

Ground-based detectors, however, are fundamentally blind to a whole class of sources: anything that merges too slowly, or is too massive, to pass through LIGO's sensitive band in a reasonable observing time. That is what LISA, the European Space Agency's Laser Interferometer Space Antenna, is built to see. Formally adopted by ESA's Science Programme Committee in January 2024 and now in its industrial build phase (ESA signed the spacecraft construction contract in June 2025), LISA will place three spacecraft roughly 2.5 million kilometers apart, trailing Earth around the Sun in a configuration that forms a nearly perfect equilateral triangle, exchanging laser beams to measure sub-kilometer arm-length changes over that vast baseline. That geometry opens an entirely different frequency window — roughly 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻¹ Hz, far below what any ground-based detector, present or future, could ever reach, because ground detectors are fundamentally limited at low frequency by seismic and gravity-gradient noise no amount of engineering fully removes. In that band, LISA's priority targets are mergers of the supermassive black holes that sit at the centers of merging galaxies, slowly inspiraling stellar-mass compact objects spiraling into those same supermassive black holes (extreme-mass-ratio inspirals, which trace out intricate, information-rich orbits before plunging in), and the galaxy's own population of compact binary stars, loud enough in this band to form a foreground "hum" LISA will need to characterize and subtract — a space-based cousin of the stochastic-background problem Day 5 described for ground detectors. ESA's current target is a launch around 2035 on an Ariane 6 rocket from French Guiana, building on the drag-free control technology that the LISA Pathfinder precursor mission successfully demonstrated in 2015-2017.

Put together, the honest state of the field at the end of this week's reading is this. Settled: gravitational waves exist and match general relativity's predictions in every test tried so far; a real, large, and still-growing population of compact-object mergers has replaced the single 2015 detection; at least one merger has been directly tied, via multi-messenger follow-up, to the production of heavy elements. Open, and likely to stay open until next-generation detectors arrive: which formation channel (or mix of channels) actually dominates the observed black-hole population; whether the stochastic gravitational-wave background is detectable with current instruments at all; whether standard sirens can ever gather enough events to adjudicate the Hubble tension; and, underneath all of it, whether general relativity's unbroken streak across nine kinds of tests reflects a true law of nature or simply the limits of what today's detectors can see. Only Einstein Telescope, Cosmic Explorer, and LISA — each still years from first light — can settle that last, biggest question either way.

## Questions to think about

1. General relativity has passed nine independent kinds of gravitational-wave tests without exception. At what point, if ever, should a theory's unbroken track record under increasingly severe tests count as strong evidence that it is exactly right, rather than merely "not yet proven wrong"?
2. Einstein Telescope (underground, triangular, 10 km arms) and Cosmic Explorer (surface, L-shaped, up to 40 km arms) pursue the same order-of-magnitude sensitivity gain through very different engineering choices. What trade-offs — cost, siting, seismic isolation, international coordination — do you think explain why two allied scientific communities converged on different designs instead of one shared blueprint?
3. LISA can only detect sources in a frequency band no ground-based detector, however advanced, will ever reach. What does that imply about the limits of "better engineering" as a strategy in experimental physics — are there other fields where the fundamental barrier is the detector's physical environment rather than its technology?
4. This week traced gravitational-wave astronomy from a single 2015 chirp to a field with hundreds of detections, its own open controversies, and three next-generation instruments under construction, all within about a decade. What would you want to see — in the catalogue, in the tests of general relativity, or in a new kind of source entirely — to judge, ten years from now, whether this decade of growth lived up to its promise?
5. If Einstein Telescope, Cosmic Explorer, and LISA all come online in the 2030s roughly as planned, which of this week's open debates (formation channels, the stochastic background, the Hubble tension, or a genuine deviation from general relativity) do you expect to be resolved first, and why?
