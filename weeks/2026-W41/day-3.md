# Gravitational-wave astronomy — Day 3/7: The growing catalogue — what hundreds of mergers reveal about black holes and neutron stars

## Context

Day 1 introduced a single event — GW150914, the first detection, one black-hole merger out of (as it turns out) a much larger hidden population. Day 2 explained how any single merger is pulled out of instrumental noise. Today's angle is what happens once you stop looking at individual detections and instead look at all of them together: the Gravitational-Wave Transient Catalog (GWTC), the LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA (LVK) collaboration's cumulative list of every confident detection, now used as a population-survey dataset in its own right.

This matters because a single merger tells you about one pair of objects; hundreds of mergers, analyzed statistically, tell you about the *demographics* of compact objects across the universe — how black-hole and neutron-star masses are distributed, how fast they spin, how often they merge, and, by extension, how they form. That demographic question could not be asked at all until the catalogue grew large enough for patterns to emerge above the noise of individual measurement uncertainty. The catalogue has grown explosively: 11 events by the end of the first observing run, 90 events in GWTC-3 (covering the first three runs, through 2020), and — in the current, ongoing fourth observing run (O4) — 218 events in the GWTC-4.0 release (August 2025) and 390 confirmed events in the newest release, GWTC-5.0 (26 May 2026). In other words, the catalogue more than quadrupled in the last year alone, and detectors are now finding new confident signals roughly three to four times a week.

Today's readings move from the established, peer-reviewed population picture (GWTC-3, closed out in 2023, which is still the most thoroughly vetted and widely cited population analysis) to the collaboration's own plain-language account of how the catalogue has grown since, and finally to the newest technical population analysis built on the current 390-event catalogue — flagged below as a preprint, since it has not yet completed peer review.

## Today's readings (3 items, ~21 min total)

**Population of Merging Compact Binaries Inferred Using Gravitational Waves through GWTC-3** (LIGO Scientific Collaboration, Virgo Collaboration & KAGRA Collaboration, *Physical Review X*, 2023) — https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevX.13.011048 — ~9 min (abstract, introduction, and the mass/spin/rate summary figures). The collaboration's own peer-reviewed statistical analysis of the population of 76 confident mergers through the third observing run. Physical Review X is a fully open-access, peer-reviewed journal of the American Physical Society; this paper is the field's standard population-analysis reference and the basis for the numbers most other work compares against.

**GWTC-5.0: Updating the catalog with observations from the second part of the fourth LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA observing run** (LIGO Scientific Collaboration, Virgo Collaboration & KAGRA Collaboration, science summary, 2026) — https://ligo.org/science-summaries/O4b_catalog_omnibus/ — ~6 min. A short, non-technical summary, written by the collaboration itself, of the May 2026 catalogue release that brought the total to 390 confirmed detections. It explains in plain language what changed since GWTC-3 and previews highlights (hierarchical-merger candidates, black-hole "ringdown" tests, new cosmology measurements) that later readings this week will unpack.

**GWTC-5.0: Population Properties of Merging Compact Binaries** (LVK Collaboration, *preprint — not yet peer reviewed*, posted May 2026) — https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.27226 — ~6 min (abstract and summary of mass/spin findings). The collaboration's own statistical population analysis built on the current 390-event catalogue (267 of which are used in the population fit). It has not yet completed peer review and is included here only for its up-to-the-month numbers, which should be read as provisional until published — a useful contrast with the vetted GWTC-3 analysis above.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

The basic method, established by GWTC-3 and simply re-run on ever-larger samples since, is hierarchical Bayesian inference: each merger gives you a noisy, individually uncertain estimate of masses and spins (because the signal is weak and the detector has finite sensitivity), but when you combine hundreds of such noisy estimates under a flexible statistical model, you can infer the *underlying* population distribution more precisely than any single event could tell you — the same logic as inferring a country's height distribution from a sample of imprecisely measured individuals. GWTC-3 applied this to 76 confident events (69 binary black holes, 2 binary neutron stars, 4 neutron star–black hole binaries, and one ambiguous case, GW190814) and GWTC-5.0 applies it to a much larger sample built from 390 total detections.

The clearest result, present in both the 2023 and 2026 analyses, is that the binary black hole mass distribution is not smooth. Rather than a simple power law trailing off at high mass, GWTC-3 found "localized over- and under-densities," with excess numbers of mergers whose two black holes combine to a chirp mass around 8.3 and 27.9 solar masses; the GWTC-5.0 population paper describes the same structure as a peak in primary mass near 10 solar masses, and a change of slope — a steepening — near 35 solar masses, with a possible third feature near 20 solar masses. The 10-solar-mass peak is plausibly explained by ordinary massive-star evolution and black holes forming in isolated binaries; the ~35-solar-mass feature is harder to explain this way, since standard models of how massive stars shed mass and collapse (via "pair-instability" supernovae) predict a gap, not a pile-up, around that range. One proposed explanation is that these are "second-generation" black holes: ones that themselves formed from an earlier black-hole merger, inheriting a larger mass and, often, a more substantial, more randomly oriented spin — a signature statisticians can look for directly.

Spin is exactly that signature. GWTC-3 found black-hole spins in the population to be modest on average (about half of the measured spin magnitudes below ~0.25) and the effective inspiral spin — a combination of spin and orbital-alignment information that is comparatively easy to measure — only mildly tilted toward positive (aligned) values. That asymmetry matters because the two leading formation channels predict different signatures: black holes born and merging in isolated binary systems tend to have spins aligned with the orbit (from the way the stars themselves rotated), while black holes that pair up dynamically in dense stellar environments like globular clusters tend to have randomly oriented spins, which average out to close to zero net alignment but with more extreme spin magnitudes mixed in. With the larger GWTC-5.0 sample, this picture has sharpened into evidence for distinct subpopulations rather than one smooth distribution: the GWTC-5.0 population paper (this week's third reading) reports evidence for a subpopulation of binary black holes hosting an unusually high-spin component (effective spin χ ≈ 0.7), together with a broader effective-spin distribution for unequal-mass binaries — a pattern the authors describe as supporting the presence of multiple subpopulations with different formation pathways, including a signature consistent with hierarchical (repeated) mergers rather than single-generation stellar collapse.

Neutron stars tell a complementary story. Using the handful of binary-neutron-star and neutron-star–black-hole events, GWTC-3 inferred a broad, fairly flat neutron star mass distribution from about 1.2 to 2.0 solar masses — consistent with, but not yet sharply constraining, nuclear-physics expectations for the maximum mass a neutron star's internal pressure can support before collapsing further. Merger rates — how many such events happen per cubic gigaparsec per year — were pinned down only loosely in GWTC-3 (binary neutron star mergers: 10-1700 per Gpc³ per year; neutron star-black hole mergers: 7.8-140; binary black holes: 17.9-44 at a reference redshift), and the newer analyses narrow these ranges as the sample grows (the GWTC-5.0 population paper reports a binary-black-hole rate of 27.5-49.4 per Gpc³ per year), though no new neutron-star-containing mergers were found in the latest observing segment (O4b), so the neutron star numbers from GWTC-3 remain the most recent robust estimate for now.

Two methodological cautions are worth carrying into the rest of the week. First, these are population inferences, not direct observations of any one event's "true" formation history — the mass peaks, spin subpopulations, and rates are statistical features of the whole sample, probabilistic conclusions that update as the catalogue grows, not certainties about any individual merger. Second, the newest numbers in this reading — from the GWTC-5.0 population paper — are from a preprint that has not yet finished peer review; the broad features it reports are consistent with, and extend, the peer-reviewed GWTC-3 picture, which is why both are presented together here rather than relying on the preprint alone.

## Questions to think about

1. The ~35-solar-mass "pile-up" in the black hole mass distribution is hard to explain with standard single-star evolution but fits a "second-generation merger" explanation. What other kinds of evidence, beyond mass and spin statistics, could help confirm or rule out that a given black hole is itself the product of an earlier merger?
2. Population inferences become more precise as the catalogue grows, but they are still inferences built on a statistical model of how mass and spin are distributed. What risks does that pose if the assumed model is wrong in a way that happens to fit the early, smaller samples reasonably well?
3. The newest numbers in today's reading come from a preprint rather than a peer-reviewed paper. What would you want to see change, or stay the same, between this preprint and its eventual published version before treating its specific numbers (rather than its broad qualitative picture) as settled?
