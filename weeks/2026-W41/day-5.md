# Gravitational-wave astronomy — Day 5/7: Controversies and open debates — how binaries form, the missing hum, and the Hubble tension

## Context

The first four days of this week told a story that sounds mostly settled: detectors work, a growing catalogue of hundreds of mergers exists, and at least one event (GW170817, Day 4) has been dissected across the whole electromagnetic spectrum. Today's reading is about the parts of the field that are explicitly *not* settled — three live debates where gravitational-wave astronomers disagree, or simply do not yet have enough data to decide.

The first debate asks a question that sounds basic but is not: when two black holes merge, how did they end up orbiting each other in the first place? Day 3 described the catalogue (GWTC) as a population of hundreds of mergers with measured masses and spins; today's reading explains why that population is read as evidence for *two competing formation pathways* — binaries that evolved together as a pair of stars from birth, versus binaries assembled later by chance gravitational encounters in a crowded star cluster — and why distinguishing them matters for what the catalogue tells us about stellar physics.

The second debate is about something that, unlike any single merger, has never been directly detected at all: a faint, continuous "stochastic background" produced by the incoherent sum of every merger too distant or too numerous to resolve individually, echoing across the universe's history. The third debate reopens a thread from Day 4 — GW170817 let astronomers measure the Hubble constant (the universe's current expansion rate) using gravitational waves alone, a method called a "standard siren" — and asks whether this new, independent method can help settle the long-standing disagreement between two other ways of measuring the same number, known as the Hubble tension.

## Today's readings (3 items, ~23 min total)

**Binary Black Hole Mergers: Formation and Populations** (Mapelli, *Frontiers in Astronomy and Space Sciences*, 2020) — https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/astronomy-and-space-sciences/articles/10.3389/fspas.2020.00038/full — ~8 min (abstract, and the sections on the isolated-binary and dynamical-formation channels). A peer-reviewed, open-access mini-review by a leading theorist of compact-binary formation, laying out the two main formation channels and the specific observational "fingerprints" (mass, spin, eccentricity distributions) each one is expected to leave in the catalogue.

**Stochastic Gravitational-Wave Backgrounds: Current Detection Efforts and Future Prospects** (Renzini, Goncharov, Jenkins & Meyers, *Galaxies*, 2022) — https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4434/10/1/34 — ~8 min (abstract, introduction, and the section on astrophysical backgrounds from compact binaries). A peer-reviewed, open-access (CC BY) review co-authored by members of the LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA stochastic-search team, explaining what a stochastic background is, why it has not yet been confidently detected, and when current and future detectors might see one.

**Constraints on the Cosmic Expansion History from GWTC-3** (LIGO Scientific Collaboration, Virgo Collaboration & KAGRA Collaboration, *The Astrophysical Journal*, 2023) — https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ac74bb — ~7 min (abstract, and the summary of the combined H0 result and its uncertainty). The collaboration's own peer-reviewed standard-siren analysis combining 47 GWTC-3 events with the Day-4 GW170817 measurement; published in a fully open-access AAS journal (open access for all AAS content since January 2022), it is the most authoritative current gravitational-wave constraint on the Hubble constant.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with formation channels. A binary black hole can arise in (at least) two very different ways. In the **isolated channel**, two massive stars are born together, evolve as a binary for millions of years, and eventually each collapses into a black hole without ever interacting with another star. This path depends on two poorly understood pieces of stellar physics: how a dying massive star's core collapses (and whether it gives the resulting black hole a "kick" that could disrupt the binary) and "common envelope evolution," a short, violent phase where one star's envelope engulfs its companion, dramatically shrinking the orbit — a process astronomers can model but not yet observe directly. In the **dynamical channel**, two black holes that formed independently are brought together later through gravitational encounters in a dense environment, typically a globular or young star cluster, where close three-body interactions or outright binary exchanges can assemble (and later eject) tight pairs. Because dynamical assembly is a chaotic, many-body process, it tends to produce binaries with randomly oriented spins and sometimes measurable orbital eccentricity, and — through repeated mergers inside a cluster ("hierarchical mergers") — can build up black holes heavier than any single dying star could produce. The isolated channel, by contrast, tends to produce more aligned spins, since the stars' spins and orbit are inherited from a shared formation history. Day 3's mention of "hierarchical-merger candidates" in the newest catalogue release is exactly this dynamical-channel signature showing up in real data. The honest state of the debate: population analyses of the growing catalogue find statistical evidence for *both* channels operating simultaneously, in different proportions depending on cluster mass and galactic environment, and no single channel can yet account for the full observed spread of masses, spins and eccentricities.

Second, the stochastic background. Any gravitational-wave detector is, in principle, bathed in the combined signal of every compact-binary merger that has ever happened in the observable universe — the vast majority too faint or too numerous to be picked out individually as the sharp "chirps" described on Day 2. Summed together, these unresolved signals should blend into a faint, persistent background hum, analogous to the cosmic microwave background but made of gravitational waves (and, in principle, carrying a cosmological-era-background component from the very early universe as well, though that signal is expected to be far fainter). Because this background is not a transient to filter out of the noise but is itself the signal, it is searched for differently: by cross-correlating the simultaneous output of two or more widely separated detectors and looking for a tiny excess of correlated, non-instrumental strain. As of the readings used this week, LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA searches — both for an overall ("isotropic") background and for background concentrated in specific sky directions ("anisotropic") — have produced only upper limits, not a confident detection; current estimates suggest the astrophysical background from compact-binary mergers alone may become detectable as the detector network reaches design sensitivity over the next several years, with next-generation ground detectors and the space-based LISA mission (both previewed on Day 7) expected to improve the reach substantially.

Third, the Hubble tension. Day 4 mentioned that combining GW170817's gravitational-wave "standard siren" distance with its electromagnetic redshift gave an early estimate of the Hubble constant, H0, with large uncertainty. That single-event method works because gravitational waves directly encode the luminosity distance to a merger, without needing the usual chain of calibrated "standard candles" (like Cepheid variable stars and Type Ia supernovae) that traditional astronomy uses. Pairing that distance with an independently measured redshift — from an identified host galaxy (a "bright" siren, as with GW170817) or, more painstakingly, from a statistical match against a galaxy catalog when no counterpart is seen (a "dark" siren) — yields H0 directly. The appeal is that this method is systematically independent of both existing techniques that disagree with each other: the cosmic microwave background (giving H0 ≈ 67 km/s/Mpc) and the local distance ladder (giving H0 ≈ 73 km/s/Mpc), a roughly 5-sigma discrepancy known as the Hubble tension. The 2023 LVK analysis combining 47 GWTC-3 events (mostly dark sirens) with GW170817 gives H0 = 68 (+12/−8) km/s/Mpc — a result whose error bars are wide enough to sit comfortably with *either* of the disagreeing values, so standard sirens cannot yet arbitrate the tension. The limiting factor is simply event statistics and localization precision: forecasts suggest that on the order of 50-100 well-measured standard sirens (bright or a larger sample of dark sirens) would be needed to reach the few-percent precision required to meaningfully weigh in, a milestone gravitational-wave astronomy is expected to approach only once catalogues grow by roughly another order of magnitude or next-generation detectors come online.

What ties these three debates together is that they are all, at bottom, statistical problems of insufficient sample size: formation-channel mixtures, a sub-threshold stochastic signal, and a cosmological parameter all require larger, cleaner catalogues than currently exist to resolve decisively. That is also why Day 6 (methods — how confidence and significance are actually assessed) and Day 7 (future detectors) follow naturally from today.

## Questions to think about

1. If both the isolated and dynamical formation channels contribute to the observed population, what additional observable (beyond mass, spin and eccentricity) might help assign an *individual* merger to one channel rather than the other?
2. Why is a stochastic background fundamentally harder to confirm than a single loud merger, even though — summed over the whole universe — it should carry much more total energy than any one event?
3. Standard sirens are "systematically independent" of the CMB and distance-ladder methods. Why does that independence matter so much for resolving the Hubble tension, even while the gravitational-wave estimate currently has the widest error bars of the three?
