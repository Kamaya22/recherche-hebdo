# Gravitational-wave astronomy — Day 4/7: Multi-messenger astronomy — GW170817, kilonovae, and the origin of heavy elements

## Context

Day 1 mentioned GW170817 in passing, as the event that turned gravitational-wave astronomy into *multi-messenger* astronomy. Days 2 and 3 then stayed with gravitational waves alone: how a signal is extracted from detector noise, and what the growing catalogue of mergers says about black-hole and neutron-star populations statistically. Today returns to that single 2017 event and asks what made it different from every other detection in the catalogue — and why it is still, a decade on, cited as one of the most information-rich nights in the history of astronomy.

On 17 August 2017, the LIGO-Virgo network detected the longest gravitational-wave signal yet seen — about 100 seconds of inspiral, consistent with two neutron stars of roughly 1.1-1.6 solar masses each, far lighter than the black holes of GW150914. Less than two seconds later, NASA's Fermi and ESA's INTEGRAL gamma-ray satellites independently flagged a short gamma-ray burst, GRB 170817A, from the same patch of sky — a coincidence so unlikely by chance that it was immediately treated as the same event seen in two different messengers. Within eleven hours, ground-based optical telescopes had pinpointed a new point of light in the galaxy NGC 4993, about 130 million light-years away, and in the following days and weeks some seventy observatories — spanning gamma-ray, X-ray, ultraviolet, optical, infrared, and radio instruments — tracked how that point of light evolved. That combination is why GW170817 matters beyond its own physics: it is the first (and so far clearest) case where the same astrophysical event was triangulated by gravitational waves, light, and (searched for but not found) neutrinos, each carrying different, complementary information that no single channel could have supplied alone.

Today's reading follows that information outward from the merger: what the near-simultaneous gamma-ray burst and gravitational-wave chirp jointly proved about the speed of gravity itself, what the fading optical/infrared glow (a "kilonova") revealed about freshly synthesized matter, and how, two years later, a single chemical element was identified by name in that afterglow — turning a theoretical idea about where gold and platinum come from into an observational result.

## Today's readings (3 items, ~21 min total)

**First Multimessenger Observations of a Neutron Star Merger** (Margutti & Chornock, *Annual Review of Astronomy and Astrophysics*, 2021) — https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-astro-112420-030742 — ~9 min (abstract, and the sections on the gamma-ray/gravitational-wave timing and the kilonova electromagnetic evolution; a free author-accepted-manuscript version is posted as arXiv:2012.04810 for readers without journal access). This is the standard peer-reviewed synthesis of everything GW170817's electromagnetic follow-up campaign found, commissioned by Annual Reviews, a nonprofit scholarly publisher — the most thorough single overview of the event available.

**Origin of the heavy elements in binary neutron-star mergers from a gravitational-wave event** (Kasen, Metzger, Barnes, Quataert & Ramirez-Ruiz, *Nature*, 2017) — https://doi.org/10.1038/nature24453 — ~7 min (abstract and the discussion of the two-component ejecta model; also free via arXiv:1710.05463). The original peer-reviewed modeling paper that matched GW170817's fading light curve to a kilonova produced by radioactive, neutron-rich ejecta containing freshly synthesized heavy elements. Written by leading kilonova theorists and published in Nature, it is the technical source behind most "neutron star mergers make gold" reporting.

**First Identification of a Heavy Element Born from Neutron Star Collision** (European Southern Observatory, press release eso1917, 2019) — https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso1917/ — ~5 min. ESO's own plain-language announcement of Watson et al.'s 2019 *Nature* study, which re-examined archival VLT/X-shooter spectra of GW170817's kilonova and identified strontium — the first time a specific heavy element (rather than just "heavy elements in general") was spectroscopically confirmed in the aftermath of a neutron-star merger. As the official channel of the observatory whose telescope took the data, it is a reliable, concise account of a result that moved the heavy-element story from modeling to direct identification.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

The first result from GW170817 had nothing to do with chemistry: it was a test of general relativity itself. Gravitational waves travel, in Einstein's theory, at exactly the speed of light. GRB 170817A's gamma rays arrived only 1.7 seconds after the gravitational-wave signal peaked, for a source roughly 130 million light-years — about 4×10^15 seconds of light-travel time — away. That tiny a delay, over that enormous a baseline, constrained any difference between the speed of gravity and the speed of light to better than one part in 10^15, ruling out or severely constraining a family of alternative gravity theories that had been proposed, in part, specifically to explain cosmic acceleration without dark energy. It is worth sitting with how unusual this is as a test of fundamental physics: it required no particle accelerator, only two space telescopes and a coincidence in time.

The gamma-ray burst itself settled an old astrophysical question. Short gamma-ray bursts (lasting under about two seconds) had been suspected since the 1990s to come from merging neutron stars, based on indirect evidence — their locations in galaxies, their lack of an associated supernova — but no merger had ever been caught in the act. GRB 170817A, with a confirmed gravitational-wave counterpart, was the first direct confirmation of that suspected progenitor, and its relative faintness (compared with the most luminous short bursts seen at cosmological distances) was best explained by the jet of high-energy particles being seen slightly off-axis, a detail later confirmed by radio observations that tracked the jet's structure over the following months.

The optical and infrared follow-up told a third, slower-moving story. The new source, catalogued as AT2017gfo, started out bright and blue within about half a day and then faded while reddening dramatically over the following one to two weeks — a color evolution unlike any supernova. Kasen and colleagues' modeling (this week's second reading) showed that this two-phase behavior fits a two-component "kilonova": merging neutron stars eject matter in two distinct ways, a smaller amount flung out dynamically during the collision itself, and a larger, slower wind driven off the hot, briefly surviving remnant disk afterward. Crucially, the two components end up with different neutron-richness, which changes what they can synthesize. All of this ejecta is thought to undergo the rapid neutron-capture process (the "r-process"): free neutrons, released in abundance by the merger, are captured by nuclei faster than those nuclei can radioactively decay, building up extremely heavy, neutron-rich isotopes that then decay down toward stability — the dominant proposed origin of roughly half of all elements heavier than iron, including essentially all of the universe's gold, platinum, and uranium. The ejecta component with a lower neutron-to-proton ratio produces mostly lighter r-process elements (roughly up to atomic mass 140) with relatively low opacity to visible light, which Kasen et al. argued explains the early blue glow; the more neutron-rich component produces heavier r-process elements, including lanthanides, whose complex electron structure makes them extremely opaque, trapping blue light and letting only the redder, infrared light escape — the explanation for the later, reddened glow. From the inferred ejecta mass and composition, Kasen and colleagues argued that a single event like GW170817 could produce a substantial fraction of a Galaxy's worth of heavy r-process material in one merger, and that if neutron-star mergers occur throughout the Milky Way's history at the rate gravitational-wave observations suggest, they could plausibly account for most of its inventory of elements like gold and platinum — a claim Day 5 will complicate, since the exact ejecta-mass figures (various follow-up papers have proposed ranges differing by a factor of several) and the merger-rate-versus-production-budget accounting are both live debates in the field.

That modeling argument rested on inferring element classes (light versus heavy r-process) from the shape and color of a light curve — a strong but indirect kind of evidence. Watson and colleagues' 2019 study (behind this week's third reading) closed part of that gap by going back to the original spectra — not just their overall brightness and color, but the detailed pattern of light absorbed at specific wavelengths — and identifying two broad absorption features, seen about 1.5 days after the merger, as the spectral signature of ionized strontium. Strontium is a comparatively light neutron-capture element (atomic number 38), so this was not proof that the merger made gold specifically, but it was the first time any single element, rather than an inferred class of elements, had been directly identified in the debris of a neutron-star merger — turning "the color evolution is consistent with r-process opacities" into "we can name an atom that was there." Later reanalyses of the same and subsequent kilonova data have reported additional candidate signatures (including tentative evidence for yttrium), but strontium's identification remains the most widely cited of these direct spectroscopic detections.

Together, these three results are why GW170817 is treated as a turning point rather than just a second detection: a timing coincidence that tested gravity to unprecedented precision, a burst that confirmed decades of indirect reasoning about gamma-ray-burst progenitors, and an afterglow that took the long-theorized idea of cosmic nucleosynthesis in neutron-star mergers from models to a named element in a spectrum.

## Questions to think about

1. The 1.7-second gamma-ray delay constrained the relative speed of gravity and light to about one part in 10^15 using a single, lucky coincidence. What makes this kind of "natural experiment" powerful compared with a dedicated laboratory test, and what are its limits (for instance, if no such coincident event had ever been observed)?
2. The case for neutron-star mergers as a dominant source of heavy elements rests on combining an ejecta-mass estimate from one well-observed event with a merger-rate estimate from the broader gravitational-wave catalogue (Day 3). What would make you more or less confident extrapolating from a sample of essentially one well-studied kilonova to a claim about the chemical history of the entire Galaxy?
3. Strontium's spectroscopic identification took two years of reanalysis after the original detection. What does that gap suggest about the difference between what a kilonova campaign can report in real time (as data for one event flooded in from seventy observatories) and what the same data later supports once more careful methods are applied to it?
