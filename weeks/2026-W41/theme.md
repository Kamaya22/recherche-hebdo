# Gravitational-wave astronomy

How detectors like LIGO opened a new window on the universe and what they've found.

## Why it matters

For most of history, astronomy meant reading light — visible, radio, X-ray — emitted by distant objects. Gravitational waves, ripples in spacetime itself predicted by Einstein in 1916, offer an entirely different channel: they are produced by the bulk motion of mass itself, pass through matter almost unimpeded, and let us "hear" cataclysms — colliding black holes, merging neutron stars — that emit little or no light. Their first direct detection in 2015, a century after the prediction, confirmed general relativity in its most extreme regime and immediately revealed a hidden population of stellar-mass black holes that electromagnetic astronomy had never seen. Since then, a growing international network of detectors (LIGO, Virgo, KAGRA) has logged hundreds of merger events, triggered the new field of multi-messenger astronomy, and turned gravitational waves into tools for testing gravity, measuring the universe's expansion, and tracing the life cycles of compact stars. The field is still young, methodologically unusual (it is as much signal-processing and statistics as astrophysics), and is about to be transformed again by next-generation ground detectors and the space-based LISA mission.

## Tentative 7-day outline

1. **Day 1 — Panorama**: what gravitational waves are, how interferometric detectors catch them, the 2015 breakthrough, why the field matters.
2. **Day 2 — Mechanisms**: how LIGO/Virgo/KAGRA actually extract a signal from noise — interferometry, the noise budget (seismic, thermal, quantum), matched filtering against waveform templates.
3. **Day 3 — Key findings**: the growing catalogue of mergers (GWTC), what it reveals about black hole and neutron star populations, masses, spins, and merger rates.
4. **Day 4 — Multi-messenger astronomy**: GW170817, the neutron-star merger seen in both gravitational waves and light, kilonovae, and the origin of heavy elements.
5. **Day 5 — Controversies and open debates**: how binary systems actually form (dynamical vs. isolated channels), the stochastic gravitational-wave background, and the "Hubble tension" as seen through standard sirens.
6. **Day 6 — Methods**: how we know a detection is real — statistical significance, Bayesian parameter estimation, numerical-relativity waveforms, data-quality vetoes, and the role of machine learning.
7. **Day 7 — Synthesis**: fundamental-physics tests of general relativity, future detectors (Einstein Telescope, Cosmic Explorer, LISA), what is settled vs. open, and pointers for going deeper.

The outline is a guide, not a contract, and may shift slightly as the week's research turns up more specific angles.
