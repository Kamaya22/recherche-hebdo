# Vaccine hesitancy and trust in science — Day 2/7: Weighing a known risk against an unknown one

## Context

Day 1 established that vaccine hesitancy is not one thing: the WHO's 3Cs (confidence, complacency, convenience) and their 2018 extension into the 5C model (adding constraints, calculation, and collective responsibility) describe a landscape of psychological antecedents rather than a single trait of "informed" versus "misinformed" people. One loose thread from yesterday is worth pulling on directly: the finding that high "calculation" — actively searching for and weighing information — does not reliably track with *less* hesitancy. That is strange only if you assume vaccination decisions are primarily a reasoning problem solved by more facts. Today's readings suggest a different picture: much of what drives the decision happens in how risks are *felt* and *framed* before any explicit weighing of facts occurs, and some of what looks like a clean cognitive bias turns out to be less robust under direct experimental test than its popularity in public-health writing would suggest.

Three mechanisms are in play today, and they operate at different distances from conscious reasoning. First, there is an asymmetry in how a known, self-caused risk (a vaccine side effect) is weighed against a diffuse, probabilistic, other-caused risk (catching a disease) — this is where "omission bias" research lives, though as you will see, its empirical status is more contested than many secondary summaries let on. Second, there is dread: some risks feel more threatening than their statistics justify because they are unfamiliar, uncontrollable, or imposed on you by someone else, independent of any bias in how action versus inaction is scored. Third, there is motivated reasoning: the tendency to evaluate evidence in a way that protects a prior identity or worldview commitment, which predicts that simply presenting more accurate information will sometimes do nothing, or backfire.

Why keep these three apart rather than lumping them into "psychological bias"? Because they point to different levers. If the dominant mechanism were a pure cognitive bias in how harms-from-action are scored, debiasing training or reframing might help. If it is dread shaped by unfamiliarity, exposure and normalization might matter more. If it is motivated reasoning anchored in identity, the deficit model of "just provide the facts" is close to useless and can even entrench the position — a claim Day 5 (controversies and what actually changes minds) will test directly against the intervention evidence.

## Today's readings (3 items, ~21 min total)

**Making sense of perceptions of risk of diseases and vaccinations: a qualitative study combining models of health beliefs, decision-making and risk perception** (Bond & Nolan, *BMC Public Health*, 2011) — https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2458-11-943 — ~8 min (abstract, background, and key findings). A peer-reviewed, open-access qualitative study (45 semi-structured interviews with Australian parents, using a hypothetical new flu strain as a probe) that found immunizing and non-immunizing parents dread different things: immunizers dread the unfamiliar disease, non-immunizers dread the unknown, long-term side effects of the vaccine. It is a trustworthy source because it is transparent about being qualitative and exploratory, and it explicitly draws on established risk-perception theory (dread, unfamiliarity, uncontrollability) rather than inventing new constructs.

**No evidence that omission and confirmation biases affect the perception and recall of vaccine-related information** (Jiménez, Mesoudi & Tehrani, *PLOS ONE*, 2020) — https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0228898 — ~7 min (abstract, introduction, and discussion). A peer-reviewed, open-access experimental study (202 participants, between-subjects design comparing imagined vaccine side effects versus imagined disease symptoms) that directly tested whether people judge and remember action-caused harms as worse than equivalent inaction-caused harms. It found no support for either bias. This is the most methodologically direct of today's sources — a controlled experiment rather than a survey or interview — and it is included precisely because it complicates the tidy "omission bias explains vaccine hesitancy" story that is common in secondary write-ups.

**The psychological roots of anti-vaccination attitudes: A 24-nation investigation** (Hornsey, Harris & Fielding, *Health Psychology*, 2018) — https://doi.org/10.1037/hea0000586 — ~6 min (abstract and discussion of the motivated-reasoning framing and the deficit-model critique). A peer-reviewed study (an online survey of 5,323 people across 24 countries) that frames anti-vaccination attitudes through motivated reasoning, finding that conspiracy-theory endorsement tracked with anti-vaccination attitudes in every country sampled, while education had only a small effect. It is trustworthy as a large-scale, cross-national survey published in a mainstream health-psychology journal, though — like any cross-sectional survey — it establishes association, not causation.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with "omission bias," because it is probably the single most-cited psychological concept in popular writing about vaccine hesitancy, and today's reading complicates it. The concept has a longer history in this literature than today's two formal readings alone suggest: a 2007 taxonomy published in the journal *Vaccine* already catalogued omission-type reasoning, alongside several other cognitive and motivational flaws, as one recurring pattern behind anti-vaccine claims, borrowing a general classification of reasoning errors from decision-making research rather than inventing a vaccine-specific one. The basic idea, borrowed from general decision-making research, is that people judge a harm they caused by *acting* (e.g., a rare vaccine side effect) as morally and emotionally worse than an equivalent or larger harm that resulted from *not acting* (e.g., catching a preventable disease) — even when the numbers say the harm from inaction is more likely or more severe. If true, this would mean parents are not simply miscalculating probabilities; they are applying a different, asymmetric moral weighting to self-caused versus not-self-caused outcomes, and the disease's harm is partly invisible to them because it never required a decision. The problem is that when Jiménez, Mesoudi and Tehrani built a direct experimental test — asking people to imagine either the vaccine-side-effect scenario or the disease scenario, then rate severity and complete a surprise recall test — they found no evidence that the vaccine-caused harm was rated worse or remembered better than the disease-caused harm. That null result does not mean the broader pattern (hesitant people treating vaccine risk and disease risk asymmetrically) is wrong; it means the specific mechanism researchers assumed was producing that pattern may not be the right explanation, or may only operate under conditions this study did not capture.

That distinction matters a great deal, because the broader asymmetric pattern shows up clearly in Bond and Nolan's qualitative interviews, just described in different terms: dread and unfamiliarity rather than an action/inaction asymmetry. Immunizing parents, in their framework, feared the disease because it was unfamiliar — most parents today have never seen a child with measles or pertussis, so the threat is abstract. Non-immunizing parents feared the vaccine because its long-term effects felt unknown and uncontrollable — a concern not about probability as much as about who controls the outcome and how legible the risk is. This is a genuinely different psychological story from omission bias: it does not require assuming people weight self-caused harms more heavily, only that *unfamiliar* and *uncontrollable* threats feel bigger than *familiar*, even if statistically larger, ones. The practical upshot is one of today's more important, slightly humbling lessons: two groups of researchers can describe what looks like the same observable behavior (parents who treat vaccine risk and disease risk asymmetrically) and reach for different underlying mechanisms, and a popular, intuitive label (omission bias) can outrun the evidence that specifically supports it. Readers of secondary coverage of vaccine psychology should treat "it's omission bias" as a hypothesis still being tested, not a settled finding — consistent with Day 1's point that the 3C/5C frameworks were built to describe the landscape, not to hand out a single tidy mechanism.

The third mechanism, motivated reasoning, operates at a different level entirely — not how a specific risk is scored or felt, but how evidence in general is processed once a person's prior identity or worldview is engaged. Hornsey, Harris and Fielding's 24-country survey found that belief in unrelated conspiracy theories (not specifically about vaccines) was the strongest and most consistent correlate of anti-vaccination attitudes, holding across very different countries and healthcare systems, while formal education had only a modest effect. The inference they draw — and this is the piece that connects directly back to Day 1's "calculation paradox" — is that for a meaningful share of hesitant people, the issue is not an information deficit at all. If it were, more education or more facts should reliably reduce hesitancy, and it mostly does not. Motivated reasoning implies people are not failing to find the right facts; they are filtering available facts through a prior commitment (distrust of concentrated institutional power, in the conspiracy-belief case) that determines which facts register as credible. This reframes the earlier puzzle about "calculation": a person who searches extensively but filters everything through a fixed prior is not failing to calculate, they are calculating from a different starting point than public-health communicators assume.

Put together, today's three mechanisms are not competing explanations for a single phenomenon so much as three layers that can all be true of the same person at different moments: dread/unfamiliarity shapes which risks feel vivid before any explicit reasoning starts; a specific omission-type bias in scoring self-caused versus not-self-caused harm may or may not be doing additional work (the evidence is genuinely mixed); and motivated reasoning determines how new information is filtered once it is encountered, especially for people whose hesitancy is anchored in a broader distrust of institutions rather than a narrow concern about one vaccine. This layering is exactly why Day 3 turns to the social and historical determinants of that institutional trust (or distrust) — because motivated reasoning does not appear from nowhere, it is downstream of a person's social position and history with the systems asking them to trust it.

## Questions to think about

1. If Bond and Nolan's dread/unfamiliarity asymmetry is a robust pattern but Jiménez, Mesoudi and Tehrani's specific test of omission bias found nothing, what would a researcher need to show to argue these are actually the same underlying mechanism rather than two different ones that happen to produce similar behavior?
2. Hornsey, Harris and Fielding's survey is cross-sectional: it shows conspiracy belief and anti-vaccination attitudes are correlated in every country sampled, not which one causes the other (or whether both stem from a third factor, like general institutional distrust). What kind of study design could distinguish these possibilities?
3. If motivated reasoning, rather than lack of information, explains a meaningful share of hesitancy, does that change what counts as an ethically acceptable public-health communication strategy — and where might "working with someone's worldview" cross into manipulation?
