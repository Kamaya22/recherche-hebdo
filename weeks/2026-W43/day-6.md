# Vaccine hesitancy and trust in science — Day 6/7: How researchers actually measure hesitancy

## Context

Every claim this week has rested on measurement that this reading has not yet examined directly. Day 1's 3C/5C frameworks, Day 4's distinction between misinformation-driven and distrust-driven skepticism, and Day 5's verdict on mandates, motivational interviewing and debunking all depend on someone, somewhere, having found a way to turn "hesitancy" — an attitude, a delay, a refusal — into a number that can be compared across people, places and time. That turns out to be genuinely hard. Hesitancy is not a single observable behavior like "vaccinated" or "not vaccinated"; it is a disposition that can produce very different behaviors (delay two months, skip one dose, refuse all of them) and that people do not always report honestly, especially when a researcher or clinician is standing in front of them. Today asks how the field closes that gap: what instruments it uses to measure the psychological side, what data it uses to measure actual behavior, and — because running a randomized trial on an entire state's vaccination law is neither ethical nor possible — how it estimates the causal effect of real-world policy changes without one.

Three distinct measurement problems run through today's three readings, and they map onto a logical sequence. First, how do you standardize a *survey instrument* so that "hesitancy" measured in one country, clinic or year means the same thing as "hesitancy" measured in another — a problem Day 1's 5C scale addressed for research purposes, but which public-health *programs* needed solved differently, for routine monitoring rather than one-off studies. Second, once many such instruments exist, how trustworthy is the resulting literature as a whole — are these tools actually validated, and applied in geographically and demographically representative settings, or is the evidence base itself lopsided in ways that limit what conclusions travel from one place to another? Third, and most consequentially for anything Day 5 claimed about mandates: since you cannot randomly assign a state to abolish vaccine exemptions and another to keep them, how do researchers extract a credible causal estimate from a policy change that happened anyway, using the same kind of administrative data that counts actual children, not reported attitudes?

This matters beyond academic tidiness. Every number quoted earlier this week — "92-93% national MMR coverage," "95% herd-immunity threshold," pooled effect sizes from Day 5's meta-analyses — is only as trustworthy as the measurement infrastructure that produced it. A reading that spent five days discussing what drives hesitancy without ever asking how anyone counted it would be building on an unexamined foundation.

## Today's readings (3 items, ~20 min total)

**Behavioural and social drivers of vaccination: tools and practical guidance for achieving high uptake** (World Health Organization, 2022) — https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240049680 — ~7 min (overview, the four-domain framework, and the guidance on using the tools). This is WHO's official implementation toolkit, developed by a technical working group convened in 2018, for routine, standardized measurement of vaccination behavior across national immunization programs — not a one-off research instrument but field-tested survey tools, indicators and a codebook meant to be reused across countries and over time. It is trustworthy as the leading institutional standard in this space: WHO is the recognized global authority on immunization policy, and the toolkit's four-domain structure (thinking and feeling, social processes, motivation, practical issues) has since been adopted or adapted in national surveys worldwide.

**Overview of Tools and Measures Investigating Vaccine Hesitancy in a Ten Year Period: A Scoping Review** (Oduwole, Pienaar, Mahomed & Wiysonge, *Vaccines*, 2022) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9412526 — ~7 min (abstract, methods, and the discussion of gaps). A peer-reviewed, open-access scoping review (MDPI's *Vaccines*, following a registered BMJ Open protocol) that systematically charted the measurement tools used across the field between 2010 and 2019 — what they measured, whether they were validated, and where the underlying studies were conducted. It is trustworthy precisely because its purpose is methodological self-scrutiny: rather than measuring hesitancy itself, it audits how the field as a whole has been measuring it, which is exactly today's question.

**Elimination of Nonmedical Immunization Exemptions in California and School-Entry Vaccine Status** (Delamater, Pingali, Buttenheim, Salmon, Klein & Omer, *Pediatrics*, 2019) — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31113831/ — ~6 min (abstract and the summary of year-one and year-two findings). A peer-reviewed quasi-experimental analysis of California's 2016 law (SB277), which eliminated personal-belief exemptions from school-entry vaccination requirements — the same category of policy tool Day 5 discussed in the abstract. It is trustworthy because it uses statewide administrative kindergarten-enrollment data (not self-reported attitudes) before and after the law took effect, and because the authors report a result that complicates rather than flatters the policy, rather than only reporting the topline number that looks good.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with why this is hard in the first place. Hesitancy is a disposition, not a single measurable act, and self-report carries two well-known distortions: social desirability bias (people shade answers toward what they think a researcher or clinician wants to hear) and the intention-behavior gap (reported willingness to vaccinate predicts actual vaccination only imperfectly — someone can sincerely intend to vaccinate a child next month and then not get around to it, for reasons that have nothing to do with "hesitancy" as the 3C/5C frameworks define it). That is why the field needs at least two complementary kinds of measurement: validated survey instruments that get at the psychological and social *why*, and administrative or behavioral data that record the *what actually happened* — whether a dose was given, a registry updated, a child entering kindergarten without the required vaccines. Today's three readings correspond, respectively, to standardizing the first kind of measurement across a global program, auditing how well the field as a whole has done that, and showing what the second kind of measurement — paired with a real policy change — can and cannot tell you.

The WHO's Behavioural and Social Drivers of vaccination (BeSD) framework is the field's current answer to the first problem, and it is a deliberate evolution beyond the 5C model Day 1 introduced. Where 5C (confidence, complacency, constraints, calculation, collective responsibility) was built primarily as a *research* instrument to identify psychological antecedents, BeSD was built for *programs*: national and subnational immunization managers who need a standardized, field-tested, repeatable survey they can run routinely to track coverage gaps and target interventions, not just publish findings about them. Its four domains — thinking and feeling (confidence-like beliefs about vaccine safety and effectiveness), social processes (norms, recommendations from trusted people), motivation (readiness and intention, including hesitancy itself), and practical issues (access, cost, logistics) — map only loosely onto 5C's five antecedents. The clearest structural difference is that BeSD gives "practical issues" equal standing as a measurement domain alongside the psychological ones, which matters because it refuses to let programs collapse a coverage gap into "the population is hesitant" when the real driver, measurable with the same instrument, might be that a clinic is only open during work hours. That design choice reflects a lesson the whole week has circled: declining to vaccinate and being unable to vaccinate conveniently can look identical in aggregate coverage numbers, and only a tool built to separate them (rather than one that assumes hesitancy from low coverage alone) can tell a program which problem it actually has.

Having a good instrument does not mean the field has used good instruments consistently, which is exactly what Oduwole and colleagues' scoping review goes looking for. Charting the measurement tools used in 26 studies across the ten years before BeSD's 2022 release, they found a field far less standardized than a reader of any single study might assume: a proliferation of tools of inconsistent quality, applied overwhelmingly to routine childhood vaccination, and — the finding most relevant to how far any of this week's conclusions can be expected to travel — geographically lopsided, with only one of the 26 included studies conducted in the WHO African Region. That is not a minor caveat. If the measurement tools themselves were built and validated mostly in high-income settings, then every downstream number this week has cited (herd-immunity thresholds aside, which are epidemiological rather than psychological) carries an implicit geographic asterisk: confidence that a scale validated on, say, European or North American parents measures the same underlying construct the same way among parents in under-studied regions is an assumption, not a demonstrated fact. The review's own call to action is specifically for more contextually validated tools outside the regions where the field's instruments were originally built and tested — a direct, practical consequence of taking measurement seriously rather than treating it as a solved background problem.

The third reading moves from measuring attitudes to measuring behavior directly, and from observational survey data to a quasi-experimental design. California's SB277, which took effect in 2016, eliminated personal-belief exemptions for school-entry vaccination — removing, in effect, the main legal route by which a hesitant or refusing parent could enroll an unvaccinated child in school without a medical justification. Because the law changed abruptly and statewide, Delamater and colleagues could treat it as something close to a natural experiment: compare the same population's vaccination status, using the state's own kindergarten-enrollment records, before and after the policy took effect, rather than relying on anyone's self-reported intentions. The topline result looks like a clean win for the policy, in the direction Day 5's review of mandates would predict: the share of kindergartners entering school without up-to-date vaccinations fell from 7.15% to 4.42% in the law's first year, driven mainly by a sharp drop in "conditional entrants" (children admitted provisionally while catching up on doses). But the paper's most important methodological contribution is what it does next, rather than stopping there: tracking where the removed personal-belief exemptions actually went. The authors found a measurable "replacement effect" — a rise in medical exemptions and in exemptions for independent-study or homeschooled children that absorbed a meaningful share of what would otherwise have been a larger coverage gain — and, in year two, the not-up-to-date rate ticked back up by 0.45 percentage points even as personal-belief exemptions kept falling, with pre-existing geographic clusters of vaccine refusal persisting largely unchanged.

Put the three readings together and a methodological throughline emerges that qualifies, without undermining, everything this week has concluded from the underlying literature. A standardized instrument like BeSD can only measure what it was built and validated to measure, in the populations it was built and validated on — which is why Oduwole and colleagues' finding about geographic skew is not a side note but a limit on how confidently any hesitancy finding generalizes globally. And even the cleanest available substitute for an impossible randomized trial — a real policy change, measured with hard administrative data rather than self-report — can produce a number (the drop to 4.42%) that is accurate and still misleading if read in isolation, because it does not by itself distinguish "parents became less hesitant" from "parents who remained just as unwilling found a different legal exit." That distinction is not a technicality: Day 5 asked whether mandates move the strongly hesitant core and found they mostly do not; Delamater and colleagues' replacement effect is a concrete demonstration of exactly that limitation, visible only because the researchers measured actual administrative categories (exemption type, entry mechanism) rather than a single before/after coverage percentage.

## Questions to think about

1. Delamater and colleagues found that eliminating personal-belief exemptions partly shifted parents toward medical exemptions and independent-study enrollment rather than toward vaccination. What additional data, beyond the exemption-category breakdown they used, would let a researcher distinguish "these medical exemptions are medically appropriate" from "this is a new loophole performing the same function as the old one"?
2. Oduwole and colleagues found only one of 26 studies measuring hesitancy came from the WHO African Region. If a global health program wanted to deploy the WHO's BeSD tools confidently in a region with little prior validation work, what would count as sufficient evidence that the tool is measuring the same construct there as where it was developed?
3. BeSD treats "practical issues" (access, cost, logistics) as a measurement domain alongside psychological drivers, explicitly separating "won't" from "can't." Given that distinction, what would it take for a program using only aggregate coverage data (without BeSD-style survey data) to mistakenly diagnose a practical-access problem as hesitancy, or vice versa — and what would that misdiagnosis cost in terms of the wrong intervention being deployed?
