# Vaccine hesitancy and trust in science — Day 1/7: What hesitancy is, and why it is back in the news

## Context

In 1998, before most current vaccination programs existed in their present form, the idea that a parent in a wealthy country might decline a free, well-tested childhood vaccine on principle was treated as a fringe position. In 2025 the United States recorded its largest measles outbreak since 1992, with clusters concentrated in communities with below-threshold MMR coverage, and national kindergarten coverage sitting around 92-93%, short of the roughly 95% needed for herd immunity against a disease as contagious as measles. Measles is a useful index case precisely because the vaccine is old, cheap, extremely well studied, and extraordinarily effective — if hesitancy can erode uptake of *this* vaccine, it says something about the phenomenon that goes well beyond any single disease or any single discredited claim.

This week treats vaccine hesitancy as a psychology-and-sociology problem rather than an immunology one, because the gap between "a vaccine exists and works" and "a given person accepts it today" is where almost all of the interesting and actionable science lives. The field's foundational move, formalized by a World Health Organization expert working group in 2015, was to stop treating non-vaccination as a single category (ignorant, anti-science, or simply lazy) and instead define vaccine hesitancy as a *spectrum*: delay or refusal of vaccination despite the availability of vaccination services, which varies by time, place, and even by which specific vaccine is in question. A parent can be fully confident in the polio vaccine and hesitant about a newer one; hesitancy is not a fixed trait of a person, it is a state that is shaped by context.

Why cover this now, and why as a full week rather than a single reading? Three reasons converge. First, the applied stakes are rising, not falling: 2025's global measles coverage has stagnated for over a decade, and outbreaks (US, Europe, parts of the Middle East and Africa) are a live test of how fragile herd immunity can become once uptake drifts below threshold in enough local pockets. Second, the explanatory frameworks the field uses (the 3Cs, later extended to 5C) are genuinely useful general-purpose tools for thinking about any health behavior that mixes small personal risk against diffuse collective benefit — the same logic shows up in mask-wearing, antibiotic use, and climate behavior. Third, hesitancy is often wrongly collapsed into "misinformation," when the research literature treats misinformation as only one driver among several — a point Day 4 will unpack in detail, after today lays out the baseline definitions and models the rest of the week will build on.

## Today's readings (3 items, ~21 min total)

**Vaccine hesitancy: Definition, scope and determinants** (SAGE Working Group on Vaccine Hesitancy, *Vaccine*, 2015) — https://www.technet-21.org/en/resources/journal-article/vaccine-hesitancy-definition-scope-and-determinants — ~8 min (the definition, scope, and Determinants Matrix sections). This is the foundational document of the field: the peer-reviewed report of the WHO Strategic Advisory Group of Experts (SAGE) working group convened specifically to give "vaccine hesitancy" a precise, usable definition after SAGE flagged declining uptake in both developed and developing countries. It introduces the widely used "3Cs" (confidence, complacency, convenience) and the three-category Determinants Matrix (contextual, individual/group, vaccine-specific influences) that nearly all later work in the field references or extends.

**Beyond confidence: Development of a measure assessing the 5C psychological antecedents of vaccination** (Betsch, Schmid, Heinemeier, Korn, Holtmann & Böhm, *PLOS ONE*, 2018) — https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0208601 — ~8 min (abstract, introduction, and the description of the five antecedents). A peer-reviewed, open-access psychometric study that built and validated a survey scale extending the WHO's 3Cs into five measurable psychological antecedents: confidence, complacency, constraints, calculation, and collective responsibility. It is the standard instrument researchers now use to quantify hesitancy in survey data, and it matters for today's purposes because it shows precisely which individual-level factors (beyond simple "trust") the field considers to be doing the causal work — several of which (calculation, collective responsibility) will come back later this week.

**Contagion comeback: unravelling the measles outbreak across the USA** (Tahir, Kumar, Faisal, Gill, Kumari, Tahir & Haque, *Frontiers in Public Health*, 2024) — https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2024.1491927/epub — ~5 min (overview of the epidemiology and drivers). A peer-reviewed Perspective article in an open-access public-health journal that lays out the recent rise in US measles cases, declining MMR coverage figures, and the mix of factors (misinformation, exemptions, pandemic-era disruption to routine childhood vaccination) behind it. It grounds today's reading in the concrete, current case that motivates the rest of the week.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with the definition, because getting it right changes what counts as evidence. The WHO working group's 2015 report defines vaccine hesitancy as a *delay* in accepting, or an outright *refusal* of, vaccination despite vaccination services being available. Two things follow from that wording. First, it deliberately excludes situations where low vaccination simply reflects lack of access — hesitancy is specifically about a behavioral gap that exists even when a vaccine is sitting there, free, at a clinic down the street. Second, "delay" and "refusal" cover very different people: someone who vaccinates their child two months late out of mild anxiety and someone who refuses on principle both count as "hesitant" in this framework, even though their psychology, and the interventions that would reach them, are likely very different. The working group's answer to "what causes it" was the 3Cs: confidence (trust in the vaccine, the provider, and the system that recommends it), complacency (whether the disease itself is still perceived as a serious threat — easier to feel complacent about a disease you've never seen), and convenience (practical access, cost, timing). Crucially, the group organized determinants into three nested levels — contextual (historical, cultural, political, economic), individual/group (personal risk perception, social networks, past experience), and vaccine-specific (how the vaccine is delivered, its perceived risk/benefit, how it is introduced) — which is the key conceptual move the rest of this week will keep returning to: hesitancy is never purely psychological or purely social, it sits at the intersection, and a theory that only looks at one level will systematically miss cases.

The 3Cs framework was useful but hard to measure directly, which is where the 2018 Betsch et al. paper comes in. Their contribution was methodological as much as conceptual: a validated psychometric scale (the "5C" model) that operationalizes five antecedents researchers can actually ask about in a survey and score. Confidence and complacency carry over directly from the 3Cs. "Convenience" is replaced and broadened into constraints, covering both structural barriers (cost, distance, time off work) and psychological ones (how much effort a person perceives vaccination to require). The two genuinely new additions are calculation — how actively a person searches for and weighs information, including risks and benefits, before deciding — and collective responsibility — willingness to vaccinate to protect others via herd immunity, independent of personal risk. Calculation deserves a pause here, because it complicates a tempting but wrong intuition: that hesitant people are simply under-informed, and that hesitancy is solved by providing more facts. Betsch and colleagues' own later work (and other research this week will touch on) finds that high "calculation" — actively searching for and weighing information — is not reliably associated with *less* hesitancy. People who search the most are not necessarily the most confident in the result; sometimes extensive searching is itself a symptom of unresolved doubt, and it can also be how a person encounters and settles on low-quality information. This is an early, important clue that "fill the knowledge gap" is too simple a theory of change — a thread Day 2 (psychological mechanisms) and Day 5 (what actually changes minds) will both pick up.

Why put all of this in a 2026 context rather than treating it as settled 2015-2018 theory? Because the applied picture described in the Frontiers in Public Health perspective shows these frameworks are not academic exercises — they map directly onto a live epidemiological event. The piece describes the United States recording its largest measles resurgence since 1992, driven by a combination of factors that line up neatly with the Determinants Matrix: contextual factors (political polarization around pandemic-era mandates, state-level policy differences in vaccine exemptions), individual/group factors (parental risk perception shaped by the fact that almost no parent today has seen a child with measles, so complacency about the disease itself is high), and vaccine-specific factors (persistent, decades-old misinformation linking the MMR vaccine to autism, despite it being one of the most thoroughly investigated and refuted claims in modern epidemiology). The epidemiological mechanism is unforgiving and nonlinear: measles requires roughly 95% population immunity to reliably prevent sustained transmission, so a national coverage figure of 92-93% can look reassuringly close to that threshold while still allowing outbreaks to take hold in specific under-vaccinated communities, because national averages mask local clustering — hesitancy is rarely uniformly distributed, it concentrates in particular schools, neighborhoods, or social networks, which is exactly why Day 3's focus on social determinants and networks matters as much as individual psychology.

One more framing point worth carrying through the week: none of today's three sources treats hesitancy as irrational in some simple sense. The 3C/5C frameworks were deliberately built to be agnostic about whether a given person's confidence, complacency, or calculation is "justified" — they describe the psychological landscape a person is navigating, not a verdict on whether they navigated it well. This matters because the most common popular framing of vaccine hesitancy (people are misinformed, or people are irrational) tends to produce interventions that only work on the subset of hesitant people for whom that framing happens to be true, while alienating everyone else. Keeping definition and mechanism separate from moral judgment is what allows the field to ask an empirical question — what actually predicts and changes vaccination behavior — rather than a rhetorical one.

## Questions to think about

1. The WHO definition explicitly separates "hesitancy" from "lack of access." In practice, how confident can researchers be that a given low-uptake community is hesitant rather than underserved, and what would distinguish the two in the data available to a health system?
2. If high "calculation" (active information-seeking) does not reliably reduce hesitancy, what does that imply about public-health communication strategies that are built around providing more information or more detailed risk statistics?
3. The 5C model treats collective responsibility (vaccinating to protect others) as a distinct antecedent from personal risk calculation. Why might this matter specifically for diseases like measles, where the people most protected by herd immunity (infants too young to be vaccinated, immunocompromised people) are not the ones making the vaccination decision?

## This week's theme

**Vaccine hesitancy and trust in science** — the psychology and sociology of why people delay or refuse effective vaccines, even when they are free, available, and backed by overwhelming evidence.

Vaccines are one of the most effective interventions in the history of public health, yet uptake of even long-established vaccines has been falling in many countries, and 2025 saw the largest US measles outbreak in more than three decades. This is not primarily a story about access — in most current cases, vaccination services exist, but people delay, decline, or vacillate. The WHO's 2015 "vaccine hesitancy" framework and its 2018 extension into measurable psychological antecedents reframed non-vaccination as a spectrum shaped jointly by individual psychology, social context, and information ecosystems, rather than a single trait of "informed" versus "misinformed" people. This week works through that framework level by level: individual cognition, social and historical determinants, misinformation dynamics, what the evidence says about changing minds, and how the whole phenomenon is actually measured.
