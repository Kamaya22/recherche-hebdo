# Vaccine hesitancy and trust in science — Day 3/7: Networks, institutions, and the weight of history

## Context

Day 1 laid out the 3C/5C frameworks, in which "confidence" — trust in the vaccine, the provider, and the system recommending it — is one of the central psychological antecedents of hesitancy. Day 2 went a level deeper into individual cognition: dread of the unfamiliar, a contested omission-bias story, and motivated reasoning anchored in conspiracy belief. But Day 2 ended on a specific thread worth pulling further: motivated reasoning does not appear from nowhere, it is downstream of a person's social position and history with the institutions asking them to trust it. Today zooms out from the individual to the social structure around them — two mechanisms that the 3C/5C framework's "contextual" determinants level gestures at but does not itself explain.

The first mechanism is structural rather than psychological: vaccination attitudes are not independently distributed across a population, they cluster in social networks, which means hesitancy is partly a property of *groups*, not just individuals. The second is historical: for some communities, "institutional distrust" is not a bias to be corrected or a deficit of information, but a documented, rational response to specific, verifiable past harms committed by medicine and the state. Treating both of these as a single "social level" of the Determinants Matrix would flatten an important distinction — one is about how attitudes propagate through a network regardless of their content, the other is about why a particular attitude (distrust) is well-founded for a particular group. Keeping them separate sets up Day 4's question directly: how do we tell hesitancy driven by the spread of misinformation apart from hesitancy driven by the spread of a historically justified, legitimate distrust, when both can look identical from the outside (a parent who says "I don't trust what they're telling us")?

## Today's readings (3 items, ~22 min total)

**Transmission of Vaccination Attitudes and Uptake Based on Social Contagion Theory: A Scoping Review** (Konstantinou, Georgiou, Kumar, Kyprianidou, Nicolaides, Karekla & Kassianos, *Vaccines*, 2021) — https://doi.org/10.3390/vaccines9060607 — ~7 min (abstract, results on social exposure and homophily). A peer-reviewed, open-access scoping review (PubMed, PsycINFO, Embase and Scopus searched, 11 cross-sectional studies included, narrative synthesis) testing whether vaccination attitudes behave like a contagious phenomenon spreading through social networks. It is a trustworthy source because it is explicit about being a scoping review of observational, cross-sectional evidence (association, not proof of transmission), and because its central finding — homophily along racial and ethnic lines shaping who influences whom — is reported as a specific, checkable result rather than a general claim.

**Determinants of Vaccine Hesitancy among African American and Black Individuals in the United States of America: A Systematic Literature Review** (Savoia, Masterson, Olander, Anderson, Mohamed Farah & Pirrotta, *Vaccines*, 2024) — https://doi.org/10.3390/vaccines12030277 — ~9 min (abstract, and the synthesis of quantitative and qualitative themes). A peer-reviewed, open-access, PRISMA-guided systematic review (literature searched through December 2022, synthesizing 16 quantitative and 14 qualitative studies on COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy specifically among Black and African American adults in the US). It is trustworthy because it follows a registered, PRISMA-based protocol and explicitly separates two distinct qualitative themes — historical mistrust and present-day institutional mistrust — rather than collapsing them into one undifferentiated "distrust" category.

**The lasting impact of the Tuskegee Syphilis Study: COVID-19 vaccination hesitation among African Americans** (Hou, Jiao, Shen & Chen, *Journal of Population Economics*, 2024) — https://doi.org/10.1007/s00148-024-01013-y — ~6 min (abstract and the geographic-distance design). A peer-reviewed economics paper using county-level CDC vaccination-tracker and US Census data as a natural experiment: it compares how fast the Black-white COVID-19 vaccination gap closed in counties at varying distances from Tuskegee, Alabama, and runs a placebo test using distance from Tulsa (site of the 1921 race massacre, but not a medical-trust-specific event) to check the effect is not just "historical trauma anywhere." It is trustworthy as a peer-reviewed quantitative study that builds in its own falsification check; note that journal access may be paywalled (an open preprint, GLO Discussion Paper 1397, may be available via glabor.org).

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with the structural mechanism, because it reframes something from Day 1 that was easy to read too individualistically. The 3C/5C model treats "confidence" as a property of a person. Konstantinou and colleagues' scoping review suggests that framing is incomplete: across the 11 studies they synthesize, people who are frequently exposed to positive vaccination attitudes among family and friends, and who discuss vaccination with them, hold more positive attitudes and vaccinate at higher rates themselves — and the reverse holds when the people around them are hesitant. This is "social contagion" in a precise sense: the direction of an attitude spreads through existing relationships, largely independent of the attitude's accuracy. The review's more pointed finding is that this spread is not random: it identifies homophily — the well-documented tendency of socially connected people to resemble each other — as a significant factor shaping *whose* networks transmit *which* attitudes, calling out race and ethnicity specifically as axes along which this clustering occurs. The practical consequence is that national vaccination coverage figures (recall Day 1's point that 92-93% national MMR coverage can mask much lower local clustering) are partly an artifact of this network structure: hesitancy does not diffuse evenly, it pools inside densely connected communities and drains out of others, which is one reason outbreaks concentrate geographically rather than appearing as background noise everywhere.

That framing explains *how* an attitude spreads once it exists, but not why a specific community might start from a different baseline of institutional trust in the first place — which is where the historical mechanism comes in, and where today's reading becomes more specific than "trust is socially constructed" in the abstract. Savoia and colleagues' systematic review of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy among Black and African American adults in the US makes a deliberate analytical move: it keeps "historical mistrust" and "present-day institutional mistrust" as two separate qualitative themes across the 14 qualitative studies it synthesizes, rather than merging them into one undifferentiated distrust category. That separation matters because it tracks a real distinction in mechanism: present-day institutional mistrust can be shaped or shifted by a clinic's current behavior (who delivers the message, how respectfully, with what transparency), while historical mistrust is anchored to events that already happened and cannot be undone by better communication alone — it can, at most, be acknowledged. Sixteen quantitative studies in the same review converge on confidence in vaccine safety/effectiveness and trust in the healthcare system as the measurable predictors, which is the same "confidence" antecedent from Day 1's 5C model — but the qualitative layer underneath it shows that antecedent is not a free-floating psychological trait; for some communities, it has a specific, nameable historical referent.

The Tuskegee Syphilis Study (1932-1972) is the referent most often named in this specific US context: the US Public Health Service withheld effective treatment from Black men with syphilis for decades under false pretenses, a government-run abuse that became public and widely known. The question the Hou, Jiao, Shen and Chen paper asks is sharper than "do people mention Tuskegee" — it asks whether the historical event left a *measurable, geographically localized, causal* footprint nearly a century later. Using county-level CDC vaccination data, they find that the Black-white COVID-19 vaccination gap closed more slowly in counties closer to Tuskegee, Alabama, than in counties farther away — and, crucially, they do not find the same pattern using distance from Tulsa, Oklahoma (site of the 1921 race massacre, a comparably severe historical atrocity against the same demographic group, but not a specifically medical betrayal). That placebo test is doing real analytical work: it is evidence that the effect is not simply "closer to any historical trauma, more distrust of any institution," but something more specific to medicine and public health as the institution whose trust was broken. This is the clearest evidence in this week's sources that institutional distrust, for some populations, is not a bias in need of correction — it is a documented, geographically traceable, and still-measurable consequence of a documented act, which puts it in a different category from the dread/unfamiliarity or omission-bias mechanisms discussed on Day 2.

Putting today's two mechanisms together against Day 1 and Day 2: individual psychology (dread, omission-type biases, motivated reasoning) operates on top of a social structure that is neither neutral nor uniform. Social contagion determines how quickly and how far an attitude — whatever its origin or accuracy — moves through a network, and homophily means that movement concentrates inside communities defined partly by race and ethnicity. Historical mistreatment, where it has occurred, supplies some communities with a starting baseline of institutional distrust that is not a cognitive error but a defensible inference from documented evidence. Neither mechanism requires assuming anyone in the chain is irrational, uninformed, or acting in bad faith — which sets up a genuinely hard question for Day 4: once hesitancy is this entangled with real historical grievance and ordinary social transmission, how does anyone — researcher, journalist, or public-health communicator — distinguish it from hesitancy driven by circulating misinformation, when the visible symptom (someone expressing distrust of a vaccine) can look identical either way?

## Questions to think about

1. If vaccination attitudes spread through networks largely independent of their accuracy (social contagion), what does that imply about public-health interventions that target individuals one at a time (like printed risk information) versus interventions that target networks (like peer-to-peer or community-messenger programs)?
2. Savoia and colleagues treat "historical mistrust" and "present-day institutional mistrust" as distinct themes. What would a health system need to do differently to address each one — and is it possible to address present-day mistrust credibly without first acknowledging the historical one?
3. The Tuskegee-distance study's placebo test (using Tulsa) is designed to rule out a confound. What other plausible confounds might produce a similar geographic pattern, and how convincingly does comparing two specific historical sites rule them out?
