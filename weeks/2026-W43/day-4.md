# Vaccine hesitancy and trust in science — Day 4/7: How misinformation spreads, and how to tell it apart from legitimate distrust

## Context

Day 3 closed on a genuinely hard question: once hesitancy is entangled with real historical grievance (Tuskegee) and ordinary social transmission (homophily in networks), how does anyone — researcher, journalist, or public-health communicator — distinguish hesitancy driven by circulating misinformation from hesitancy driven by a historically grounded, legitimate distrust, when the visible symptom (a person expressing distrust of a vaccine) can look identical either way? Today's readings take that question head-on, moving from the social-network and historical lens of Day 3 to the specific information ecosystem in which vaccine claims circulate: what kinds of false or misleading claims actually spread, how the social-media environment structures who sees what, and — most directly — whether misinformation belief and institutional distrust behave as the same phenomenon or as two separable things.

This matters because the two problems call for different responses. If hesitancy in a given population is mostly downstream of false factual claims (a fabricated link between a vaccine and a specific harm), the fix is at least in principle about information: better content moderation, pre-bunking, fact-checking. If it is mostly downstream of a reasoned distrust of the institutions delivering the message — a distrust that, as Day 3's Tuskegee-distance study showed, can be geographically traceable to a specific documented betrayal — then correcting "misinformation" does nothing, because there is no false claim to correct; the problem is the credibility of the messenger, not the accuracy of the message. Conflating the two risks a public-health response that treats a legitimately distrustful community as simply misinformed, which is itself a way of not listening.

Today's three sources approach this from three angles: first, a systematic look at what COVID-19 vaccine misinformation on social media actually contains and where it circulates; second, a large-scale structural analysis of how the online vaccine debate is organized into clusters, and what distinguishes the sources anti-vaccine users draw on; and third, a direct empirical test, in a nationally representative sample, of whether institutional trust and misinformation susceptibility are the same underlying factor or two independent ones. That last paper gives today's question its most concrete answer so far this week.

## Today's readings (3 items, ~21 min total)

**Misinformation About COVID-19 Vaccines on Social Media: Rapid Review** (Skafle, Nordahl-Hansen, Quintana, Wynn & Gabarron, *Journal of Medical Internet Research*, 2022) — https://doi.org/10.2196/37367 — ~8 min (abstract, themes, and discussion). A peer-reviewed, open-access, PROSPERO-registered (CRD42021277524) rapid review that screened 757 records and synthesized 45 studies on COVID-19 vaccine misinformation on social media. It is trustworthy because it follows a registered protocol and is explicit about its limits — most included studies were observational and from industrialized Western countries, and the authors call for more rigorous experimental designs rather than overstating causal claims.

**Characterizing polarization in online vaccine discourse — a large-scale study** (Mønsted & Lehmann, *PLOS ONE*, 2022) — https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0263746 — ~7 min (abstract, results on clustering and source types). A peer-reviewed, open-access, large-scale computational study that used deep learning to classify vaccine sentiment across a very large Twitter dataset (on the order of tens of billions of tweets, from which vaccine-related discourse was identified and classified) and mapped how users with similar vaccine attitudes cluster together online. It is a trustworthy primary source because its methods and classifier validation are reported in detail and its central claims (clustering by attitude; a link between strongly anti-vaccine accounts and commercial alternative-health sources) are specific and checkable, not vague generalizations about "echo chambers."

**Trust in institutions and misinformation susceptibility both independently explain vaccine skepticism** (Roozenbeek, van den Berg, Lambooij, van der Linden, Maertens, Ferreira, van Dijk & Roozenbeek, *Scientific Reports*, 2025) — https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-21452-1 — ~6 min (abstract, results on the independence of the two predictors, and the test of the "buffer" hypothesis). A peer-reviewed, open-access study using a preregistered, probability-based sample of 1,356 Dutch households with validated measures. It is today's most directly relevant source and is trustworthy because it is preregistered (reducing the risk of post hoc reframing of a null result as a finding) and because it explicitly tests, rather than assumes, whether trust and misinformation susceptibility are the same thing.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with what the misinformation actually looks like, because "misinformation" is often used as a single undifferentiated label in popular discussion. Skafle and colleagues' rapid review sorted the COVID-19 vaccine misinformation found across 45 studies into three recurring themes: medical misinformation (false claims about ingredients, side effects, or mechanisms — the modern descendants of the discredited MMR-autism claim that Day 1's Frontiers in Public Health source traced through the current US measles resurgence), claims about vaccine development (questioning the speed, safety testing, or motives behind how the vaccines were made), and conspiracy narratives (claims about hidden agendas by governments, pharmaceutical companies, or other institutions). This three-way split matters because it maps onto different plausible mechanisms from earlier in the week: a false claim about ingredients is a factual error that is, in principle, correctable by accurate information; a conspiracy narrative about hidden institutional motives looks much more like the motivated-reasoning and institutional-distrust mechanisms Day 2 and Day 3 described, where the issue is not a missing fact but a prior commitment that filters all facts. The review also flagged a geographic and platform skew worth remembering as a limitation: most included studies examined Twitter and Facebook, in industrialized Western countries, so what the literature currently knows about "how vaccine misinformation spreads" is itself unevenly sampled — a reminder that today's state-of-the-art readings describe the best-studied slice of the phenomenon, not necessarily the whole of it.

Mønsted and Lehmann's large-scale Twitter study adds the structural layer: given that these three types of claims exist, how does exposure to them actually get organized across a population of users? Their central finding echoes and sharpens Day 3's social-contagion result (that vaccination attitudes cluster along network lines, with homophily organizing who influences whom) but adds a mechanism specific to the misinformation angle: strongly anti-vaccine accounts in their dataset disproportionately shared content originating from commercial sources selling alternative-health products. This is a useful, concrete detail, because it complicates a simple "people are just scared and confused" story — some portion of the misinformation ecosystem has a financial interest behind it, a feature that a purely psychological account of hesitancy (dread, omission bias, motivated reasoning) would not capture on its own. It also reinforces why clustering, not just individual belief, matters operationally: if anti-vaccine users are structurally positioned to encounter more of this commercially motivated content and more of each other's content, the same social-contagion dynamics from Day 3 become a channel not just for peer attitudes but specifically for a particular type of claim with a particular type of source.

That sets up the question today's reading is really organized around: are "misinformation susceptibility" and "institutional distrust" the same underlying thing, measured two different ways, or genuinely separate? This is not a rhetorical question the field has settled by assumption — Roozenbeek and colleagues tested it directly, and their design is worth walking through because the result is more specific than "both matter." Using a preregistered, nationally representative sample, they measured general trust in institutions, trust specifically in government regarding vaccination, and susceptibility to misinformation, then tested how each related to vaccine skepticism. They found that trust specific to vaccination policy was a far stronger predictor than general institutional trust — a result that lines up with Day 1's point that hesitancy is not a fixed trait but context- and vaccine-specific, and with Day 3's point that "confidence" is not a generic personality variable but something anchored to specific institutions and specific histories. Separately, misinformation susceptibility was also associated with skepticism, across every way they specified the statistical model. The two did not collapse into each other.

The most pointed part of the design is what the paper calls the "buffer" hypothesis: the idea that people with high institutional trust are protected from the effects of believing misinformation, so that misinformation only matters for people who already distrust institutions. If that were true, it would suggest the two problems from this reading's opening paragraph are really one problem wearing two faces — fix distrust, and misinformation stops mattering. Roozenbeek and colleagues found no evidence for this buffering effect: misinformation susceptibility predicted skepticism similarly regardless of how much a person trusted institutions. That is the clearest answer this week has produced to the question Day 3 left open. Misinformation belief and institutional distrust are correlated — people who distrust institutions are somewhat more likely to find misinformation credible — but they are not reducible to one another, and they do not cancel or protect against each other. A person can be highly misinformation-susceptible while trusting institutions a great deal, or deeply distrustful of institutions while being quite resistant to specific false claims, exactly as the Tuskegee-distance research in Day 3 would predict for a population whose distrust is a documented, specific, historically grounded response rather than a general credulity. Practically, this means a single intervention aimed at "misinformation" will not reach a skepticism problem that is really about institutional trust, and vice versa — the two need to be diagnosed, and addressed, separately, which is exactly the groundwork Day 5's look at what interventions actually work will need.

## Questions to think about

1. Roozenbeek and colleagues found that trust specific to vaccination policy predicted skepticism far better than general institutional trust. If a health system wanted to measure "how much a community trusts us" in a way that is actually useful for predicting vaccination behavior, what would that measurement need to ask about instead of a generic trust survey?
2. Mønsted and Lehmann found a link between strongly anti-vaccine accounts and commercial alternative-health sources. Does the presence of a financial motive behind some misinformation change what counts as an appropriate response (e.g., regulation, platform moderation, disclosure requirements) compared to misinformation that circulates without any commercial interest?
3. If misinformation susceptibility and institutional distrust are genuinely independent — neither buffers nor fully explains the other — what would it look like to design a single public-health campaign that addresses both at once, rather than picking one?
