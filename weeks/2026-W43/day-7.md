# Vaccine hesitancy and trust in science — Day 7/7: Synthesis — what's settled, what's still open, and where to go next

## Context

Six days ago this week started with a definitional move: the WHO's 2015 SAGE working group insisted that "vaccine hesitancy" be treated as a spectrum of delay and refusal, not a single trait of informed versus misinformed people, and organized its causes into the 3Cs and a three-level Determinants Matrix (Day 1). Everything since has been an attempt to fill in that matrix with evidence: individual psychology and risk perception (Day 2), social networks and historical institutional betrayal (Day 3), the information ecosystem that misinformation and legitimate distrust both move through (Day 4), what interventions actually do to behavior (Day 5), and how any of this gets measured in the first place (Day 6). Today's job is not to introduce a new layer but to look back across all six and ask a harder question than any single day asked: which of this week's findings are robust — replicated across independent methods, by independent teams, often with a result the researchers did not expect going in — and which are still resting on a single study, a plausible-sounding theory, or evidence from one narrow context?

That distinction matters for a reason this week kept circling back to without ever naming it directly: a finding is more trustworthy not when it sounds intuitive, but when different methods converge on it despite looking for different things. The clearest version of this showed up on Day 2, where the intuitive, widely-cited "omission bias" explanation failed a direct experimental test (Jiménez, Mesoudi & Tehrani, 2020) even though the broader asymmetric pattern it was meant to explain held up perfectly well under a different method entirely — qualitative interviews about dread and unfamiliarity (Bond & Nolan, 2011). Today applies that same discipline across the whole week, and closes with two readings chosen specifically to stress-test how far this week's conclusions travel beyond where most of its evidence came from: an intervention synthesis covering a wider span of countries and years than Day 5's readings, and a review that leaves the COVID-19/US frame entirely and asks what vaccine hesitancy looks like in a part of the world — South Asia — that Day 6's own methodological audit flagged as under-studied.

The aim is not to produce a tidier theory than the field itself has. It is to leave this reading with an honest map: a short list of conclusions worth acting on, a short list of open questions worth remaining genuinely uncertain about, and pointers to follow if any of this week's seven days deserves a deeper look of your own.

## Today's readings (2 items, ~14 min total)

**Interventions addressing vaccine hesitancy in the WHO European Region and in North America** (Pennisi, Dinardo, Bardanzellu, Colzani, Ferro, Lopalco, Unim, Vicentini, Fortunato, Signorelli & Odone, *Public Health Reviews*, 2026) — https://doi.org/10.3389/phrs.2026.1609375 — ~8 min (abstract, methods, and the summary of effective intervention categories). A peer-reviewed, open-access systematic review (PROSPERO-registered, CRD42024565588) following Cochrane Handbook methodology and PRISMA 2020 reporting, searching PubMed, Scopus, PsycInfo, Cochrane Library and Embase through January 2024 and synthesizing 59 studies. It is a trustworthy capstone for the week's intervention question because it is more recent and geographically broader (WHO European Region plus the US and Canada) than any single intervention study read earlier in the week, and because it reports which categories of intervention — not just which single trials — showed an effect.

**Factors influencing vaccine hesitancy toward non-COVID vaccines in South Asia: a systematic review** (Stuetzle et al., *BMC Public Health*, 2025) — https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-025-22462-4 — ~6 min (abstract, and the mapping of findings onto the 5C model). A peer-reviewed, open-access systematic review synthesizing 44 quantitative, qualitative and mixed-methods studies on hesitancy toward routine, non-COVID vaccines across India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal and Afghanistan, with findings explicitly mapped onto the 5C framework from Day 1. It is today's most important corrective: nearly every other source cited this week was conducted in a high-income Western country or specifically about COVID-19, which is exactly the generalizability gap Day 6's measurement audit (Oduwole et al.) flagged as a limit on how far this week's conclusions can be trusted to travel.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with what this week leaves genuinely settled — findings that held up when different teams, in different countries, using different methods, went looking for them. First: hesitancy is not a fixed trait or a single behavior, it is a context- and vaccine-specific spectrum, and this framing has now been operationalized three separate ways without being overturned — as the WHO's 3Cs (2015), as Betsch and colleagues' measurable 5C antecedents (2018), and as the WHO's program-oriented BeSD toolkit (2022, Day 6), each building on the last rather than replacing it. Second: social transmission and historical institutional betrayal are real, separable mechanisms, not the same thing wearing different names. Konstantinou and colleagues' scoping review found vaccination attitudes spread through networks largely independent of their accuracy, with homophily organizing who influences whom (Day 3); entirely separately, Hou, Jiao, Shen and Chen's geographic natural experiment found a measurable, specifically medical (not general-trauma) footprint of the Tuskegee Syphilis Study on COVID-19 vaccination gaps nearly a century later, with a placebo test (distance from Tulsa) built in to rule out the obvious confound. Third, and perhaps this week's sharpest result: Roozenbeek and colleagues' preregistered Dutch study (Day 4) found that institutional distrust and misinformation susceptibility independently predict vaccine skepticism, with neither buffering the other — a direct empirical answer, not an assumption, to the question Day 3 left open about how to tell historically-grounded distrust apart from misinformation-driven belief.

Now the part that is less tidy than any single day made it look in isolation: several of this week's most quotable claims did not survive contact with a second, more rigorous look. The "omission bias" story — that people weigh self-caused vaccine harms worse than equivalent disease harms — is the clearest case: a direct experimental test found no such effect (Day 2), even though the related dread/unfamiliarity pattern held up under a different method. The 2017 "backfire effect" finding that correcting vaccine myths can entrench them did not replicate either, across two independent follow-up timepoints (Ecker, Sharkey & Swire-Thompson, Day 5) — but this is where the honest picture gets genuinely two-sided rather than simply "the fear was wrong." Day 5's own second reading on policy tools found that mandates and passports move aggregate uptake without the feared net backfire, while a separate strand of research on "vaccination policy reactance" found real, if smaller, spillover costs — lower stated intention to get an unrelated, still-voluntary vaccine among people who felt mandated. Backfire effects, in other words, were overstated specifically in the belief-correction literature but are not fully closed as a question at the policy level. Layered onto that: Labbé and colleagues' 2025 meta-analysis found motivational interviewing showed no measurable advantage over standard education in pooled vaccination-rate outcomes — but attached its own caveat that the MI evidence base is thin and rarely checked whether MI was actually delivered as intended, which is a null result built on weak evidence, not strong evidence of no effect.

Today's first new reading, Pennisi and colleagues' 2026 systematic review, updates that intervention picture with more recent, broader evidence than any single study read on Day 5: synthesizing 59 studies across the WHO European Region, the US and Canada, it reports that the approaches with the most consistent evidence of effectiveness are not single-lever interventions at all, but multicomponent strategies — community engagement, reminder-and-recall systems, educational campaigns and legislative measures used together rather than any one alone. That is a meaningfully different conclusion than reading Day 5 alone would suggest, where each lever (debunking, MI, mandates) was evaluated mostly on its own and found to be narrower or more conditional than hoped. It does not contradict Day 5's finding that no single tool reliably reaches the strongly hesitant core; it reframes it — the field's best current answer to "what works" is combination, not selection, and even that answer comes with the same caveat as everything intervention-related this week: it mostly describes European and North American health systems.

That caveat is exactly what today's second reading tests directly. Stuetzle and colleagues' review of 44 studies on non-COVID vaccine hesitancy across five South Asian countries maps its findings onto the same 5C structure Day 1 introduced — confidence, constraints and the rest still organize the evidence there — but the texture underneath differs in emphasis from what a week built mostly on US and European, mostly COVID-era, sources would lead you to expect. The drivers the review foregrounds — narrow vaccine-specific confidence, miscommunication between providers and communities, and gaps in practical knowledge, set against structural constraints — are recognizable within the 5C vocabulary, but the review's broader point is that hesitancy in this context is sharply context- and vaccine-specific, in line with Day 1's founding definition, and it explicitly recommends community-co-created interventions rather than imported, top-down campaigns. Put next to Day 6's finding that only one of 26 measurement studies in a decade-long audit came from the WHO African Region, this week's geography has a consistent shape: the frameworks (3C/5C/BeSD) appear durable wherever they have been tested, but the specific findings about what drives hesitancy, and what reliably changes it, have mostly been tested in a narrow slice of the world, and generalizing past that slice is an assumption this week's own sources warn against making casually.

Five questions remain genuinely open after all seven days, not because no one has looked, but because the evidence that exists does not yet resolve them. First, what — if anything — reliably moves the strongly hesitant core, given that debunking, motivational interviewing and structural mandates each move the population average while consistently failing to reach this specific group (Days 2, 5)? Second, how far do this week's findings about mechanisms and interventions actually generalize beyond the WHO-Europe/North America/COVID-era settings most of the underlying studies come from, now that both Day 6's measurement audit and today's South Asia review flag this as an open empirical question rather than a safe assumption? Third, can institutional distrust and misinformation susceptibility — shown to be statistically independent (Day 4) — be addressed by a single, practical intervention, or does their independence mean they require genuinely separate programs? Fourth, does the "backfire effect" really differ between information-level correction (where it appears not to replicate) and policy-level mandates (where a real, if smaller, reactance cost has been found elsewhere), or is this an artifact of how few studies have tested the comparison directly? Fifth, now that BeSD gives programs a tool that separates "won't" from "can't," how much of what gets labeled hesitancy in the historical record Day 6 reviewed was actually an access problem mislabeled as an attitude problem?

## Questions to think about

1. Of this week's three main lenses — individual psychology (Days 1-2), social and historical structure (Day 3), and information ecosystem (Day 4) — which do you think a resource-constrained public-health system should prioritize first, given that Pennisi and colleagues' 2026 review found multicomponent strategies, not any single lever, carry the most consistent evidence?
2. Today's second reading found the 5C framework still organized the evidence in South Asia, but with a different emphasis among its components than the US/European sources read earlier this week. Does a framework "working" in a new context but surfacing different drivers mean the framework is validated, or that it is flexible enough to fit almost any finding — and how would you tell the difference?
3. This week repeatedly found that a popular, intuitive explanation (omission bias, the backfire effect) failed a more rigorous test, while a less intuitive one (dread/unfamiliarity, independent-not-buffering distrust and misinformation) survived. What would you want to see, methodologically, before trusting a new claim in this field that you have not seen independently replicated?
