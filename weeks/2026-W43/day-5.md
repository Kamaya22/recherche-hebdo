# Vaccine hesitancy and trust in science — Day 5/7: What actually changes minds — debunking, motivational interviewing, and policy tools

## Context

Day 4 left this week with a sharp, specific result: institutional distrust and susceptibility to misinformation are independently associated with vaccine skepticism, and neither buffers the other. That is a diagnosis, not a treatment plan. If a community's hesitancy is driven mostly by a false factual claim, and another community's is driven mostly by a historically grounded distrust of the institutions delivering the message, then whatever "works" for one might do nothing for the other — and a single all-purpose intervention is unlikely to move both. Today asks the question the whole week has been building toward: across the different mechanisms this week has identified (Day 2's risk-perception biases, Day 3's networks and historical distrust, Day 4's misinformation ecosystem), what does the evidence actually say about changing minds, or at least changing behavior?

Three distinct intervention types compete for attention in this space, and they operate at different levels. The first is direct correction — showing someone a myth and a fact side by side, an approach shadowed for nearly a decade by fear of the "backfire effect": the idea that correcting a myth can paradoxically entrench it. The second is a difference in conversational style rather than content — motivational interviewing (MI), a clinical technique built to respect a patient's autonomy and work with their own ambivalence rather than correct them, which matters directly given Day 3's point that some distrust is a reasoned response to real history, not a factual error to be fixed. The third bypasses persuasion altogether: mandates, vaccination passports, and financial incentives change what it costs to remain unvaccinated, rather than trying to change what anyone believes.

These three levels also let today's reading test, from a different angle, the backfire question Day 4 only partly settled. If correcting a specific myth can backfire, and if a mandate can provoke the kind of reactance that makes people dig in, then "interventions can make hesitancy worse" would be a usefully general caution across every level of this week's topic. If instead the empirical record mostly fails to support that fear — while still showing real limits on how much any of these tools accomplish — that is itself an important, under-appreciated finding, and precisely the kind of claim this week's sources rules say must be checked against the evidence rather than assumed.

## Today's readings (3 items, ~21 min total)

**Correcting Vaccine Misinformation: A Failure to Replicate Familiarity or Fear-Driven Backfire Effects** (Ecker, Sharkey & Swire-Thompson, *PLOS ONE*, 2023) — https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0281140 — ~7 min (abstract, method summary, results). A peer-reviewed, open-access, pre-registered-style replication (with a 7-day delayed follow-up) of an influential 2017 study that had reported vaccine-myth corrections backfiring. It is trustworthy because it directly reproduces the original four conditions plus an added condition, reports results relative to both a control group and a pre-intervention baseline, and is explicit that a null backfire result does not mean every correction format works equally well.

**Addressing vaccine hesitancy: A systematic review comparing the efficacy of motivational versus educational interventions on vaccination uptake** (Labbé, Lavoie et al., *Translational Behavioral Medicine*, 2025) — https://doi.org/10.1093/tbm/ibae069 — ~8 min (abstract, pooled results, discussion of study quality). A peer-reviewed systematic review and meta-analysis pooling dozens of randomized trials (55 RCTs of educational interventions alone) to compare motivational-interviewing/motivational-communication (MI/MC) approaches against standard educational approaches and against usual care. It is a trustworthy source because it reports pooled effect sizes with confidence intervals rather than a narrative impression, and because it explicitly flags the low methodological quality of the MI/MC evidence base rather than overselling a null result as a strong finding.

**Do COVID-19 Vaccination Policies Backfire? The Effects of Mandates, Vaccination Passports, and Financial Incentives on COVID-19 Vaccination** (Fayaz-Farkhad & Jung, *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, 2024) — https://doi.org/10.1177/17456916231178708 — ~6 min (abstract, summary of policy comparisons, limitations). A peer-reviewed review article in a journal of the Association for Psychological Science, synthesizing the empirical literature on three structural policy tools. It is trustworthy because it addresses the "backfire" fear directly and explicitly distinguishes population-level effects from effects on the specific subgroup of strongly hesitant people, rather than treating "works" as a single yes/no verdict.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start where the backfire-effect debate began. In 2017, Pluviano, Watt and Della Sala (also *PLOS ONE*) tested three pro-vaccination strategies — a myths-versus-facts correction, a fact-and-icon infographic, and images of sick unvaccinated children — and reported that the correction strategies largely failed, and that some actually strengthened false beliefs about vaccine side effects and the (fully discredited) vaccine-autism link relative to control. That finding mattered well beyond one study: if telling people the facts can backfire, careful fact-based public-health communication would be not just ineffective but actively counterproductive, and the three-way misinformation taxonomy from Day 4 (medical claims, development claims, conspiracy claims) would need a very different response than "correct the false claim." Ecker, Sharkey and Swire-Thompson's 2023 replication is the reading that actually tests whether that fear holds up. Reproducing the original four conditions and adding a fifth, and measuring beliefs both immediately and after a 7-day delay, they found no backfire at all: none of the conditions increased misconceptions relative to control, at either timepoint, or relative to a pre-intervention baseline. The myths-versus-facts format was in fact the only one of the four that successfully reduced belief in vaccine side effects and the autism link. The infographic and the fear-based image did not help — but they did not hurt either. The practical upshot is specific rather than sweeping: the original fear that factual correction backfires does not replicate, but that does not mean every correction format is equally useful, and the authors are careful to say that formats that showed no benefit here should not be discarded on this evidence alone.

That result answers a question about content — what you say — but Day 3's point about reasoned, historically grounded distrust raises a question about manner — how you say it. Motivational interviewing is built for exactly that gap: instead of presenting a myth-versus-fact correction, a clinician using MI asks open questions, reflects a patient's own stated concerns back to them, and avoids direct confrontation, on the theory that ambivalence yields to a patient's own reasoning more readily than to being told they are wrong. It is a plausible fit for someone whose skepticism traces to something like the Tuskegee-distance effect from Day 3, where no false claim needs correcting because the underlying concern is not factually mistaken. Labbé and colleagues' 2025 meta-analysis is the most direct test of whether that theoretical advantage shows up in actual vaccination rates. Pooling across trials, they found a vaccination rate of 52% (95% CI 0.48–0.56) for educational interventions versus 45% (95% CI 0.29–0.62) for MI/MC interventions — not a significant difference (P = .417) — and, in the comparison that matters most for a real-world decision, MI/MC interventions were not significantly better than usual or standard care (relative risk 1.07, 95% CI 0.78–1.45, P = .691). Critically, the authors attach a methodological caveat to this null result rather than treating it as definitive: the MI/MC evidence base is thin (far fewer trials than for educational interventions) and of generally poor quality, including a frequent lack of fidelity assessment — meaning many "MI" trials never verified that the clinicians actually delivered MI as intended. A null result built on a handful of underpowered, unverified trials is evidence of absence of proof, not proof of absence of effect.

The third reading moves to a level where persuasion is not the mechanism at all. Mandates, vaccination passports, and financial incentives do not try to change what anyone believes about vaccines; they change the cost of remaining unvaccinated — losing access to a workplace, a venue, or a cash payment. The feared failure mode here is not "backfire" in the belief-correction sense but reactance: the psychological pushback against being told what to do, which could in principle reduce voluntary uptake net of any coercive gain. Fayaz-Farkhad and Jung's review found the evidence does not support that fear in aggregate — mandates and vaccination passports showed large positive effects on vaccination at the population level, while financial incentives produced effects that were more variable and typically shorter-lived. But their review also names the same limitation that keeps resurfacing this week from different directions: none of these tools reliably move the strongly hesitant core, the group for whom Day 1's point that hesitancy sits on a spectrum, not a binary, matters most. That caveat is not just a footnote — a companion literature on "vaccination policy reactance" (Sprengholz, Felgendreff, Böhm & Betsch, *Journal of Health Psychology*, 2021/2022) found that mandates can produce real reactance costs elsewhere even while raising uptake of the mandated vaccine itself: in their cross-sectional surveys and preregistered experiments, perceived mandate pressure was associated with lower stated intention to get an unrelated, still-voluntary vaccine (influenza) and lower willingness to adhere to other protective measures. The two reviews are not strictly contradictory — a policy can raise uptake of the specific vaccine it targets while still provoking a measurable, if smaller, backlash elsewhere — but together they show that "do mandates backfire?" is still a live, two-sided empirical question rather than one either paper has fully closed.

Put the three readings side by side and a pattern emerges that cuts across all of this week's mechanisms. At every level tested here — correcting a specific false belief, changing the manner of a one-on-one conversation, and changing the structural cost of non-vaccination — the dramatic failure mode (backfire, net reactance) is less supported by the best current evidence than the popular fear of it would suggest. But at every level, the positive effect is also narrower and more conditional than a simple "intervention X works" headline implies: debunking helps only in the one format actually tested with real success (myths-versus-facts), motivational interviewing shows no measurable advantage over standard education in the pooled trial evidence currently available, and structural policy tools move aggregate numbers while consistently failing to reach the strongly hesitant minority this entire week has been circling back to. None of today's three readings, individually or together, closes that gap — which is itself the most honest finding today's reading can report.

## Questions to think about

1. Mandates and passports show large positive population effects but, per Fayaz-Farkhad and Jung, do not move the strongly hesitant; MI shows no measurable advantage over standard education in Labbé and colleagues' pooled trials. If you had to recommend where a public-health system with limited resources should invest — debunking training, MI training, or policy — what would you want to know that today's readings don't tell you?
2. Ecker and colleagues found that myths-versus-facts was the only correction format in their test that worked, out of four. Given Day 4's finding that misinformation susceptibility and institutional distrust are independent predictors of skepticism, would you expect a myths-versus-facts correction to help someone whose distrust is rooted in documented historical mistreatment (Day 3) rather than belief in a specific false claim — and if not, what would?
3. Labbé and colleagues flagged that many MI/MC trials never checked whether clinicians actually delivered MI as intended (fidelity assessment). What would a rigorous trial need to include to really test whether motivational interviewing works, rather than merely failing to find a difference because the "MI" being tested wasn't reliably MI?
