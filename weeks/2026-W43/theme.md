# Vaccine hesitancy and trust in science

The psychology and sociology of why people delay or refuse effective vaccines, even when they are free, available, and backed by overwhelming evidence.

## Why it matters

Vaccines are one of the most effective interventions in the history of public health, yet uptake of even long-established, well-studied vaccines (measles, polio, pertussis) has been falling in many countries, and 2025 saw the largest US measles outbreak in more than three decades. This is not primarily a story about access: in most of the cases driving current concern, vaccination services are available, but people delay, decline, or vacillate. The WHO's Strategic Advisory Group of Experts on Immunization formally named this behavior "vaccine hesitancy" in 2014 precisely because it did not fit older models that treated non-vaccination as either ignorance or flat refusal — hesitancy is a spectrum, it is context-specific, and the same person can be hesitant about one vaccine and not another. Understanding it matters for a very concrete reason: herd immunity thresholds are not just numbers in a textbook, they are thresholds that, once crossed in the wrong direction, bring back diseases that had been pushed to near-elimination. The topic sits at the intersection of individual psychology (risk perception, trust, cognitive biases), social structure (networks, communities, historical relationships with medicine and the state), and information ecosystems (how claims about vaccines spread and compete for belief) — which is why this week treats it as a psychology-and-sociology topic first, rather than an immunology one.

## Tentative 7-day outline

1. **Day 1 — Panorama**: what vaccine hesitancy is and isn't, the WHO's 3Cs/5C determinants frameworks, and why it is a live public-health concern again in 2025-2026.
2. **Day 2 — Mechanisms**: individual-level psychology — risk perception biases, omission bias, motivated reasoning, and how people weigh a small known risk (the vaccine) against a larger but less vivid one (the disease).
3. **Day 3 — Key findings**: the social and historical determinants — trust in institutions, social networks and norms, and how specific communities' documented mistreatment by medicine and the state shapes present-day hesitancy.
4. **Day 4 — State of the art**: information ecosystems — how misinformation and contested claims about vaccines spread, and what distinguishes hesitancy driven by misinformation from hesitancy driven by legitimate distrust.
5. **Day 5 — Controversies and open debates**: what actually changes minds — the evidence (and limits of the evidence) on debunking, motivational interviewing, mandates, and incentives, including the "backfire effect" debate.
6. **Day 6 — Methods**: how researchers measure an attitude/behavior that people may not report honestly — survey scales like the 5C model, behavioral/administrative data, and natural experiments.
7. **Day 7 — Synthesis**: what is settled vs. still open, the biggest unresolved questions, and pointers for going deeper.

The outline is a guide, not a contract, and may shift as the week's research turns up more specific angles.
