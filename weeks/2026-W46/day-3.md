# Bird migration navigation — Day 3/7: Celestial cues and how compass systems calibrate one another

## Context

Day 1 introduced three compass systems — magnetic, sun, star — and flagged that they are not independent backups but a partially integrated toolkit, calibrated against one another during a sensitive early-life period. Day 2 went deep on just one of those systems, the magnetic sense, and found that even there the field has had to revise itself: a light-dependent, quantum-chemical mechanism in the eye (cryptochrome) sits alongside a newly proposed iron-based mechanism in the liver that only seems to matter when the sky is overcast. That overcast-dependence is the thread this reading picks up: if different systems matter under different conditions, birds must have some way of reconciling them, especially during the weeks when a young bird is building its compass system for the first time with no one to correct its mistakes.

Today looks at the two celestial systems in more detail — the sun compass and its reliance on an internal clock, and the star compass and how long it actually takes a young bird to learn it — and then at the central unresolved question of *calibration*: when a bird's magnetic and celestial cues disagree, which one does it trust, and which one is actually "teaching" the other during development? This is not a settled hierarchy. Decades of cue-conflict experiments, in which researchers deliberately shift the magnetic field relative to the real sky (or vice versa) and watch which direction a bird chooses, have produced genuinely mixed results: some studies conclude the magnetic field is the fixed reference that recalibrates celestial cues, others conclude the opposite, and the explanation increasingly looks like it depends on species, developmental stage, and exactly which celestial cue (the sun's position, the stars' rotation, or the polarized-light pattern at sunset) is on offer.

Why this matters beyond basic biology: if young birds need weeks of exposure to a natural night sky or clear horizon to calibrate their systems properly, that has direct implications for conservation practices that remove birds from natural light cues during exactly that developmental window — hand-rearing, captive breeding, and translocation programs for endangered migratory species, a concern Day 7 will return to alongside light pollution more broadly.

## Today's readings (~21 min)

**"Feasibility of sun and magnetic compass mechanisms in avian long-distance migration"** (Muheim, Schmaljohann & Alerstam, *Movement Ecology*, 2018) — DOI: 10.1186/s40462-018-0126-4 — ~7 min (abstract, introduction, discussion). A peer-reviewed, fully open-access review (BioMed Central / Springer Nature) that compares how far a migratory bird could get using only a sun compass or only a magnetic compass, held to a single fixed heading for the whole journey, versus how often real populations likely need to correct course or draw on additional "map" information. It is the most direct entry point into how the sun compass actually functions as a practical long-distance navigation tool, not just a laboratory phenomenon.

**"Star compass learning: how long does it take?"** (Michalik, Alert, Engels, Lefeldt & Mouritsen, *Journal of Ornithology*, 2014) — DOI: 10.1007/s10336-013-1004-x, vol. 155: 225–234 — ~7 min. A peer-reviewed primary study (Deutsche Ornithologen-Gesellschaft / Springer) that raised hand-reared European robins under an artificial, rotating star pattern for three weeks, one week, or a single night, then tested their orientation. It is the most direct test of how much early-life exposure a young bird actually needs before it can use the sky's rotation as a compass — a question Day 1 raised but did not quantify.

**"Calibration of magnetic and celestial compass cues in migratory birds: a review of cue-conflict experiments"** (Muheim, Moore & Phillips, *Journal of Experimental Biology*, 2006) — DOI: 10.1242/jeb.01960, vol. 209(1): 2–17 — ~7 min (abstract, introduction, discussion). A peer-reviewed review (The Company of Biologists) synthesizing decades of experiments in which researchers artificially shifted the magnetic field relative to the real sky and recorded which cue birds followed. It lays out the central, still-unresolved debate head-on: some results suggest the magnetic field is the fixed reference against which celestial cues get calibrated, others suggest the reverse, and the review argues that the polarized-light pattern visible near the horizon at sunrise and sunset may be a shared reference that several systems calibrate against.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with the sun compass, because it is mechanically the most intuitive of the three systems and the one Day 1 left least explored. A sun compass only works if a bird can correct for the sun's steady drift across the sky — roughly 15 degrees of azimuth per hour — and that correction requires an internal clock, which is why clock-shift experiments (keeping a bird on an artificial light-dark schedule offset from local time, then releasing it) are the classic way to prove a sun compass is in use: a six-hour clock shift reliably produces a roughly 90-degree orientation error, exactly what you would expect if the bird is reading the sun's position correctly but applying the wrong time-correction to it. Muheim, Schmaljohann and Alerstam's review asks a more practical question than "does the sun compass work" — it asks how *far* a bird could actually get using nothing but a sun compass (or nothing but a magnetic compass) held to one fixed heading for an entire migration. Their answer is nuanced: a single, uncorrected compass course is a plausible strategy for some populations and some route geometries, but for many real migrations the bird likely has to reorient its heading once or several times along the way, or supplement a compass with genuine "map" information about its position — a reminder that "compass" and "successful long-distance navigation" are not the same problem, and that the practical feasibility of any one cue depends heavily on the specific route a species flies.

The star compass raises a different kind of question: not whether it works (Emlen settled that in the 1960s, as Day 1 described), but how much early-life exposure to the night sky a young bird actually needs before it can use it reliably. Michalik and colleagues addressed this directly and quantitatively, using hand-reared European robins split into groups given a rotating artificial star pattern for three weeks, one week, or a single night during development. Birds given only a week or a single night of exposure went on to show inappropriate or essentially random orientation, while the birds given three weeks of exposure did not — a result that pushes the minimum learning period for a functional star compass meaningfully longer than earlier work had assumed, and underscores a point Day 1 only gestured at: this is a genuinely developmental process with a real time cost, not something a bird's brain simply has pre-wired and ready to use. That time cost is precisely why hand-rearing or short-term captivity during a chick's first weeks of life is not a navigationally neutral intervention.

The calibration question is the hardest of the three, and the one Muheim, Moore and Phillips's review confronts most directly: when a young bird is building its compass system, which cue teaches which? Cue-conflict experiments — artificially rotating the magnetic field relative to the true sky and watching what a bird does afterward, with no external conflict present — have produced results on both sides. In some experiments, birds that experienced a shifted magnetic field during a sensitive developmental period went on to orient according to the *shifted* magnetic direction even once the conflict was removed, as if the celestial sky had calibrated their magnetic compass to a now-wrong setting; in others, the pattern runs the other way, with the magnetic field apparently acting as the stable reference that recalibrates celestial cues instead. Rather than treating this as a simple contradiction to be resolved in favor of one side, the review's more interesting proposal is that the two sets of results may reflect different components of the sky doing different jobs: the pattern of polarized light that forms a band across the sky near the horizon at sunrise and sunset is visible to birds, is tightly tied to true geographic direction (unlike the stars' rotation axis, which a bird still has to discover), and may function as a shared calibration reference that several systems — magnetic, star, and sun — each check themselves against at different points in development or migration, rather than there being one single master compass that all others simply obey.

Put together, this reframes the "three independent compasses" picture from Day 1 into something closer to a layered, partially hierarchical system that is still being actively calibrated throughout a bird's early life and possibly during migration itself: a sun compass whose practical range is limited by how often a given route actually tolerates a single fixed heading; a star compass that takes multiple weeks of real sky-watching to establish; and a calibration relationship between celestial and magnetic cues that most plausibly runs through a shared reference point (polarized light at twilight) rather than one system simply dominating the other. This also sharpens the question Day 2 left open about the overcast-dependent liver-macrophage pathway: if polarized-light calibration requires at least some visible structure in the sky near the horizon, a bird navigating under heavy, uniform cloud cover may be relying on exactly the magnetic systems Day 2 described specifically because its normal calibration reference is unavailable — consistent with cue systems that substitute for each other by necessity, not by preference.

## Questions to think about

- If a young bird needs roughly three weeks of exposure to a natural, rotating night sky to build a working star compass, what do clock-shift and cue-conflict experiments that keep birds in captivity for shorter periods risk missing or misinterpreting?
- The calibration review raises the possibility that polarized light at sunrise/sunset, not the magnetic field or the stars themselves, is the shared reference several systems calibrate against. What kind of experiment could distinguish "polarized light calibrates everything else" from "the magnetic field calibrates everything else," given that both are usually present together in the wild?
- Given that a single fixed sun or magnetic compass heading is only a feasible strategy for some migratory routes, what would you expect a bird relying on a "map and compass" strategy to actually need to sense, beyond the three compass systems covered so far — and does anything in this week's readings so far hint at what that might be?
