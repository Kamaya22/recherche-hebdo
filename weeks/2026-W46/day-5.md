# Bird migration navigation — Day 5/7: Controversies and open debates — which cues actually dominate, and what counts as a "map"?

## Context

Days 1 through 4 built up the pieces of avian navigation one at a time: three compass systems (Day 1), the disputed physical mechanism behind the magnetic sense (Day 2), how the sun and star compasses work and calibrate against the magnetic sense (Day 3), and the tracking technology that lets researchers check cage-based conclusions against real, free-flying migration (Day 4). Day 3 already flagged that the calibration question — when celestial and magnetic cues disagree, which one wins, and which one is "teaching" the other — is genuinely unresolved, with cue-conflict experiments producing results on both sides of the question since at least Muheim, Moore and Phillips's 2006 review.

Today confronts that lack of resolution head-on, because it has not gone away in the two decades since: newer reviews and experiments still disagree about which compass system dominates during real migratory flight, and about why so many magnetic-cue and cue-conflict experiments fail to replicate cleanly across labs, species, and years. This is not a minor technical squabble — it cuts to whether "the" avian compass hierarchy even exists as a single, general answer, or whether the right conclusion is that different species, and possibly different individuals, use genuinely different strategies. Today also introduces a second, deeper controversy that the first four days have been building toward without naming directly: the difference between a "compass" (which only gives direction) and a "map" (which requires some sense of current position relative to a goal), and how much of the basic navigational problem a compass can even solve.

Why this matters beyond resolving an academic dispute: Day 4 showed that modern tracking technology exists precisely to test whether lab-derived compass hierarchies hold up in free-flying birds, and the controversies covered today are the live record of that test not yet converging on a single answer. Getting the hierarchy and the map question right matters for the conservation stakes Day 7 will cover, because different hypotheses about which cue dominates, and whether a magnetic or other map underlies long-distance navigation, imply different vulnerabilities to human-caused disruption of migratory cues.

## Today's readings (~20 min)

**"A hierarchy of compass systems in migratory birds"** (Pakhomov & Chernetsov, *Biological Communications*, 2020) — https://doi.org/10.21638/spbu03.2020.306 — vol. 65(3): 262–276 — ~7 min (abstract, introduction, discussion). A peer-reviewed, open-access review published by St. Petersburg University Press that systematically works through the cue-conflict literature on nocturnal songbird migrants and asks the central question directly: does one compass system dominate the others, or does some single cue calibrate all the rest? The authors conclude the honest answer is "it depends on the species" — astronomical cues reliably calibrate the magnetic compass before a bird's first migration, but strategies during migration itself are far more varied, and they cite telemetry evidence (a 2011 study radio-tracking free-flying European song thrushes) that different songbird species appear to have evolved different hierarchies rather than sharing one universal rule.

**"A New View on an Old Debate: Type of Cue-Conflict Manipulation and Availability of Stars Can Explain the Discrepancies between Cue-Calibration Experiments with Migratory Songbirds"** (Sjöberg & Muheim, *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*, 2016) — https://doi.org/10.3389/fnbeh.2016.00029 — vol. 10, article 29 — ~7 min (abstract, discussion). A peer-reviewed, fully open-access paper (Frontiers Media, freely available via PubMed Central) that tackles the reproducibility problem methodologically rather than biologically: comparing published cue-conflict studies, the authors argue that whether a study used funnel cages that blocked a bird's view of the horizon, versus an open setup with a full sunset sky visible, can explain much of the disagreement over whether the magnetic or the celestial system acts as the calibration reference. Notably, the authors' own new experiment — testing garden warblers given a full view of polarized light near the horizon at sunset — still failed to show the recalibration their hypothesis predicted, which the paper itself treats as an open problem rather than papering over it.

**"Long-distance navigation and magnetoreception in migratory animals"** (Mouritsen, *Nature*, 2018) — https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-018-0176-1 — vol. 558(7708): 50–59 — ~7 min (abstract, figures, and the concluding open-questions section). A peer-reviewed invited review in Nature by one of the field's most prominent researchers (University of Oldenburg), which reframes the whole topic by separating the "compass" problem (which direction is correct) from the "map" problem (where am I relative to my goal) and lays out, at the end, twenty specific unresolved mechanistic questions intended to guide the field for the following two decades — several of which are the same open questions this week's readings keep circling back to.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Pick up where Day 3 left off: the calibration question — does the magnetic field recalibrate celestial cues, or do celestial cues recalibrate the magnetic field — has not been settled by more data; if anything, more data has sharpened how genuinely split the field is. Pakhomov and Chernetsov's 2020 review is useful precisely because it resists the temptation to declare a winner. Working through the cue-conflict literature on nocturnal songbird migrants, they separate two different questions that earlier work often ran together: what calibrates what *before* a bird's first migration (astronomical cues reliably recalibrating the magnetic compass during this early developmental period, consistent with Day 3's account of star-compass learning) versus what happens *during* migration itself, when the evidence is far messier. Their most interesting move is to treat that messiness as a finding rather than noise: citing a 2011 radio-telemetry study of free-flying European song thrushes, which found that these birds did not recalibrate their magnetic compass from solar/twilight cues the way related North American thrush species had been shown to do, but simply let one compass (the magnetic one) dominate instead, they argue that different songbird species — and possibly different populations — may have evolved genuinely different hierarchies suited to their own migratory routes and ecology, rather than there being one "correct" hierarchy that contradictory lab results are failing to reveal.

Sjöberg and Muheim's 2016 paper takes a different, more methodological angle on the same reproducibility problem, and it is worth reading precisely because it is candid about its own hypothesis's limits. Comparing published cue-conflict experiments, the authors notice a pattern in how studies were built rather than in the birds themselves: experiments that used funnel-shaped orientation cages, which restrict a bird's view to a narrow patch of sky, tended to find the magnetic compass dominant, while experiments that let birds see the full sunset horizon tended to find celestial cues recalibrating the magnetic sense instead. That is a genuinely useful, falsifiable explanation for why labs using different apparatus kept reaching opposite conclusions — but the paper does not simply confirm it and move on. The authors ran their own new experiment on garden warblers, giving them a full, unobstructed view of the polarized-light pattern near the horizon at sunset — exactly the condition their hypothesis predicted should produce recalibration — and the birds still showed no recalibration. Rather than quietly dropping this inconvenient result, the paper reports it directly and proposes an extended version of its theory (that birds may need to see the sky near the horizon at *both* sunset and sunrise, not just one or the other, to fully recalibrate). That a paper proposing a tidy methodological resolution to a decades-old contradiction immediately produced its own partial counterexample is a good illustration of how hard-won and provisional progress in this field actually is.

Both of today's first two readings, in other words, converge on the same honest conclusion Day 1 already anticipated: "one compass dominates" is probably the wrong frame. What is still disputed is not whether cue systems interact — everyone agrees they do — but whether the right explanation for decades of contradictory results is biological (genuine species- and population-level differences in hierarchy, as Pakhomov and Chernetsov argue) or methodological (differences in what birds could actually see during the experiment, as Sjöberg and Muheim argue) — and the honest answer, based on today's readings, is probably some of both, which is exactly why a single clean resolution keeps failing to arrive.

Mouritsen's 2018 Nature review reframes the whole debate by pointing out that "which compass dominates" is really only half of the navigational problem, and arguably the easier half. A compass — magnetic, sun, or star — can only tell a bird which direction is "forward" relative to some fixed reference; it cannot, by itself, tell the bird where it currently *is* relative to its goal, which matters enormously if weather blows a bird off its intended course. Mouritsen's review proposes that long-distance navigation breaks into (at least) three phases: a first phase, far from the goal, where simply holding a fixed compass heading may be good enough (consistent with Day 3's point that a single uncorrected heading is a feasible strategy for some routes); a middle phase requiring something closer to a true "map" sense, so a displaced bird can detect that it is off course and correct toward the goal rather than just continuing in the same direction; and a final, close-range phase of pinpointing the exact target. The review argues, based on behavioral and physiological evidence, that the "map" component likely uses different sensory pathways than the magnetic compass does, even though both may ultimately draw on the same underlying magnetic field — which maps suggestively onto the two different roles Day 2's readings described for magnetic sensing (an eye-based, light-dependent system more suited to a compass, and the iron-rich macrophage system whose overcast-only effect looked more like a supplementary intensity or "map" cue).

What exactly supplies that map sense is one of the oldest and still least settled controversies in the whole field, and it goes beyond songbirds to the species it has been studied in most intensively: homing pigeons. For over fifty years, one camp has argued pigeons build a mental "map" chiefly from atmospheric odor gradients that vary predictably across a landscape (an idea most associated with the Italian researcher Floriano Papi), while critics have long argued the experimental evidence for a genuinely odor-based map is weaker than it first appears, and that intensity or inclination gradients in the geomagnetic field are a more plausible candidate for at least part of the map sense. Mouritsen's review treats this as still open and explicitly multifactorial — the "map" is probably not one single sense but some combination of magnetic, olfactory, and possibly visual-landmark information whose relative contributions likely vary by species, location, and circumstance, echoing exactly the "it depends" conclusion that today's first two readings reached about the compass hierarchy. That a review written in 2018, summarizing half a century of pigeon and songbird research, ends not with an answer but with twenty explicitly listed open mechanistic questions is itself the clearest evidence that this is a field whose foundational puzzle — how does a brain the size of a fingertip know both which way is forward and where it currently is — remains, after 70-plus years of dedicated research, genuinely unresolved rather than merely under-published.

## Questions to think about

- Pakhomov and Chernetsov argue that contradictory results across studies partly reflect real, evolved differences in compass hierarchy between species or populations. Sjöberg and Muheim argue contradictions partly reflect differences in experimental apparatus (funnel cages vs. open sky view). What kind of multi-lab, standardized experiment could distinguish how much each explanation actually contributes to the disagreement in the published literature?
- Mouritsen's "compass vs. map" distinction implies a bird could have a perfectly functioning compass and still get lost, if its map sense is impaired or if it is displaced far from its expected route. What would that predict about how a severely storm-displaced migratory bird should behave, and is that something existing tracking technology (Day 4) could realistically detect?
- If the avian "map" sense turns out to be genuinely multifactorial — some combination of magnetic, olfactory, and landmark information whose weighting differs by species or circumstance — what does that imply for how confidently a conservation decision (say, siting a wind farm or managing artificial light) can generalize findings from one well-studied species, like the homing pigeon, to a different migratory songbird?
