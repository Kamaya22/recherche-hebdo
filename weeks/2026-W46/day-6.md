# Bird migration navigation — Day 6/7: Methods — how researchers test what a bird can't simply report

## Context

Every claim made so far this week — that birds have a magnetic sense (Day 1), that the mechanism behind it is disputed between a quantum radical-pair process and an iron-based one (Day 2), that celestial and magnetic cues calibrate each other in some species but not others (Day 3), that satellite tags, Motus, and radar reveal real migration routes (Day 4), and that which compass dominates during actual flight is still an open argument (Day 5) — rests on methods that have to infer what an animal perceives without ever being able to ask it directly. Day 5 already put a spotlight on this problem without naming it as such: Sjöberg and Muheim's 2016 finding that funnel cages restricting a bird's view of the sky produced systematically different conclusions than open-sky setups was not really a finding about birds at all, it was a finding about apparatus. Today looks directly at that apparatus and at the two other major experimental paradigms the field relies on: deliberately disrupting a candidate sense to see what breaks, and displacing a bird — physically or virtually — to test whether it can tell it has moved.

The throughline across all three methods is the same logic biologists use whenever the thing being measured is internal and unobservable: manipulate one variable (what the bird can see, what electromagnetic noise it is exposed to, what the local magnetic field says) while holding everything else constant, and read the result off a proxy behavior (which direction a caged bird hops toward) that stands in for a perception that can never be observed directly. Getting that proxy right matters enormously, which is why a seemingly mundane question — what kind of cage, what kind of magnetic coil, what counts as "disrupted" orientation — turns out to be central to whether the last five days' conclusions are solid or fragile.

Today also closes a loop Day 4 left open. That day's survey of satellite tags, Motus, and radar exists specifically to check whether lab-derived compass hierarchies hold up in free-flying birds; today's cage-, disruption-, and displacement-based methods are the lab side of that same comparison, and the two can only be weighed against each other once both are understood on their own terms.

## Today's readings (~21 min)

**"Emlen funnel experiments revisited: methods update for studying compass orientation in songbirds"** (Bianco, Ilieva, Åkesson & colleagues, *Ecology and Evolution*, 2016) — https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.2383 — vol. 6(19): 6930–6942 — ~7 min (abstract, introduction, discussion). A peer-reviewed, fully open-access (CC BY) paper in Wiley's *Ecology and Evolution* that puts the Emlen funnel itself — the circular cage lined with paper that a bird's claws scratch as it tries to orient, invented in 1966 and still the field's single most-used tool — under scrutiny. The authors compare the traditional manual scoring of scratch marks against new computer-vision and video-based scoring methods, directly addressing why a 60-year-old low-resolution method is still the backbone of a field this contested.

**"Anthropogenic electromagnetic noise disrupts magnetic compass orientation in a migratory bird"** (Engels, Schneider, Lefeldt, Hein, Zapka, Michalik, Elbers, Kittel, Hore & Mouritsen, *Nature*, 2014) — https://doi.org/10.1038/nature13290 — vol. 509(7500): 353–356 — ~7 min (abstract, results, discussion). A peer-reviewed paper in *Nature* (paywalled; a free copy is hosted by the University of Oldenburg's own research-group site) from Henrik Mouritsen's lab — the same lab behind Day 2's liver-macrophage finding — showing that European robins lose magnetic-compass orientation in unscreened wooden huts on a university campus, regain it once the huts are electrically grounded and shielded, and lose it again the moment broadband electromagnetic noise is deliberately reintroduced. It is the clearest published example of using an environmental perturbation, rather than surgery or genetics, as an experimental "lesion" to probe a sense that cannot otherwise be switched off.

**"Eurasian reed warblers compensate for virtual magnetic displacement"** (Kishkinev, Chernetsov, Pakhomov, Heyers & Mouritsen, *Current Biology*, 2015) — https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2015.08.012 — vol. 25(19): R822–R824 — ~7 min (full short report, it is a brief dispatch-style paper). A peer-reviewed paper in Cell Press's *Current Biology* (paywalled) demonstrating the "virtual displacement" paradigm directly relevant to Day 5's compass-vs-map debate: birds are tested in Emlen funnels sitting inside a Helmholtz coil that reproduces the magnetic field of a distant real location, so the bird's physical surroundings never change but the one cue a magnetic-map sense would use does. Reed warblers reoriented as if they had actually been transported, which is the method's whole point: it isolates the magnetic field as the sole changed variable in a way no physical relocation can.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with the orientation cage, because almost every other method this week has depended on it. The basic design is simple almost to a fault: a bird that wants to migrate, placed alone in a funnel-shaped cage lined with paper (originally thermal paper, ink, or — in the earliest versions — plain paper and a typewriter correction pad), will repeatedly hop and flutter toward the direction it would fly if it could, leaving scratch marks whose mean direction researchers read as the bird's "intended" heading. Bianco and colleagues' 2016 paper is valuable precisely because it does not try to replace this design, which the paper confirms is still the dominant method six decades after Emlen and Emlen introduced it in 1966, but because it quantifies what is lost by scoring it the traditional way: simple visual or manual assessment of scratch patterns yields only a mean direction and a crude activity estimate, with no information about when in the test session a bird made its choice or how many separate orientation attempts it made. Comparing manual scoring against new video- and image-analysis methods on the same European robins, the authors found that much of the disagreement between methods traced back to scatter in the paper record itself — video could isolate individual takeoff attempts that the paper simply blurred together. That is a modest-sounding result, but it reframes Day 5's finding about funnel cages versus open-sky setups: if the recording method inside a given apparatus already introduces scatter and loses temporal information, then comparing results across labs that used slightly different cage designs, paper types, or scoring criteria was always going to be noisier than comparing the birds themselves.

The second method inverts the logic of the cage experiment. Rather than asking what direction a bird chooses to orient toward, Engels and colleagues asked what happens to that choice when a candidate sense is deliberately disturbed. Their 2014 Nature paper is a natural experiment that became a controlled one: European robins kept in ordinary wooden huts on the University of Oldenburg campus could not orient using their magnetic compass at all, while robins in otherwise identical huts that had been electrically grounded and wrapped in aluminium shielding — cutting background electromagnetic noise in the 50 kHz–5 MHz range by roughly two orders of magnitude — oriented normally. The causal test came from reversing the manipulation twice: removing the grounding, or deliberately generating broadband noise inside an already-shielded hut, brought the disorientation straight back, and the effect disappeared again once the birds were tested 1–2 km outside the city, where ambient electromagnetic noise is naturally much lower. This is the logic of a "lesion" experiment transplanted from neuroscience into sensory ecology: because researchers cannot surgically remove a bird's magnetic sense without removing the organ that might house it (and nobody yet agrees on what that organ is — see Day 2), disrupting the physical signal itself, reversibly and remotely, is the closest available substitute for the kind of knock-out experiment biologists would normally reach for. The result does not, by itself, prove which mechanism (the radical-pair chemistry discussed on Day 2, which theory predicts should be unusually sensitive to this frequency range, versus a magnetite-based alternative) is responsible — the paper is explicit that it demonstrates disruption, not mechanism — but it is exactly the kind of evidence a mechanism hypothesis has to be able to survive, which is why it keeps appearing in discussions of the radical-pair debate.

The third method solves a different problem: distinguishing a compass from a map, the exact distinction Mouritsen's review made central to Day 5's discussion. A compass experiment can show a bird holds a heading; it cannot show a bird knows where it currently is. Classic answers to that question physically moved birds hundreds of kilometers and tracked where they reoriented — most famously A. C. Perdeck's 1958 displacement of nearly 19,000 starlings and chaffinches from the Netherlands, a study still debated today (a 2020 *Journal of Avian Biology* paper by Piersma, Loonstra, Verhoeven and Oudman argued the original data could not actually rule out that displaced juveniles were simply following local adult flocks rather than reading an innate compass, and a 2024 *Biology Letters* reanalysis of Perdeck's original archived ringing records by Pot, Visser, Helm, von Rönn and van der Jeugd concluded the social-following explanation does not hold up, strengthening the case that young starlings inherit their migratory direction rather than learning it from other birds). Physical displacement of that kind is powerful but blunt: moving a real bird changes everything about its surroundings at once — smells, sounds, visual landmarks, local weather — not just the magnetic field, which makes it hard to isolate the map sense specifically. Kishkinev and colleagues' 2015 paper sidesteps that confound entirely: rather than moving the bird, they put Emlen funnels inside a Helmholtz coil (a pair of electromagnetic coils that can generate an artificial, highly controllable magnetic field) and simply changed the field the birds experienced to match a location roughly a thousand kilometers from where the birds actually were. Every other cue — sights, smells, sounds, actual geography — stayed exactly the same; only the magnetic field "lied" about where the bird was. Reed warblers responded by reorienting as though they really had been moved, shifting their intended heading in a way consistent with correcting back toward their normal migratory route from the simulated, false position. That a bird's behavior tracks a purely magnetic manipulation this precisely is some of the strongest available evidence that at least part of the avian "map" Day 5 described is magnetic, and it is only interpretable as such because the virtual-displacement design can change one cue while leaving every other cue, including the bird's actual location, untouched.

Put the three methods together and a pattern emerges that mirrors Day 4's point about tracking technologies: each method is strong exactly where the others are limited. Orientation cages are cheap, scalable, and directly measure a choice of direction, but — as Bianco's paper and Day 5's funnel-versus-open-sky finding both show — they are acutely sensitive to apparatus details that have nothing to do with bird biology. Noise-disruption experiments like Engels and colleagues' can demonstrate that a sense depends on something real and physical without requiring any assumption about what the sensory organ looks like, but on their own they cannot distinguish between competing mechanisms, only rule some out indirectly. Virtual displacement isolates the map question cleanly in a way physical relocation cannot, at the cost of only ever testing the magnetic component of the map and needing specialized coil infrastructure most field sites do not have. None of these methods, alone, could have produced the confident (if still contested) picture built up over Days 1–5; each supplies a kind of evidence the others structurally cannot, which is precisely why reconciling cage-based, disruption-based, displacement-based, and tracking-based evidence is still, as Day 5 showed, an unfinished project rather than a solved one.

## Questions to think about

- Bianco and colleagues found that much of the disagreement between orientation-cage studies traced back to how the data were scored, not to the birds themselves. If a methods paper like this one had existed in, say, 1990, how much of the "reproducibility crisis" Day 5 described might have been avoided — and how much is genuinely about biological variation that no amount of better scoring can remove?
- Engels and colleagues' electromagnetic-noise experiment shows that magnetic orientation depends on something sensitive to broadband radio-frequency noise, which is consistent with the radical-pair mechanism from Day 2 but does not prove it. What additional experiment, ideally one that is also non-invasive, would move this from "consistent with" to something closer to a direct test?
- Virtual magnetic displacement can isolate the magnetic component of a bird's "map," but it cannot test whether olfactory or landmark cues (Day 5's other candidates for the map sense) would have overridden or confirmed the same result if they had been available during the test. What would an experiment have to do to pit a virtual magnetic displacement against a real olfactory cue, and is that technically feasible with current methods?
