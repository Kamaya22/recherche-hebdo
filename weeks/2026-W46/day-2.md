# Bird migration navigation — Day 2/7: Mechanisms — what actually senses the magnetic field?

## Context

Day 1 established that birds draw on (at least) three compass systems — magnetic, sun, and star — and that the magnetic sense is the most studied and, paradoxically, the most experimentally frustrating of the three. Today goes one level deeper: granting that birds can detect Earth's magnetic field, what physical structure or molecule actually does the detecting? This is a genuinely hard question because a magnetic sense, unlike vision or hearing, has no obvious organ — there is no "magnetic eye" to dissect and no nerve that obviously lights up the way the optic nerve does for light. Researchers have had to infer the mechanism almost entirely indirectly, through behavior, biochemistry, and, increasingly, quantum physics.

Two hypotheses have dominated the field for decades, and they are not mutually exclusive: a **radical-pair mechanism** rooted in the light-sensitive protein cryptochrome, thought to operate in the retina and to sense the field's *direction* (an "inclination compass"); and a **magnetite-based mechanism**, long thought to sit in the upper beak via the trigeminal nerve and to sense field *intensity*, contributing to a bird's internal "map." Both hypotheses have had serious setbacks — the beak-magnetite story in particular was largely dismantled by a 2012 study that is itself one of today's readings — and both remain, in different ways, unresolved after 2026's new twist: a paper proposing that iron-rich immune cells in the pigeon *liver*, not the beak or the eye, may be doing much of the intensity-sensing work. Today's readings trace that arc: a landmark result supporting the cryptochrome/radical-pair hypothesis, the study that overturned the original beak-magnetite story, and the 2026 liver finding that reopens the question of where iron-based sensing actually happens.

This matters beyond settling an academic dispute. Which mechanism(s) a bird actually uses determines what kinds of human activity can interfere with migration — a cryptochrome-based compass is a light-dependent quantum process that could in principle be disrupted by certain radiofrequency noise, while an iron-based sensor is a magnetic/biochemical structure with different vulnerabilities — so getting the mechanism right has direct relevance to the conservation questions Day 7 will return to.

## Today's readings (~22 min)

**"Magnetic sensitivity of cryptochrome 4 from a migratory songbird"** (Xu, Hore, Mouritsen et al., *Nature*, 2021) — DOI: 10.1038/s41586-021-03618-9 — ~8 min (abstract, figures, and accompanying News & Views). A peer-reviewed primary research paper in Nature showing that cryptochrome 4 (CRY4) purified from the European robin's retina is magnetically sensitive in a test tube, and more strongly so than the same protein taken from two non-migratory birds (chicken and pigeon). This is the strongest direct biochemical evidence to date that a specific bird protein has the physical properties a radical-pair magnetoreceptor would need — though, as the paper and its accompanying commentary stress, it is in-vitro evidence, not proof that CRY4 senses magnetic fields inside a living bird's eye.

**"Clusters of iron-rich cells in the upper beak of pigeons are macrophages not magnetosensitive neurons"** (Treiber, Salzer, Riegler, Edelman, Sugar, Breuss, Pichler, Cadiou, Saunders, Lythgoe, Shaw & Keays, *Nature*, 2012) — DOI: 10.1038/nature11046 — ~7 min. A peer-reviewed primary study that re-examined the iron-rich cells in the pigeon beak long assumed to be magnetite-based sensory neurons feeding the trigeminal nerve. Using MRI, CT, and electron microscopy, the authors showed these cells are in fact macrophages (immune cells) containing ordinary iron-storage proteins, not neurons, and that their number and distribution vary too much between individual birds to plausibly form a precise sensory map. This paper is essential context: it is the reason the "iron sensor is in the beak" story fell out of favor, which is exactly why the 2026 liver finding below was such a surprise.

**"Homing pigeon navigation relies on superparamagnetic macrophages under overcast conditions"** (Lisowski, Dechmann, Thorup, Hasenauer, Fester, Kuerten, Farle, Wiedwald, Wikelski, Kurts et al., *Science*, 2026) — DOI: 10.1126/science.ady2486, *Science* 392(6801): 985–991 — ~7 min (abstract and accompanying Perspective). A peer-reviewed primary study (University Hospital Bonn, Max Planck Institute of Animal Behavior, and collaborators) screening multiple pigeon organs for magnetic responsiveness and finding the strongest signal, by far, in liver macrophages rich in iron-storage ferritin — the very same cell type the 2012 paper above found, misleadingly, in the beak. Depleting these liver macrophages left pigeons unable to navigate home accurately on overcast days, while birds with the cells intact, and all birds on sunny days, navigated normally. A companion Perspective by Spiro & Drakesmith cautions that this does not rule out other organs contributing, especially when the sun is unavailable.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with the cryptochrome story, because it has the clearest logical chain. Cryptochromes are light-sensitive proteins found in the retinas of many animals, including migratory birds, and a specific variant, CRY4, is both present in bird eyes and chemically unusual: when hit by blue light, it generates a short-lived pair of molecules called a "radical pair," each carrying an unpaired electron. Quantum mechanics predicts that the relative spin orientation of those two unpaired electrons — and therefore the molecule's subsequent chemical behavior — can be nudged by a magnetic field as weak as Earth's, in principle translating a magnetic direction into a measurable biochemical signal. This is the "radical-pair mechanism," and it is one of the few places in biology where a textbook quantum effect has been proposed to directly drive animal behavior. Xu and colleagues' 2021 Nature paper gave this hypothesis its best direct evidence yet: CRY4 purified from a migratory robin's retina showed stronger, more clearly magnetically-sensitive photochemistry in the test tube than the same protein from two non-migratory birds. That is a meaningful correlation — the protein behaves the way the hypothesis predicts, and does so more in a species that actually needs the sense — but, as the authors and outside commentators were careful to note, it is still evidence from a protein in a dish, not proof that this is what is happening inside a living bird's retina during flight.

The second long-standing hypothesis — magnetite-based sensing in the beak — illustrates how a plausible, widely repeated story can later be substantially revised. Magnetite (an iron oxide) is a genuinely magnetic mineral, and earlier electron-microscopy work had reported magnetite clusters inside nerve terminals in the pigeon's upper beak, feeding into the trigeminal nerve, with behavioral and electrophysiological experiments appearing to support a role in sensing field intensity (part of a bird's internal "map"). Treiber and colleagues' 2012 Nature paper went back to re-examine these structures directly and found something different: the iron-rich cells were macrophages — ordinary immune cells whose job is partly to store iron — not sensory neurons, and their number and distribution varied so much between individual pigeons, and even showed up in unrelated tissues like feather follicles and skin, that they could not plausibly serve as a precisely located sensory organ. This did not kill the magnetite hypothesis outright (the authors explicitly left room for "sparsely distributed" true magnetoreceptors elsewhere in the beak that their methods might have missed), but it substantially undercut the specific story that had been built around those beak cells, and left the field without a confirmed location for intensity-sensing for over a decade.

That is what makes the 2026 Science paper from the Bonn/Max Planck team so striking, and such an apt bookend for this story. Lisowski and colleagues systematically screened multiple pigeon organs for magnetic responsiveness — eyes, beak, brain, and others — rather than starting from an assumption about where the sensor should be, and found by far the strongest signal in the liver, in exactly the cell type (iron-rich macrophages) that the 2012 paper had ruled out as a beak-based sensor. The key experimental test was functional, not just anatomical: when the researchers depleted these liver macrophages, pigeons lost their ability to navigate home accurately specifically under overcast skies, while birds with intact macrophages, and all birds on sunny days, found their way home normally (in 70–90 minutes either way). That sun-dependence is an important clue: it suggests the liver-macrophage pathway may be a backup or complementary intensity/map cue that matters most when celestial information is unavailable, consistent with Day 1's point that these systems are an integrated toolkit rather than independent backups. Electron microscopy further showed the iron-rich macrophages sitting close to nerve fibers, hinting at (but not yet proving) a pathway for this information to reach the brain.

Three cautions are worth carrying into the rest of the week. First, correlation and even depletion experiments are not the same as pinpointing a molecular transduction mechanism — nobody yet knows exactly how a magnetically-responsive iron-storage cell in the liver would convert field intensity into a neural signal, just as nobody has directly shown CRY4 doing that job inside a living retina. Second, the 2012 and 2026 papers together are a reminder that the same cell type (iron-rich macrophages) can show up in the wrong place for the wrong reason (ordinary iron storage in the beak) and in a functionally important place for the right reason (the liver) — anatomical presence of iron is not, by itself, evidence of a sensory role, which is why the depletion experiment mattered so much more than the earlier anatomical beak finding. Third, the accompanying Science Perspective explicitly warns against over-correcting to "it's all the liver": the overcast-only effect suggests this is one contributor among several, not a replacement for the cryptochrome/eye story, which is presumably still doing most of the work on clear days and nights.

Taken together, the current working picture is that birds likely combine a light-dependent, quantum-chemical compass in the eye (direction, via cryptochrome/radical pairs) with at least one iron-based system elsewhere in the body (intensity/map information, with the liver now a serious candidate and the beak's role genuinely uncertain) — and that both halves of this picture have had to be revised, sometimes by the very experiments meant to confirm them. Day 3 turns to the celestial cues (sun and star compasses) that calibrate against this magnetic system, before Day 5 returns to open controversies about how these pieces fit together during real flight.

## Questions to think about

- The 2012 paper ruled out one specific anatomical story (beak macrophages as sensory neurons) without ruling out magnetite-based sensing in general. What would it take to be confident that *no* magnetite-based mechanism exists anywhere in a bird, as opposed to simply not having found it yet?
- The 2026 liver finding only showed an effect under overcast conditions. What does that pattern suggest about how birds might weight or switch between different sensory inputs depending on circumstances, and how would you design a follow-up experiment to test whether the liver pathway and the eye/cryptochrome pathway are used together or as alternatives?
- Xu et al.'s CRY4 result and the liver-macrophage result are both compelling in vitro or ex vivo but stop short of showing the mechanism operating inside a freely behaving, navigating bird. What kind of experiment — ethically and technically — could close that gap for either hypothesis?
