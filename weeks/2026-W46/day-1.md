# Bird migration navigation — Day 1/7: Panorama — the navigational problem and the three main compass systems

## Context

Twice a year, an enormous fraction of the world's bird species — more than 300 species in North America alone — undertake journeys of hundreds or thousands of kilometers between breeding and wintering grounds. Many individuals do this on their very first attempt, alone, with no experienced bird to follow, and still arrive within a few kilometers of a specific target. That combination — long distance, no prior experience, and precision — is what makes avian navigation a genuinely hard problem: an animal needs both a "compass" (a way to maintain a fixed heading) and, for some species, something closer to a "map" (a way to know where it currently is relative to a goal). Researchers have spent over 70 years picking apart which senses birds actually use to solve this problem, and — as this week will show — important parts of the answer are still disputed.

This panorama day lays out the three broad classes of cues that research has identified: a magnetic sense (birds can detect some property of Earth's geomagnetic field), a sun compass (using the sun's position, corrected for time of day), and a star compass (using the night sky's pattern of rotation, used mostly by nocturnal migrants). The rest of the week goes deeper: the disputed physical mechanism behind magnetic sensing, including a striking 2026 finding (Day 2); how celestial cues work and how they calibrate against the magnetic sense (Day 3); what modern tracking technology has revealed about actual migration routes (Day 4); open controversies about which cues matter most during real flight (Day 5); the experimental methods that let scientists infer what an animal perceives without being able to ask it (Day 6); and the conservation stakes of light pollution and electromagnetic interference (Day 7).

Why this matters beyond curiosity: migratory species are disproportionately vulnerable to human-caused disruption of exactly the cues this week describes — skyglow from cities can obscure stars, offshore wind farms and some communication infrastructure introduce electromagnetic noise, and shifting climate patterns are decoupling long-evolved timing cues. Decisions about where to site infrastructure and how to manage artificial light increasingly depend on a good working model of how birds actually navigate, which is also a decent lens on the much broader scientific challenge of inferring an internal sense from external behavior alone.

## Today's readings (~20 min)

**"A conceptual framework on the role of magnetic cues in songbird migration ecology"** (Karwinkel, Peter, Holland, Thorup, Bairlein & Schmaljohann, *Biological Reviews*, 2024) — https://doi.org/10.1111/brv.13082 — ~8 min (abstract plus framework sections). A peer-reviewed invited review in a leading evolutionary-biology review journal (Cambridge Philosophical Society / Wiley) that synthesizes decades of caged-bird and free-flying-bird experiments on magnetic-cue use, organizes them into migratory stages (stopover versus active flight), and is candid about a central problem this week returns to repeatedly: many magnetic-cue experiments have produced contradictory results, likely reflecting real species- and context-dependent variability rather than simple experimental error.

**"Nature's GPS: how animals use the natural world to perform extraordinary feats of navigation"** (Richard Holland, *The Conversation*, 2021) — https://theconversation.com/natures-gps-how-animals-use-the-natural-world-to-perform-extraordinary-feats-of-navigation-173700 — ~6 min. Written by a professor of animal behaviour at Bangor University who runs experiments on bird magnetic sensing; explains, for a general audience, how researchers test what a migrating reed warbler can sense (including temporarily disrupting its magnetic sense with a small magnet) and why tiny-brained animals are capable of surprisingly sophisticated navigation. Acceptable here as a researcher-authored piece in a reputable outlet, not a secondhand press summary.

**"The Basics: How Birds Navigate When They Migrate"** (Cornell Lab of Ornithology, *All About Birds*) — https://www.allaboutbirds.org/news/?p=18686 — ~6 min. Academic-outreach explainer from one of the world's leading ornithology research institutions, covering the classic experiments that established the sun compass (Kramer, 1951) and the star compass (Emlen's planetarium experiments with indigo buntings), plus the magnetic-compass evidence from European robins tested with celestial cues blocked out. A useful complement to the two pieces above because it focuses on the two non-magnetic compasses this week will cover on Day 3.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

The starting puzzle is this: a bird flying at night, or a juvenile making its first migratory journey with no one to follow, needs some internal reference that tells it which way is "south" (or whatever its genetically or experientially set heading is). Decades of research converge on at least three distinct cue systems that can supply that reference, and the evidence for each comes from a similar logical structure: take away or manipulate one cue in a controlled setting, and see whether the bird's orientation behavior changes. Richard Holland's lab, for instance, has fitted reed warblers with a small magnet over the head to scramble their magnetic sense, then recorded which direction the birds try to hop or fly in an orientation cage; when the magnetic cue is disrupted, birds that would otherwise orient correctly become disoriented, which is taken as evidence they were relying on that cue in the first place.

The sun compass was the first of the three to be nailed down experimentally, by Gustav Kramer in 1951, using caged starlings that reoriented their hopping direction as mirrors were used to redirect sunlight. Crucially, the sun's position in the sky changes through the day (it moves at roughly 15 degrees of azimuth per hour), so a sun compass only works if it is paired with an internal clock that "time-compensates" for that movement — and experiments with young pigeons exposed to only part of a day's sunlight showed they learn this correction empirically rather than being born with it fully formed. This clock-dependence is a recurring theme in navigation research: several of the compass systems this week will discuss are not simple, hard-wired reflexes, but calibrated systems that young birds partly learn during development.

The star compass, established mainly through Stephen Emlen's planetarium experiments with indigo buntings in the 1960s, solves the problem for the many songbird species that migrate at night, when the sun is obviously unavailable. Rather than recognizing particular stars or constellations as fixed markers, young buntings appear to learn the point in the sky around which the whole star field appears to rotate over the course of a night (close to Polaris, the North Star, in the Northern Hemisphere) — Emlen demonstrated this by raising birds under an artificial planetarium sky rotating around a different star (Betelgeuse) and showing the birds oriented relative to that false "north" instead. This means the star compass, unlike the sun compass, requires no time compensation (rotation gives a fixed reference point directly) but does require a period of early-life learning.

The magnetic sense is both the most studied and, as Karwinkel and colleagues' 2024 review makes clear, the most experimentally frustrating of the three. The classic demonstration comes from European robins: when placed in covered cages with sun and star cues eliminated, robins still oriented in their correct seasonal migratory direction, which can only be explained by some remaining cue — inferred to be geomagnetic. But the review's broader point is sobering: across many follow-up studies, magnetic-cue experiments have often failed to replicate cleanly, and the authors argue this is probably not just noise but a real signal that birds integrate magnetic information flexibly and conditionally, varying by species, migratory stage (a bird deciding when to leave a stopover site may weigh magnetic cues differently than one correcting course mid-flight), and context. That the "most important" compass is also the hardest to pin down experimentally is a fitting entry point for Day 2, which looks at why the physical mechanism behind the magnetic sense itself remains disputed.

Taken together, these three systems are best understood not as independent, redundant backups but as a partially integrated toolkit: young birds calibrate their magnetic compass against celestial cues during a sensitive early-life period, and which cue dominates can shift depending on circumstances (cloud cover, time of night, migratory stage). That interdependence — rather than a single, dominant "true" compass — is likely to be the more accurate picture, and it is also why isolating any one cue's contribution experimentally is so difficult, a methodological challenge Day 6 returns to directly.

## Questions to think about

- If young birds partly *learn* their star and sun compasses during a sensitive developmental period, what happens to individuals that are hand-raised in captivity or translocated before that learning period, and what might that imply for conservation translocations of migratory species?
- The Karwinkel et al. review suggests magnetic-cue use is "flexible and context-dependent" rather than fixed. What kind of experimental evidence would be needed to distinguish genuine biological flexibility from simple experimental noise or low statistical power?
- Given that sun, star, and magnetic systems calibrate against one another, what would you predict happens to a bird's navigation if two of its cue systems gave conflicting information at the same time — and how might researchers test that prediction ethically?
