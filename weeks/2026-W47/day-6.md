# The economics and sociology of remote work — Day 6/7: Methods — how economists actually know what they claim to know

## Context

Over the past five days you have absorbed a lot of specific numbers stated with some confidence: a 46% long-run decline in Manhattan office values, a 2.26% fall in transit ridership for every 1% drop in on-site workers, hybrid work cutting Trip.com turnover by about a third with no loss of output, return-to-office mandates lifting equity-analyst forecast accuracy while raising turnover. Day 5 already put a dent in that confidence by showing that two of the US government's own flagship surveys — the Current Population Survey and the American Time Use Survey — disagree with each other by 60% on how many hours a week Americans spend working from home, over the identical period. That was framed as a controversy about return-to-office specifically. Today's readings zoom out: the disagreement is not a quirk of those two surveys, and it is not limited to measurement. It runs through every one of the three research designs this week has leaned on — large repeated surveys, firm-level randomized trials, and natural-experiment or difference-in-differences studies — each of which buys a different kind of credibility at a different price.

It helps to name the three families explicitly, because they rarely announce which one they are. Monday's SWAA-based review and Friday's BLS comparison are both *survey* evidence: ask many people a structured question, repeatedly, and track how the answer moves. Day 2's Trip.com study is a true *randomized controlled trial*: assignment to hybrid versus office was decided by birthday parity, not by anyone's choice, which is as close as social science gets to a clean causal lever. Day 4's transit-ridership study and Day 5's return-to-office papers are *natural experiments* analyzed with difference-in-differences: no one randomized anything, but a policy change (a mandate, a lockdown, an office closure) hit some people and not others, and the method compares how the two groups' outcomes diverged afterward. Surveys are cheap and representative but can get the basic scale of a phenomenon wrong before causality even enters the picture. RCTs settle causality for the specific population studied but say little about anywhere else. Difference-in-differences is the workhorse for everything in between — it is how most of this week's "natural experiment" claims were actually produced — but it rests on an assumption that cannot be directly verified, only argued for.

Today's three readings map onto exactly that structure. The first extends Friday's single-agency survey comparison into a systematic look at why many different credible surveys of remote work disagree with each other by tens of percentage points. The second is a classic, widely taught essay on why applied economics reorganized itself in the 1990s and 2000s around exactly the kind of design-based causal inference that made Day 2's RCT so persuasive. The third is a recent, technical synthesis of what specifically can go wrong with difference-in-differences designs — directly relevant to re-reading Day 4 and Day 5's natural-experiment claims with sharper eyes, not to discredit them, but to know what to ask of them.

## Today's readings (~23 min)

**"How Many Americans Work Remotely? A Survey of Surveys and Their Measurement Issues"** (Erik Brynjolfsson, John J. Horton, Christos Makridis, Alexandra Mas, Adam Ozimek, Daniel Rock & Hong-Yi TuYe, *Review of Income and Wealth*, 2025, vol. 71, no. 4, e70029) — https://doi.org/10.1111/roiw.70029 — ~8 min. A peer-reviewed article in the journal of the International Association for Research in Income and Wealth, published by Wiley and openly licensed (CC BY), with an earlier draft freely available as NBER Working Paper No. 31193. The authors compare remote-work estimates across many US surveys (not just the CPS/ATUS pair Friday's BLS note examined) and isolate the specific methodological choices — survey mode, inclusion of the self-employed, occupational mix, and especially whether already-remote pre-pandemic workers are excluded — that explain most of the divergence, estimating the CPS may understate work-from-home prevalence by up to 25 percentage points relative to other instruments.

**"The Credibility Revolution in Empirical Economics: How Better Research Design Is Taking the Con out of Econometrics"** (Joshua D. Angrist & Jörn-Steffen Pischke, *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 2010, vol. 24, no. 2, pp. 3–30) — https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.24.2.3 — ~8 min. A peer-reviewed review essay in the same AEA flagship journal that carried Monday's reading, freely readable ungated on aeaweb.org. Angrist and Pischke argue that applied microeconomics was transformed less by better statistical fixes to untrustworthy models than by a shift toward research designs — randomized trials, natural experiments, instrumental variables, difference-in-differences — that borrow the logic of a true experiment wherever one cannot be run, trading some generality for a credible answer to a narrower question.

**"What's Trending in Difference-in-Differences? A Synthesis of the Recent Econometrics Literature"** (Jonathan Roth, Pedro H. C. Sant'Anna, Alyssa Bilinski & John Poe, *Journal of Econometrics*, 2023, vol. 235, no. 2, pp. 2218–2244) — published version (paywalled): https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jeconom.2023.03.008 — free accepted-manuscript version: https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.01194 — ~7 min (abstract, introduction, and practitioner recommendations). A peer-reviewed synthesis article in a leading Elsevier econometrics journal, summarizing roughly a decade of technical work on when the difference-in-differences method's core "parallel trends" assumption breaks down and how modern estimators correct for it — the most directly applicable of today's three readings to several of this week's own sources.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with the survey question, since it is the most intuitive extension of Friday's reading. Brynjolfsson, Horton, Makridis and colleagues do not simply note that surveys disagree; they decompose why. Comparing many credible US instruments side by side, they find the single largest driver of divergence is not sampling noise but a definitional choice: whether a survey's question wording and sample construction implicitly excludes people who were already working from home before the pandemic, counting only the newly remote. Surveys that make that exclusion, like the CPS, report systematically lower remote-work levels — by the authors' estimate, as much as 25 percentage points lower than instruments that do not. Mode (web versus mail), inclusion of the self-employed, and the occupational mix of who actually responds add further gaps on top of that. None of this means any single survey is "wrong." It means "the share of Americans working remotely" is not one number waiting to be measured more precisely; it is several related but distinct quantities, and a chart comparing two surveys' trend lines is only informative once you know whether they are asking the same question of the same population in the same way — exactly the audit the BLS performed on its own CPS and ATUS programs on Friday, now shown to be the general case rather than the exception.

Angrist and Pischke's essay explains why a different kind of evidence — Day 2's Trip.com trial — was treated all week with a confidence that survey-based claims rarely earn. Their argument is that empirical microeconomics, through the 1990s and 2000s, shifted away from large structural models (which require many assumptions that cannot themselves be tested) toward designs that mimic random assignment: true RCTs where feasible, and natural experiments, instrumental variables, or difference-in-differences where they are not. The payoff is internal validity — a credible answer to "what did this specific intervention do to this specific group" — purchased at the cost of external validity, since the result may not generalize beyond the studied setting. This is precisely the trade-off underlying Day 2's trial: randomizing by birthday at one Chinese travel-technology firm is about as clean as causal identification gets, which is why the null finding on performance was taken seriously, but it is also a single firm, a single country, and a six-month window, so nothing in the design itself tells you whether the result would hold at a manufacturing firm or a hospital.

Most of this week's other causal claims are not RCTs at all; they are difference-in-differences designs wearing the looser label "natural experiment." Day 4's finding that transit ridership fell roughly twice as fast as driving when on-site work declined, and Day 5's finding that 35 separate return-to-office mandates across 23 brokerages raised analyst accuracy while raising turnover, both work by comparing how a "treated" group's outcomes moved relative to an untreated comparison group, then attributing the gap to the policy. Roth, Sant'Anna, Bilinski and Poe's synthesis explains two ways that comparison can mislead. First, when treatment arrives at different times for different units — exactly the structure of 35 mandates landing in different months at different firms — naive versions of the standard regression can produce badly biased, even sign-reversed, estimates; a decade of econometric work (several of the names behind the modern staggered-adoption estimators appear throughout Roth et al.'s reference list) exists specifically to fix this. Second, and more fundamentally, the method's "parallel trends" assumption — that the comparison group's outcome would have tracked the treated group's absent the policy — is not something a dataset can directly confirm. Researchers can show the two groups moved in parallel before treatment, which is reassuring, but a flat pre-trend is a necessary check, not proof that the trends would have stayed parallel afterward too.

None of this overturns Day 4 or Day 5's findings; it recalibrates how much weight a single point estimate should carry. The equity-analyst study's staggered design is exactly the setting Roth et al. flag as historically mishandled by naive estimators, which is a reason to ask (though not something this week's reading can settle) whether the authors used a modern, bias-corrected approach — and a reason the Day 5 reading was explicitly logged as a preprint, not yet peer reviewed, rather than settled science. The transit-ridership study's parallel-trends assumption rests on the claim that, absent the pandemic-era shift to remote work, transit and driving would have continued on their pre-2020 relative trajectories — plausible, but asserted rather than independently verified by the data itself. By contrast, the Trip.com RCT's individual-level, birthday-based randomization is largely immune to both of these specific critiques, which is a real methodological advantage — it simply buys that advantage with a narrower claim about who the result applies to.

Put together, the week's three evidentiary families each fail in characteristic ways rather than being simply better or worse than one another. A survey can get the basic scale of a phenomenon wrong before any causal question is even asked, often for reasons as mundane as question wording. An RCT gets causality right but only for the exact population it studied. A difference-in-differences design can extend causal claims to realistic policy settings an RCT could never reach, but it does so by leaning on an assumption that is persuasive, not provable. The honest way to read any of this week's headline numbers, in other words, is to first ask which of the three families produced it, and then ask what that family's specific failure mode would look like if it had occurred here.

## Questions to think about

- Brynjolfsson and colleagues find that excluding already-remote pre-pandemic workers explains most of the gap between the CPS and other surveys. If you were designing a new national survey of remote work from scratch today, what single question-wording decision would you worry most about getting wrong, and why?
- Angrist and Pischke frame the move to design-based causal inference as trading generality for credibility. Looking back at Day 2's Trip.com RCT, what would it take — in terms of additional studies, not just more theory — to responsibly generalize its finding to, say, a European public-sector agency or a US hospital system?
- Roth and colleagues note that a flat pre-trend supports, but cannot prove, the parallel-trends assumption after treatment. For Day 4's transit-ridership study or Day 5's return-to-office mandates, what real-world event (other than the policy itself) could plausibly have broken parallel trends right around the time treatment started?
