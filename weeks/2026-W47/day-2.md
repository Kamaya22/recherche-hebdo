# The economics and sociology of remote work — Day 2/7: Productivity — what firm-level experiments actually show

## Context

Day 1 established the headline fact: work-from-home days have settled at roughly a quarter of all paid US working days, with hybrid arrangements (a few days home, a few in the office) now the dominant pattern among people who can do their jobs remotely at all, while fully remote work has receded considerably from its pandemic peak. Today's question is the one that determines whether that settlement is good news or bad news for the economy: does working away from the office raise or lower what gets produced?

Until recently, this question was answered mostly with surveys asking managers or workers to guess at productivity effects — a method vulnerable to selection (who chooses to work from home) and to motivated reasoning (managers who dislike remote work rate it poorly; workers who like it rate it well). What has changed since 2020 is that researchers now have genuine randomized controlled trials run inside real firms, plus very large, high-frequency personnel and digital-trace datasets that let them observe output, hours, meetings and communication patterns directly rather than inferring them from self-report. That is the toolkit this week keeps coming back to, and today's three readings are a representative sample of it.

The central tension worth holding in mind while reading is the one Day 1 flagged: the literature's answer depends enormously on whether the arrangement under study is hybrid (typically two or three office days a week) or fully remote (rarely or never in the office). Today's readings line up almost perfectly on either side of that divide — a large hybrid randomized trial finding no performance cost, and two studies of fully remote arrangements finding real costs to coordination, collaboration and (in one case) measured output. Day 3 will pick up a related thread — who gets access to each arrangement — and Day 5 will revisit the ensuing controversy over return-to-office mandates in light of this same evidence.

## Today's readings (~21 min)

**"Hybrid working from home improves retention without damaging performance"** (Nick Bloom, Ruobing Han & Zhichun Jenny Liang, *Nature*, 2024, vol. 630, pp. 920–925) — open-access copy via PubMed Central: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11208135/ — ~8 min. A peer-reviewed article in Nature reporting a genuine randomized controlled trial: 1,612 employees at Trip.com, a large Chinese travel-technology firm, were randomly assigned to a hybrid schedule (two fixed remote days a week) or to the office full-time, and followed for six months with performance-review data collected for two years afterward. Because assignment was random, this is about as close to causal evidence as the literature gets, and the lead author (Bloom, a Stanford economist) also co-runs the main US survey of remote-work patterns cited on Day 1 — making this a natural extension of yesterday's descriptive picture into causal territory.

**"Work from Home and Productivity: Evidence from Personnel and Analytics Data on Information Technology Professionals"** (Michael Gibbs, Friederike Mengel & Christoph Siemroth, *Journal of Political Economy Microeconomics*, 2023, vol. 1, no. 1, pp. 7–41) — published version (paywalled): https://doi.org/10.1086/721803 — free open working-paper version with near-identical results, IZA Discussion Paper No. 14336: https://docs.iza.org/dp14336.pdf — ~8 min. A peer-reviewed article in a University of Chicago Press journal, using personnel and IT-analytics data (badge logs, keystrokes, calendars) on over 10,000 skilled employees at a large Asian IT-services company before and during a shift to full-time, non-voluntary work from home in 2020 — a natural experiment rather than a randomized trial, but with unusually granular, objective data rather than self-report.

**"The effects of remote work on collaboration among information workers"** (Longqi Yang, David Holtz, Sonia Jaffe et al., *Nature Human Behaviour*, 2022, vol. 6, pp. 43–54; first published online September 2021) — https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-021-01196-4 — ~5 min (abstract and key figures). A peer-reviewed article in a Nature Portfolio journal, analysing anonymized email, calendar, chat, call and active-hours data for 61,182 US Microsoft employees across the first half of 2020, when the company moved to firm-wide remote work. It is the most directly relevant study on the "collaboration and innovation" side of today's question, since it tracks the structure of who talks to whom, not just how much gets done.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with the trial that most resembles a true experiment, because it carries the most causal weight. Bloom, Han and Liang's six-month randomized trial at Trip.com randomly assigned over 1,600 employees to either a hybrid schedule (home on Wednesdays and Fridays) or full-time office work, then tracked performance reviews and promotion outcomes for two years. The headline finding is a null result stated with statistical teeth: using equivalence tests (not just a non-significant p-value, which can mean "underpowered" as easily as "no effect"), the authors show hybrid work did not measurably hurt performance grades, promotion rates, or engineers' lines of code, for the company as a whole or for any major subgroup. What hybrid work clearly did do was raise retention — quit rates fell by about a third, concentrated among non-managers, women, and employees with long commutes — and raise self-reported job satisfaction. Tellingly, the 395 managers surveyed before the trial predicted hybrid work would reduce productivity by about 2.6%; after seeing two years of real performance data, their own estimate flipped to a perceived gain of about 1%. That reversal is itself informative: much of the skepticism toward hybrid work in this literature traces to manager intuition formed before firms had real data to check it against, rather than to firm measurement of output.

Now contrast that with the two studies of fully remote work, which is the arrangement the Trip.com trial deliberately did not test (employees were never assigned to zero office days). Gibbs, Mengel and Siemroth followed over 10,000 employees at a large Asian IT-services firm as the pandemic forced an abrupt, company-wide shift to full-time remote work. Using objective badge and system-log data rather than self-report, they find total hours worked rose by roughly 30% — including an 18% increase in work outside normal business hours — while measured output barely moved, implying productivity (output per hour) fell by somewhere around 8–20%, depending on the specification. Critically, the authors can see why: time spent in meetings and on coordination activities rose, uninterrupted individual work time shrank, and employees' networks of internal contacts and one-on-one time with supervisors both narrowed. Parents working from home increased their hours more than non-parents and saw a correspondingly larger productivity hit, though the overall coordination-cost story held regardless of parental status. The puzzle this leaves — more hours, flat output, worse coordination — is best read not as "remote work is bad" in general, but as "an abrupt, fully remote, non-chosen arrangement imposes real coordination costs that a well-designed hybrid schedule may avoid."

Yang, Holtz and colleagues' Microsoft study supplies the mechanism in more structural detail, using the pandemic's forced shift as a natural experiment on an entirely different kind of data: the shape of who collaborates with whom. They find that firm-wide remote work made Microsoft's internal collaboration network measurably more siloed — the share of employees' collaboration time spent with contacts outside their own group fell by about 25% relative to pre-pandemic levels, and the network itself became more static, with employees both adding fewer new collaborators and dropping fewer old ones. Communication shifted toward asynchronous channels (email, chat) and away from real-time conversation; interestingly, total meeting hours fell slightly (about 5%), so the "videoconference fatigue" many people reported in 2020 was apparently not driven by remote work having increased meeting load — something else was. The authors' concern is less about today's output than tomorrow's: a more siloed, less dynamic network is plausibly worse at moving novel information between parts of a firm, which is closer to an innovation story than a productivity-this-quarter story, and effects of this kind would be very hard to see in short-run output data of the kind Gibbs, Mengel and Siemroth measure.

Putting the three together, a coherent (if not fully settled) picture emerges. The strongest causal evidence — the Trip.com RCT — says a modest, structured hybrid schedule imposes no detectable performance cost and meaningfully improves retention. The strongest evidence on fully remote, non-chosen arrangements — Gibbs, Mengel and Siemroth's natural experiment — says the same firm, pushed to zero office days during an emergency, saw real coordination-driven productivity losses. And the Microsoft collaboration study suggests why: distance degrades the rich, cross-group ties that may matter less for routine individual output and more for the kind of serendipitous information flow that feeds innovation over a longer horizon than any of these studies can observe. None of the three studies, it's worth being honest, can fully separate "hybrid versus fully remote" from "voluntary versus pandemic-forced" or "two years of adjustment versus six months of emergency improvisation" — a limitation Day 6's methods discussion will return to.

## Questions to think about

- The Trip.com trial found no performance cost from hybrid work, while the IT-services study found a real productivity cost from fully remote work. How much of that gap do you think is really about "two office days versus zero," and how much might instead be about voluntary, planned hybrid schedules versus an abrupt, forced, pandemic-era shift to full-time remote work?
- Managers in the Trip.com trial predicted a productivity loss from hybrid work before the trial, then revised to a predicted gain afterward. What does this suggest about how much of the broader "return-to-office" debate (which Day 5 covers) rests on untested intuition rather than firm-level measurement?
- The Microsoft study finds that remote work narrows collaboration networks in ways that plausibly matter more for innovation than for routine output. Why might this kind of effect be especially hard to detect in the short-run productivity data that most firm-level studies, including today's other two readings, actually measure?
