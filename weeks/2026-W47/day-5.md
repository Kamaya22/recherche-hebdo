# The economics and sociology of remote work — Day 5/7: Controversies — return-to-office mandates, measurement disputes, and the fight over mentorship

## Context

Day 2 left a reasonably settled-sounding conclusion: a modest, voluntary hybrid schedule (two or three office days) carries no detectable performance cost and raises retention, while an abrupt, fully remote, non-chosen arrangement imposes real coordination costs. Days 3 and 4 then showed that this productivity question is tangled up with who gets to choose their arrangement at all, and with what happens to cities when millions of people choose differently than they used to. Today's readings pick at a looser thread that cuts across all three: when firms actually impose a *return-to-office mandate* — a top-down requirement rather than a voluntary hybrid schedule reached by mutual agreement — the evidence on whether it helps or hurts is far messier, and some of the mess turns out to be about how badly we measure "being in the office" in the first place.

Three distinct fights are bundled into what gets reported in the press as a single "RTO debate." The first is a genuine empirical disagreement: do mandates raise measurable output, as some firm-level studies suggest, or do they mostly raise exit rates and hurt morale with no offsetting financial gain, as others suggest — and can both be true at once for different outcomes? The second is a measurement dispute hiding underneath the first: official US surveys of how much work happens at home disagree with each other by a wide margin depending on exactly how the question is asked, which should make anyone cautious about any study that treats "in the office" as a clean, unambiguous variable. The third is a slower-burning argument about mentorship and innovation — whether the under-25s literature flagged on Day 2 (siloed collaboration networks) is really a story about junior employees losing the kind of informal, in-person feedback that builds skill over years, not quarters.

None of today's three readings is a settled, peer-reviewed consensus; that is rather the point of a "controversies" day. Two are working papers still circulating for comment (one clearly still under revision at a journal), and one is a government methodology note rather than a causal study. Flagged honestly as such, they are nonetheless the best currently available evidence on live, unresolved questions — and Day 6 will look in more detail at why building better evidence here is harder than it sounds.

## Today's readings (~20 min)

**"The Impact of Return-to-Office Mandates on Equity Analysts"** (Peixin Li, Baolian Wang & Jiawei Yu, SSRN Working Paper No. 5206058, posted 2025) — https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5206058 — ~7 min (abstract plus main results). **Preprint — not yet peer reviewed.** A working paper by finance researchers at the University of Florida's Warrington College of Business, using a difference-in-differences design across 35 distinct return-to-office mandates at 23 brokerage firms between 2020 and 2023 to study the research analysts who work there — a cleaner natural-experiment setting than most RTO studies, since brokerage mandates hit comparable professionals with precisely measurable, publicly recorded output (earnings forecasts).

**"The Power of Proximity to Coworkers: Training for Tomorrow or Productivity Today?"** (Natalia Emanuel, Emma Harrington & Amanda Pallais, NBER Working Paper No. 31880, first posted November 2023, last revised June 2026) — https://www.nber.org/papers/w31880 — ~7 min (abstract and key results). **Preprint — not yet peer reviewed** (circulated for comment; as of this writing not yet published in the journal it has been under review at). A working paper by labor economists, using office-closure and return-to-office natural experiments at a Fortune 500 software firm with two physically separate buildings to isolate the effect of simply sitting near one's teammates, independent of any other change in work arrangement.

**"A Comparison of Hours of Work at Home Estimates Between the Current Population Survey and the American Time Use Survey"** (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, *Monthly Labor Review*, April 2025) — https://www.bls.gov/opub/mlr/2025/article/a-comparison-of-hours-of-work-at-home-estimates-between-the-current-population-survey-and-the-american-time-use-survey.htm — ~6 min. A methodology article published directly by the US government's main labor-statistics agency, comparing two of its own flagship survey programs' estimates of how many hours people actually work from home over the same period (October 2022–December 2023) — a rare case of an institution auditing the disagreement between its own instruments rather than a third party doing so.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with the cleanest natural experiment on mandates specifically, since it is the one most directly comparable to Day 2's Trip.com trial but in the opposite direction — imposed rather than offered. Li, Wang and Yu track 35 separate brokerage-firm mandates forcing equity analysts back into the office, and find that forecast accuracy and timeliness both improved afterward, with the largest gains among younger, less-experienced and female analysts, and among analysts facing the most time pressure around earnings announcements. Read in isolation, this looks like a clean win for in-person work — but the same mandates raised annual analyst turnover from about 15.2% to 17.6%, a cost concentrated in the first year or two after each mandate and fading by 2022–2023. Put bluntly: mandates bought measurable accuracy at the price of losing some analysts, and both effects are real, not artifacts of how the study is framed — they are simply different outcome variables that do not have to move together.

That "different outcomes, different answers" pattern recurs, and gets harder to reconcile, once you look beyond analysts to firms broadly. A separate working paper (Yuye Ding & Mark Ma, University of Pittsburgh, SSRN Working Paper No. 4675401, "Return-to-Office Mandates," posted 2023) studying S&P 500 firms' mandates finds no significant change in financial performance or firm market value after a mandate takes effect, alongside a significant drop in employee job satisfaction — the authors go as far as to argue their data is "consistent with managers using RTO mandates to reassert control over employees and blame employees as a scapegoat for bad firm performance," rather than mandates being evidence-driven. Both studies can be correct at once: a mandate can sharpen output on a specific, measurable task (analyst forecasts) for the subset of workers whose job is well captured by that one metric, while doing nothing for a firm's bottom line and quietly eroding morale for the broader workforce whose contribution is harder to isolate in a stock price.

Before taking sides in that fight, it is worth asking what "return to office" is even measuring, and this is where the Bureau of Labor Statistics' own comparison of its two household surveys is a useful reality check. Over the identical 15-month window, the Current Population Survey (CPS) reports average telework hours of 5.6 per week, while the American Time Use Survey (ATUS) — a time-diary method asking people to account for an entire day minute by minute — reports 9.1 hours per week of work done at home, 60% higher. The gap is not noise; BLS traces most of it to definitional and sampling differences (ATUS counts any work done at home, whether or not the respondent calls it "telework," and the two surveys draw somewhat different samples), and the agency is explicit that the two numbers are not measuring quite the same thing. A related 2026 staff report by Federal Reserve Bank of New York economists Natalia Emanuel and Emma Harrington (the same Harrington behind the Proximity paper below) sharpens the point in the other direction: even on days employees are physically in the office, roughly half of that time is spent on video calls or solo work with no colleague present, and compliance with mandates is "spotty" — employees average about 2.6 in-office days against an average mandate of roughly 3.7. If a large share of nominal "office time" is not actually spent collaborating with anyone, and surveys cannot even agree on how many hours of remote work are happening in the first place, then a debate conducted in terms of "days in the office" is arguing over a number that may not capture what anyone actually cares about.

That distinction — presence versus collaboration — is exactly what the Proximity paper tries to pull apart experimentally rather than by survey. Emanuel, Harrington and Pallais use the fact that a Fortune 500 software firm's campus spans two buildings, so some teammates share a building and some do not, combined with pandemic office closures and reopenings, to isolate the effect of physical proximity to teammates specifically (not merely "being in an office," but being near the people you actually work with). They find proximity measurably increases the coding feedback engineers give and receive — concentrated among younger engineers and women, the same groups Day 2 and Day 3 flagged as disproportionately losing out when collaboration networks go remote — but it also measurably reduces the proximate engineers' own short-run coding output, especially for senior engineers who do more of the mentoring. Their reading is a genuine trade-off, not a contradiction: sitting near colleagues trades a little of today's individual output for tomorrow's skill-building, with the trade concentrated on exactly the junior employees whose careers are most sensitive to that transfer.

Put all four pieces together and the "controversy" looks less like scientific disagreement and more like four different instruments pointed at four different targets. The equity-analyst and Pittsburgh firm studies disagree because one measures a narrow, well-defined task and the other measures a diffuse, firm-wide outcome that a single policy lever is unlikely to move much either way. The BLS and Federal Reserve findings disagree with the entire framing because they suggest "time in the office" is neither reliably measured across the government's own instruments nor a good proxy for actual in-person collaboration once you are there. And the Proximity paper suggests that even where proximity clearly helps, it helps a long-run outcome (junior training) at a measurable short-run cost (senior output) — meaning any single-number verdict on "does RTO work" was always going to depend on which of several moving parts, over which time horizon, a given study happened to capture.

## Questions to think about

- The Federal Reserve staff report finds that roughly half of nominal "in-office" time is spent on video calls or alone, and that compliance with mandates is well below what firms actually require. If a large share of office presence is not meaningfully collaborative, what would a more meaningful metric than "days badged in" look like, and could it realistically be collected?
- The equity-analyst study's turnover cost faded within a year or two while its accuracy gains persisted. What kinds of adjustment costs (search frictions, self-selection of who stays, learning effects) might explain a short-run cost that fades alongside a longer-run benefit that does not?
- The Proximity paper frames its finding as a genuine trade-off — less senior short-run output for more junior long-run training — rather than a simple "proximity is good" or "proximity is bad" result. If you were designing an office policy, how would you decide how much of today's output to trade for tomorrow's mentorship, and who should bear that cost?
