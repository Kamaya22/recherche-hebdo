# The economics and sociology of remote work — Day 1/7: Panorama — scale, patterns, and why this is still a live question

## Context

In early 2020, within a few weeks, tens of millions of office-based employees around the world went from commuting daily to working entirely from home. That was an emergency improvisation, not a considered policy choice — but five years later, the arrangement it produced has not gone away. In the United States, remote work accounted for roughly 5% of paid working days before the pandemic; it peaked above 60% during the strictest lockdowns, then settled into a plateau of around a quarter of all paid workdays, a level that has held remarkably steady since 2023. That persistence, rather than a quick snap-back to the office, is the starting fact this week's readings try to explain and unpack.

What makes this a genuine economics-and-sociology topic, rather than a lifestyle trend, is that the shift touches several distinct questions that researchers can now study with unusually good data: Does working away from the office raise or lower output, and does the answer depend on whether the arrangement is fully remote or hybrid (a mix of home and office days)? Who gets access to this flexibility, and does unequal access widen existing gaps in pay and career advancement? And what happens to the physical and fiscal fabric of cities — office towers, transit systems, downtown retail — when a quarter of the workforce no longer shows up five days a week? Each of these is contested, with real researchers on different sides, which is what makes the topic suitable for a week that looks at evidence rather than received wisdom.

This panorama day sets the stage with the scale and shape of the shift. Day 2 turns to the productivity question in detail, using firm-level experiments that let researchers get closer to causal answers than survey data alone can. Day 3 looks at who has access to remote work and how that access maps onto existing inequalities. Day 4 turns to the urban and real-estate consequences. Day 5 covers the open controversies, including the clash between return-to-office mandates and the productivity evidence. Day 6 looks at the methods behind these numbers — how do economists even measure something this diffuse? — and Day 7 ties the week together.

## Today's readings (~20 min)

**"The Evolution of Work from Home"** (José María Barrero, Nicholas Bloom & Steven J. Davis, *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 2023, vol. 37, no. 4, pp. 23–50) — https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.37.4.23 — ~9 min (abstract plus main sections). A peer-reviewed review article in a flagship American Economic Association journal, written by the three economists behind the main US survey data on this topic (the WFH Research / SWAA project). It lays out why the shift away from pre-2020 norms has persisted rather than reversed, how remote-work rates differ by age, sex, education, parental status, industry and population density, and surveys the evidence on pay, productivity and innovation effects — the backbone for several later days this week. JEP articles are published ungated by the AEA, so this is freely readable.

**"The new geography of remote jobs"** (chapter in *OECD Regions and Cities at a Glance 2024*, OECD, 2024) — https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-regions-and-cities-at-a-glance-2024_f42db3bf-en/full-report/the-new-geography-of-remote-jobs_6fe0d665.html — ~6 min. From the OECD's flagship regional-statistics publication: this chapter documents that remote work is concentrated in large cities (around 29% of city workers did some remote work in 2022, versus about 21% in towns and 18% in rural areas) and that the post-pandemic pattern across OECD countries is a shift from full-time remote work toward hybrid arrangements rather than either a full return to the office or a continuation of all-remote work. Useful as an internationally comparative counterweight to the mostly US-focused data elsewhere this week.

**"Working from Home in 2025: Five Key Facts"** (José María Barrero, Nicholas Bloom, Steven J. Davis et al., Stanford Institute for Economic Policy Research (SIEPR), 2025) — https://siepr.stanford.edu/publications/essay/working-home-2025-five-key-facts — ~5 min. A short, open-access research essay from a university economic-policy institute, drawing on the Global Survey of Working Arrangements (college-educated workers across 40 countries, late 2024–early 2025). It reports that global average remote-work days fell from 1.6 per week in 2022 to about 1.3 by 2023 and have since stabilized, that remote work is concentrated in wealthier, English-speaking economies, and that hybrid arrangements are especially common among parents — a first thread on the inequality angle Day 3 develops further.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

The headline number to hold onto for the rest of the week is this: in the United States, work-from-home days made up roughly a quarter of all paid working days in 2025, up from about 5% before 2020 and down from a pandemic peak above 60%. That quarter-share has been essentially flat since 2023, which is itself informative — it suggests the economy has settled into a new equilibrium rather than being in the middle of a slow reversion to pre-pandemic habits. The same pattern — a sharp rise, a partial retreat, then a plateau — shows up internationally: a 40-country survey of college-educated workers found the global average fell from about 1.6 remote days a week in 2022 to roughly 1.3 by 2023, and has barely moved since.

That global average, however, hides large variation, and the variation itself is a clue to what is driving the whole phenomenon. Remote work is far more common in English-speaking, high-income economies (the US, UK, Canada, Australia) than in continental Europe, and far more common there than in much of Asia, where in-office norms remain stronger even for jobs that are, in principle, equally "remote-compatible." This is not simply a wealth effect: it correlates with job composition (more purely computer-based, individual knowledge work), management culture, and the housing stock and commuting patterns of a country's major cities. It also correlates heavily with occupation and education within countries — a thread Day 3 will follow in detail, since remote work's benefits are not evenly distributed across the workforce.

A second useful distinction, one the research literature treats quite differently depending on the exact arrangement, is between fully remote work (never or rarely coming into an office) and hybrid work (splitting the week, typically with two or three days at home). The data on where the plateau has settled tells a hybrid-dominant story: among people who can do their jobs remotely at all, the modal arrangement in most countries is now hybrid rather than fully remote, and hybrid's growth has been the main driver of the persistence story — fully remote work, after its pandemic peak, declined considerably, while hybrid work held up. Day 2 explains why this distinction matters so much for the productivity question: the evidence for hybrid and fully-remote arrangements often points in different directions.

Geographically, remote and hybrid work is not just a workplace-policy question but an urban one, because it changes where economic activity physically happens. The OECD's cross-country data shows that remote work, perhaps counterintuitively, is concentrated in large cities rather than suburbs or rural areas — the kinds of knowledge-economy jobs that can be done remotely are disproportionately based in cities even when some of the work is now done from an apartment or a satellite town nearby. That matters for Day 4's discussion of commercial real estate and downtown foot traffic: the relevant shift is not "workers leaving cities" so much as "city-based workers spending fewer days physically inside city-center office buildings," which has a very different set of economic consequences.

Finally, it is worth being honest about where the uncertainty actually sits, because it will recur throughout the week. The scale and persistence of the shift are not seriously disputed — multiple independent survey programs across many countries converge on broadly the same trajectory. What is disputed, and what the rest of this week focuses on, is the causal interpretation: whether this level of remote/hybrid work is good or bad for output, who benefits and who is left out, and whether current urban adaptation is sufficient or a sign of deeper trouble for downtown economies. Those are the questions a panorama day can only flag, not answer.

## Questions to think about

- The data shows remote work concentrated in large cities rather than suburbs or rural areas. What does this suggest about who is actually benefiting from the "flexibility" the shift has created — and who might be excluded by default?
- Hybrid work has proven far more durable than fully remote work. Before reading Day 2's evidence, what mechanisms can you think of that might make a 2-or-3-day office rhythm more sustainable for firms than either extreme?
- Global adoption varies enormously by country even among otherwise-similar rich economies (e.g., the US/UK versus much of continental Europe or Asia). What kinds of explanations — housing stock, management culture, labor law, commuting infrastructure — would you want to test before concluding this gap is about "preference" versus "constraint"?

## This week's theme

**The economics and sociology of remote work — productivity, inequality, and the reshaping of cities after the shift to hybrid work.** The pandemic forced the largest uncontrolled experiment in work arrangements in modern history, and five years on, hybrid work has become the default for a large share of knowledge workers rather than a temporary accommodation. This matters for firm productivity (now testable with unusually clean experiments), for inequality (access to remote work is unevenly distributed by education, income, occupation and gender), and for cities (downtown real estate, transit, and tax bases are visibly adapting to a world where office buildings are used differently than before).
