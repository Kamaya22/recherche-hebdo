# The economics and sociology of remote work — Day 4/7: Cities — commercial real estate, downtown activity, and the "urban doom loop" debate

## Context

Days 2 and 3 stayed inside the firm and the household: does remote work change what gets produced, and who gets to do it. Today's question is what happens to the physical and fiscal container that knowledge-worker jobs used to fill five days a week — downtown office towers, the shops and restaurants that depended on lunchtime foot traffic, the buses and subways built around a commuting peak, and the city budgets that taxed all of it. If a quarter of paid workdays (Day 1's baseline) no longer happen at a downtown desk, that is not a lifestyle footnote; it is a demand shock to a dense, interdependent urban economy that took a century to build around the assumption of daily commuting.

This is also where the week's most dramatic claims live. Commentators talk about an "urban doom loop": fewer commuters depress downtown retail and transit revenue, which pushes cities toward service cuts and higher taxes, which drives out the residents and firms who are still there, which depresses the tax base further — a self-reinforcing spiral rather than a one-time adjustment. Today's three readings let us check that story against actual data on three separate channels — office property values, the geography of spending and foot traffic, and transit ridership — rather than taking the headline at face value. A theme worth holding throughout: each of today's papers finds a real and sizeable effect, but each is also explicit that the size of the effect is scenario-dependent, varies enormously across cities, and is still being revised as more data comes in. That tension between "real" and "settled" is exactly what Day 5's controversies and Day 6's methods will pick apart in more detail.

Today's geography also connects directly back to Day 3. Mongey, Pilossoph and Weinberg's occupational divide — who can work from home at all — is the same divide that determines which workers are missing from downtowns on any given weekday; today's readings are, in a sense, what that occupational sorting looks like once you aggregate it up to a city.

## Today's readings (~22 min)

**"Work from Home and the Office Real Estate Apocalypse"** (Arpit Gupta, Vrinda Mittal & Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh, *American Economic Review*, 2026, vol. 116, no. 2, pp. 674–709) — published version (paywalled): https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20231619 — free NBER working-paper version with an earlier draft of the same analysis (figures were later revised in the peer-reviewed version), NBER Working Paper No. 30526: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w30526/w30526.pdf — ~8 min. A peer-reviewed article in the American Economic Association's flagship journal. The authors build a structural model of the Manhattan office market, combining lease, occupancy and valuation data, to estimate how far remote and hybrid work will depress office rents and asset values; the published version reports a 46% long-run decline in New York City office building values and roughly $556.8 billion in value destruction across all US office markets — the clearest quantitative anchor for the "office real estate" leg of the doom-loop story.

**"How Working From Home Reshapes Cities"** (Arjun Ramani, Joel Alcedo & Nicholas Bloom, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 2024, vol. 121, no. 45, e2408930121) — https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2408930121 — ~7 min. A peer-reviewed article in PNAS (fully open access). Using high-frequency spending, commuting, migration and housing data across many countries, the authors document the "donut effect": economic activity has moved from city centers toward suburbs and smaller cities, with the effect concentrated in metro areas where hybrid work is common and largely absent where it is not — the clearest evidence on the "downtown foot-traffic and retail" leg.

**"Impacts of Remote Work on Vehicle Miles Traveled and Transit Ridership in the USA"** (Yunhan Zheng, Shenhao Wang, Lun Liu, Jim Aloisi & Jinhua Zhao, *Nature Cities*, 2024, vol. 1, pp. 276–286) — https://doi.org/10.1038/s44284-024-00057-1 — ~7 min. A peer-reviewed article in a Nature Portfolio journal (this article's open-access status is unconfirmed; Nature Cities is a hybrid-access journal). Using US state- and metro-level data from April 2020 to October 2022, the authors estimate that a 1% drop in on-site workers is associated with a 0.99% fall in vehicle miles traveled but a 2.26% fall in transit ridership — transit losing riders roughly twice as fast as driving falls — translating into an estimated $3.7 billion in annual transit fare-revenue loss for every 10-percentage-point drop in on-site work, the clearest evidence on the "transit and municipal budget" leg.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with the most tangible channel: the buildings themselves. Gupta, Mittal and Van Nieuwerburgh build a model of how office leases get renewed, how long they run, and what happens to rents and vacancy as tenants shrink their footprints when leases expire — rather than assuming an instant repricing. That matters because office leases are long (often 5–10 years), so the 2020 shock to remote work takes years to show up fully in measured property values; this is why their long-run estimate (a 46% decline in New York City office values) is much larger than anything visible in the first year or two of the pandemic. The mechanism generalizes beyond New York: offices built for an assumption of five-day occupancy are now competing for tenants who need less space, and the buildings that lose out hardest tend to be older, lower-amenity "Class B" stock — a bifurcation that matters for which buildings become candidates for conversion or abandonment, a policy question Day 5 will return to.

Lower office values feed the doom-loop story in two ways: directly, through the commercial property tax base that funds city services, and indirectly, through everything downstream of office occupancy — the lunch spot, the dry cleaner, the gym near the office that depended on five days of nearby foot traffic, not two or three. Ramani, Alcedo and Bloom's "donut effect" paper is the systematic evidence for that second channel. Using granular spending, mobility and housing data, they show that it is not simply that aggregate downtown activity fell — it moved, mostly to the suburbs of the same metro area rather than to entirely different regions, and the size of the shift tracks almost mechanically with how much of that metro's workforce does hybrid work. Cities with a large share of remote-eligible, high-education jobs (think San Francisco or Washington, DC) show a pronounced donut; cities whose job mix is less teleworkable (recall Day 3's occupational divide) barely show one at all. That geographic concentration is itself informative: it tells us the urban effects of remote work are not a uniform national phenomenon but depend entirely on a city's pre-existing industry and occupational mix.

The third channel — transit — shows the sharpest asymmetry of the three. Zheng and colleagues find that when on-site work falls, driving falls roughly in proportion, but transit ridership falls about twice as fast. The likely explanation is structural rather than simply about preference: transit systems were built around a commuting peak, and a large share of their pre-pandemic riders were exactly the downtown-office commuters now working from home some days; once those riders are gone, the remaining ridership is disproportionately off-peak, lower-fare, or trips that were never commute-related to begin with. Because transit agencies' operating budgets depend heavily on fare revenue, and because many US transit systems are run by city or regional authorities rather than being backstopped by broad-based city taxes, this is the channel most directly, mechanically connected to municipal fiscal stress — and the one hardest to recover from by simply waiting, since service cuts in response to lower revenue tend to drive away more riders, which cuts revenue further.

Put the three together and the doom-loop story has real mechanical plausibility: lower office occupancy depresses property values and tax revenue; the same shift in foot traffic hollows out downtown retail; and the commuters who leave transit leave a fare-revenue hole that is hard to fill. But notice what is missing from all three papers, and from the quantitative honesty built into each: none of them claims a self-reinforcing spiral is actually underway citywide. Gupta, Mittal and Van Nieuwerburgh's headline figures are long-run model projections under stated assumptions about lease renewal and future remote-work rates, not observed outcomes — and their own earlier working-paper drafts carried different (sometimes smaller) numbers than the final published version, a sign the estimate is still moving as better data arrives. Ramani, Alcedo and Bloom explicitly find the donut effect is small or absent outside high-hybrid metros, meaning "cities" as a category are not uniformly at risk. And Zheng and colleagues' data window ends in October 2022, well before several transit systems' post-pandemic ridership recoveries, so it cannot speak to whether the transit-fare gap has since narrowed, widened, or stabilized. The honest summary is: three real, substantial, and mechanistically linked shocks to urban economies, each well short of proof that any particular city is caught in a self-sustaining downward spiral.

## Questions to think about

- Gupta, Mittal and Van Nieuwerburgh's headline number is a long-run model projection, not something anyone has yet observed; their own estimates shifted across drafts of the same paper as more lease and valuation data came in. What would you want to see — what data, over what time horizon — before treating a projection like this as settled rather than provisional?
- Ramani, Alcedo and Bloom find the donut effect is concentrated in high-hybrid-work metros and weak or absent elsewhere. If "urban doom loop" risk is really this city-specific, does it make sense to debate remote work's effect on "cities" as a single category at all, or does the policy conversation need to be metro-by-metro from the start?
- Zheng and colleagues show transit ridership fell about twice as fast as driving for the same drop in on-site work, which they attribute to transit's reliance on a commuting peak that has partly disappeared. If that structural explanation is right, is the right policy response to try to win commuters back, or to redesign transit systems and funding models around a workforce that commutes less and less predictably?
