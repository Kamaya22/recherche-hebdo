# The economics and sociology of remote work — Day 7/7: Synthesis — what's settled, what's open, and where to go deeper

## Context

Six days ago this week started from a single, well-established fact: work-from-home days have settled at roughly a quarter of all paid US working days, down from a pandemic peak but far above the pre-2020 baseline, with hybrid arrangements now the norm among workers who can do their jobs away from an office at all (Day 1). Everything since has been an attempt to find out what that plateau actually means. Day 2 asked whether it is good or bad for output, and found the answer depends sharply on hybrid versus fully remote. Day 3 asked who gets to make that choice in the first place, and found two separable inequalities — one structural (occupation), one attitudinal (stigma) — stacked on top of each other. Day 4 followed the same workers up to the scale of a city and found real, substantial, but geographically uneven effects on office values, downtown spending, and transit revenue. Day 5 showed that the loudest public fight — return-to-office mandates — is really four different instruments pointed at four different targets, each measuring something the others are not. Day 6 pulled back further still, to ask how economists know any of this at all, and found that surveys, randomized trials, and difference-in-differences designs each buy a different kind of credibility at a different price.

Today closes the week in two moves. First, two new readings look *forward* from where Day 2 through Day 5 left off, rather than sideways into new territory: one follows the mentorship-and-proximity trade-off from Day 2 and Day 5 into a concrete labor-market outcome — what happens to hiring when fewer junior employees get in-person training — and the other extends Day 4's urban story from "what has happened to cities so far" into "what might happen next," using a dynamic model that allows for more than one stable outcome. A third reading, a nonpartisan US government synthesis of the whole topic, is today's explicit "where to go deeper" pointer for anyone, including Kamil, who wants a single accessible document that cross-references most of this week's threads. Second, the guided summary below tries to separate, as plainly as possible, what this week's sources treat as genuinely settled from what remains open — a distinction this week's own Day 6 reading on research design makes a strong case for taking seriously rather than treating as a rhetorical hedge.

## Today's readings (~20 min)

**"Remote Work Leaves Younger Workers Sidelined"** (Natalia Emanuel, Emma Harrington & Amanda Pallais, Federal Reserve Bank of New York, *Liberty Street Economics*, June 1, 2026) — https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/06/remote-work-leaves-younger-workers-sidelined/ — ~7 min. A research blog post by Federal Reserve Bank of New York economists, published directly by the institution hosting the research (the same Harrington and Pallais behind Day 5's NBER "Proximity" working paper). It summarizes new analysis arguing that the roughly fourfold rise in remote work since the pandemic can account for about 64% of the rise in unemployment among young college graduates between 2017–19 and 2022–24, because remote arrangements reduce employers' incentive to hire and train inexperienced staff. Trustworthy as a first-party account from the institution and researchers who did the underlying analysis, though it is a blog summary of still-circulating research rather than a peer-reviewed article, so treat the specific 64% figure as a researcher's own back-of-envelope estimate, not a settled number.

**"Remote Work and City Structure"** (Ferdinando Monte, Charly Porcher & Esteban Rossi-Hansberg, *American Economic Review*, 2026, vol. 116, no. 8, pp. 3152–3196) — published version (paywalled): https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/aer.20231414 — free working-paper version, NBER Working Paper No. 31494 (revised March 2025): https://www.nber.org/papers/w31494 — ~7 min (abstract and key results). A peer-reviewed article in the American Economic Association's flagship journal, building a structural model in which commuting to a downtown office trades off against valuable in-person interaction, estimated across 274 US cities. Its central, forward-looking result is that cities which have settled at a high remote-work share are disproportionately likely to sit in a part of the model's parameter space that admits more than one stable equilibrium — meaning, in principle, the same city could have ended up at a very different steady state, and some may not yet be at their long-run resting point. The published version reports average welfare losses of 2.3% for cities with permanently low commuting, a model-based projection rather than an observed outcome.

**"Economic Development Implications of Remote Work in the Post-Pandemic Environment"** (Adam G. Levin, Congressional Research Service, R48528, May 8, 2025) — https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R48528/R48528.2.pdf — ~6 min. A report from the nonpartisan research arm of the US Congress, written for lawmakers rather than specialists, synthesizing existing research (not new primary analysis) across exactly this week's four threads — productivity, worker earnings and access, regional/geographic effects, and business and economic-development impacts — and explicitly flagging that researchers have reached "somewhat mixed conclusions" on the productivity question. Useful today less for any single finding than as a single, freely accessible, cross-referenced overview document for anyone who wants a starting point for further reading.

## Guided summary (~9 min)

Start with what this week's readings support with real confidence. The scale and persistence of the shift to remote and hybrid work are not seriously disputed: multiple independent survey programs across many countries converge on the same broad trajectory — a sharp 2020 spike, a partial retreat, then a plateau around a quarter of US paid workdays that has held since 2023 (Day 1, reinforced by Day 6's survey-of-surveys). The causal effect of a modest, voluntary, structured hybrid schedule is about as well established as anything in this literature gets: the Trip.com randomized trial found no detectable performance cost from two fixed remote days a week, while retention rose by about a third (Day 2) — a result that, per Day 6's methods discussion, carries unusual weight precisely because it was individually randomized, even though that same design feature means it speaks confidently only to the population and six-month window it studied. Access to remote work is also solidly, structurally unequal: Mongey, Pilossoph and Weinberg's occupational measures (Day 3) show the ability to work from home at all tracks education and income closely enough that it is largely a restatement of existing labor-market advantage, not a new axis of inequality layered on cleanly. And the urban and fiscal effects are real on at least three separate, independently measured channels — office values, the "donut" shift of downtown spending to suburbs, and a transit-ridership decline roughly twice the size of the driving decline (Day 4) — even though, as today's Monte, Porcher and Rossi-Hansberg reading reinforces, none of those effects is close to finished playing out.

The open side of the ledger is just as important, and this week kept finding the same structure underneath it: a result that looks clean at one level of aggregation turns out to be contested or incomplete one level down. Does fully remote work (as opposed to hybrid) genuinely cost firms output, or did the 2020 studies finding real coordination losses (Day 2's IT-services firm) simply capture the cost of an abrupt, non-voluntary, emergency shift rather than remote work itself? Does a return-to-office mandate help or hurt a firm — Day 5 showed both can be true simultaneously, for different outcome variables, in different studies of different firms, which is less a resolved contradiction than evidence that "does RTO work" is not one question. Is the loss of informal, in-person mentorship (Day 2's Microsoft collaboration study, Day 5's Proximity paper) a short-run adjustment cost or a lasting structural shift in how junior workers get trained — today's Liberty Street Economics reading raises the stakes on this considerably by tying it to actual youth unemployment, but a single institution's back-of-envelope estimate from one analysis is evidence to weigh, not a settled causal finding, and the post's own commenters flagged reasonable objections about overgeneralizing from it. And will cities currently sitting at a high remote-work share keep drifting toward a new, lower-density steady state, snap back, or stay exactly where multiple equilibria make them structurally unstable — today's AER reading reframes Day 4's urban findings explicitly as a question about the future rather than a settled description of the present, which is a genuinely different and more unsettled claim than "office values have fallen."

Running underneath nearly every one of those open questions is Day 6's methodological point, worth restating plainly because it recurred all week: a difference-in-differences design — which produced most of this week's urban and return-to-office findings — borrows the logic of an experiment without ever truly randomizing anything, which means its causal claims rest on a parallel-trends assumption that can be checked before treatment but never proven after. Surveys, meanwhile, can disagree with each other by dozens of percentage points for reasons as mundane as who counts as "already remote" before you ever get to a causal question (Day 6's Brynjolfsson et al. reading). None of this means this week's headline numbers are wrong; it means the honest way to hold any of them is to ask which of the three evidentiary families — survey, RCT, or natural-experiment/difference-in-differences — produced it, and what that family's specific failure mode would look like if it had occurred here.

For anyone who wants to keep following this topic past this week, three starting points are worth naming. The WFH Research project (the Barrero–Bloom–Davis team behind Day 1 and Day 2's main US data) publishes a continuously updated monthly survey, so the "quarter of US workdays" figure in this week's readings is a snapshot that the same instrument will keep updating. The OECD's regional and social-policy working-paper series (Day 1, Day 3) tracks the international and gender dimensions that most US-only data cannot speak to. And today's CRS report is, deliberately, a single accessible document that cross-references most of this week's four threads for a non-specialist reader — a reasonable first stop before diving back into any individual primary study.

## Questions to think about

- The Trip.com RCT (Day 2) found a modest, voluntary, structured hybrid schedule carries no detectable performance cost. Nothing this week studied a true random assignment to *fully* remote work. What would it take — what firm, what design — to get comparably clean causal evidence on fully remote arrangements specifically, rather than inferring it from the costs of an abrupt, pandemic-forced shift?
- Today's Liberty Street Economics reading ties remote work to a 64% share of the rise in young college graduates' unemployment, through a mentorship-and-training channel this week's Day 2 and Day 5 readings flagged but could not quantify at the labor-market level. If that mechanism is right, whose responsibility is it to fix — individual firms deciding their own office policy, or is this the kind of externality (a firm under-invests in junior training because other firms will eventually hire the graduates it trained) that calls for a policy response?
- Monte, Porcher and Rossi-Hansberg's model implies some cities sit in a genuinely unstable equilibrium — small pushes either way could tip them toward much higher or much lower remote-work shares long-term. If a city government believed this about itself, is there anything coherent it could do differently today, given that Day 4 showed office-market and transit effects already take years to fully materialize?
- This week leaned on three different evidentiary families — large surveys, one randomized trial, and several difference-in-differences natural experiments — each with a different, named failure mode (Day 6). Looking back across Days 2 through 5, which single finding would you now trust the least, and does your answer track the strength of its research design, or something else (sample size, replication, how closely it matched your own prior expectations)?
- Day 3 showed that flexibility stigma is weaker in countries with more generous family policy and stronger worker bargaining power, while Day 1 and today's CRS report show the US has unusually high, unusually unequal access to remote work by international standards. If the US return-to-office debate (Day 5) is really downstream of weaker family policy and labor protections rather than a dispute about desks and productivity data, why do you think the public conversation is framed almost entirely around the latter?
