# The economics and sociology of remote work — Day 3/7: Inequality — who actually gets to work remotely, and what happens to them if they do

## Context

Day 1 established that remote and hybrid work has settled into a durable plateau rather than reverting to 2019 norms, and Day 2 showed that, for the kind of knowledge workers who were randomly assigned to a hybrid schedule in a real firm, two office days a week carried no detectable performance cost. Both of those findings quietly assumed something today's reading questions: that the population able to work remotely at all is a reasonably representative slice of the workforce, and that two workers in the same job who both have access to flexibility will be treated the same way for using it. Neither assumption survives contact with the data.

There turn out to be two separable inequalities bundled inside "remote work," and today's three readings map onto them directly. The first is structural and mostly settled: whether a job can be done away from an employer's premises at all is determined by occupation, and occupation is itself stratified by education and income, so the ability to even consider working from home is distributed very unevenly before any manager, policy, or personal choice enters the picture. The second is attitudinal and far more contested: among people whose jobs are remote-eligible, who actually uses that eligibility, and what happens to their career prospects when they do, turns out to depend heavily on gender, parenthood, and the national culture and policy environment a worker happens to sit in — exactly the kind of "who benefits, who is judged" question that a panorama day can only flag.

This matters for the rest of the week in a specific way. Day 4's discussion of cities and commercial real estate is really a story about which occupations' workers are physically absent from downtowns on any given day, which is downstream of today's access question. And Day 5's controversy over return-to-office mandates will be easier to evaluate once it's clear that "going back to the office" is not a neutral policy lever applied to an undifferentiated workforce — it interacts with exactly the gender and parenthood patterns today's readings document.

## Today's readings (~21 min)

**"Which Workers Bear the Burden of Social Distancing Policies?"** (Simon Mongey, Laura Pilossoph & Alex Weinberg, *Journal of Economic Inequality*, 2021, vol. 19, no. 3, pp. 509–526) — open-access copy via PubMed Central: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8328128/ — ~8 min. A peer-reviewed article in a Springer economics journal. The authors build occupation-level measures of how feasible a job is to do from home and how much physical proximity to others it requires, validate them against time-use and realized pandemic work-from-home data, and then use US survey data (CPS, PSID) to characterize the workers who hold low-feasibility, high-proximity jobs — establishing the structural, occupation-based layer of today's inequality question.

**"Teleworking through the Gender Looking Glass: A Research and Policy Agenda"** (Chloé Touzet, OECD Social, Employment and Migration Working Paper No. 285, 2023) — https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/teleworking-through-the-gender-looking-glass_8aff1a74-en.html — ~7 min. A literature-review working paper from the OECD's own social-policy directorate, freely published. It takes stock of existing data and research on the gendered side of teleworking — who gets access, the gender wage gap, and career progression — and argues that national gender norms likely mediate all three outcomes, which is the bridge into today's second reading.

**"Flexibility Stigma Across Europe: How National Contexts Can Shift the Extent to Which Flexible Workers Are Stigmatised"** (Heejung Chung & Hyojin Seo, *Social Indicators Research*, 2024, vol. 174, no. 3, pp. 945–965) — https://doi.org/10.1007/s11205-024-03420-w — open-access accepted-manuscript copy via the University of Kent's institutional repository: https://kar.kent.ac.uk/105356/ — ~6 min. A peer-reviewed multilevel study matching individual survey responses across European countries with national-level data on gender norms, family policy generosity, and labour-market conditions, to test which national contexts predict more or less stigma against workers who use flexible or remote arrangements.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Start with the layer that is easiest to settle empirically: whether your job can be done remotely at all. Mongey, Pilossoph and Weinberg's measures — built from detailed occupational task data and cross-checked against how people actually behaved once the pandemic forced the issue — show that low-work-from-home, high-physical-proximity jobs are heavily concentrated among workers with less education and lower income, who also hold fewer liquid financial assets relative to their income and are more likely to rent than own their homes. This is not a coincidence of timing: the same occupational measure that predicts low remote-work feasibility also predicted which occupations lost the most employment in the 2007–09 recession, well before remote work was a live policy question — meaning the jobs most exposed to being unable to work from home are also, structurally, the jobs most exposed to economic shocks generally. Put plainly, "can I work from home" is mostly a restatement of "do I hold a university-credentialed, higher-income, desk-based job," which means remote work's benefits — from Day 2's retention gains to whatever locational freedom Day 4 will discuss — flow disproportionately to a workforce that was already relatively advantaged before 2020.

That settles access at the level of occupations, but it leaves an odder puzzle that Touzet's OECD review takes up: even among people whose jobs are equally remote-eligible, men and women do not use that eligibility at the same rate, and the direction of the gap is not what a simple "women do more unpaid care work, so they more often choose to work from home" story would predict on its own. Touzet reports that in a pre-pandemic EU sample (2018), about 45% of women's jobs were technically teleworkable versus about 30% of men's — women are concentrated in administrative, clerical and other desk-based occupations that are disproportionately remote-compatible — yet the shares who actually worked from home were nearly identical and very low for both (around 10–11%). During the early pandemic, when employers had little choice but to send teleworkable jobs home, a ten-country OECD average briefly showed men working from home at a slightly higher rate than women (about 39% versus 42%, OECD note: figures varied somewhat by country and wave). Touzet's point is that the technical-feasibility gap, the realized-use gap, and the career-and-pay consequences of using flexible work are three different things that move independently of each other, and national gender norms are the most likely reason they do not line up the way a purely occupation-based story would predict.

Chung and Seo's study supplies the mechanism for why realized use and career consequences can diverge from technical access: stigma against workers who use flexible or remote arrangements is not evenly distributed, either across people or across countries. Building on Chung's earlier UK finding that men are more likely to hold negative views of flexible workers while women — especially mothers — are more likely to actually suffer career consequences from those views, the 2024 cross-national study finds that this stigma is weaker in countries with more egalitarian gender norms and a stronger work–life-balance culture, weaker where family policy (parental leave, childcare support) is more generous, and weaker where workers have stronger collective bargaining power or face a tighter labour market. In other words, whether using remote work quietly costs you a promotion is not a fixed fact about remote work itself — it is a fact about the country, workplace culture, and policy environment you happen to be using it within, layered on top of the gendered pattern of who tends to use it in the first place (often mothers, seeking to combine paid work with caregiving).

Putting the three together: today's inequality question is not one gap but two, stacked on top of each other. The first, occupational one, is largely closed to policy tinkering in the short run — you cannot make a warehouse job remote by changing a norm — and it means remote work's advantages, including the retention and satisfaction gains Day 2 documented, mostly accrue to an already more-educated, higher-income workforce. The second, attitudinal one, is in principle much more malleable, since Chung and Seo show it tracks policy and culture rather than the technology or the job itself — which is exactly why Day 5's debate over return-to-office mandates will turn out to be as much a fight over gender norms and management bias as it is a fight over desks and productivity data.

## Questions to think about

- Mongey, Pilossoph and Weinberg show that the same occupational measure predicted both low pandemic-era remote-work feasibility and larger employment losses in the 2007–09 recession. What does this suggest about whether remote work is creating a new form of labor-market inequality, or simply adding one more dimension to an existing one?
- Touzet's OECD review finds that women's jobs were more technically teleworkable than men's, yet realized remote-work rates were nearly identical before the pandemic. What kinds of explanations — commuting and childcare logistics, employer discretion, workplace culture, self-selection — would you want to rule in or out before concluding this gap is really about norms rather than preferences?
- Chung and Seo find that flexibility stigma is weaker in countries with more generous family policy and stronger worker bargaining power. If that is right, is "return-to-office versus remote" really the right policy axis to debate, or should Day 5's controversy be reframed around parental leave, childcare policy, and management training instead?
