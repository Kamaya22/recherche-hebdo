# The regeneration paradox — Day 2/7: Mechanisms — dedifferentiation, positional memory, and the signaling toolkit

## Context

Yesterday's panorama left us with a structure that recurs wherever epimorphic regeneration happens — the blastema, a mass of proliferating cells that forms at the wound site and rebuilds everything distal to the injury — and with a puzzle: mice and salamanders carry much of the same genetic toolkit, yet only one of them can run this program on a full limb. Today's angle asks what, mechanistically, the blastema actually does. Two problems have to be solved simultaneously for regeneration to work. First, differentiated cells near the wound (muscle fibers, periosteum, dermal fibroblasts) have to re-enter the cell cycle and loosen their fixed identity without becoming a dangerous, unconstrained stem-cell pool — the dedifferentiation problem. Second, the new tissue has to know what it is rebuilding: a blastema that regrows a wrist cannot be allowed to regrow an elbow instead, so the cells forming it must retain, or recreate, information about their position along the limb's axes — the positional memory problem.

Both problems are now understood well enough to be approached molecularly rather than just descriptively, which is itself a change from the largely observational picture presented on Day 1. Lineage-tracing work already told us that blastema cells mostly stay within their tissue of origin — a muscle cell becomes a muscle progenitor in the blastema, not a universal stem cell. Today's readings go one level deeper: what signals cause that constrained dedifferentiation, and what molecular "address system" tells the regenerating structure, and the surrounding tissue, exactly how much is missing and where. The answer turns out to combine a handful of conserved embryonic signaling pathways (Wnt, FGF, BMP, Shh) redeployed in adulthood, plus a genuinely surprising piece of cellular memory that a study published earlier this year helped pin down at the molecular level.

## Today's readings (3 items, ~21 min total)

**Positional Memory in Vertebrate Regeneration: A Century's Insights from the Salamander Limb** (Otsuki & Tanaka, *Cold Spring Harbor Perspectives in Biology*, 2022) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9248832/ — ~8 min (abstract, introduction, and the sections on Prod1 and retinoic acid). A peer-reviewed, freely-readable-via-PMC review tracing a century of evidence that limb cells retain a "positional value" tied to where they sat on the proximo-distal axis, from classic grafting experiments through the discovery of the newt membrane protein Prod1 and the role of retinoic acid signaling in resetting that value. Written by the Tanaka lab (Institute of Molecular Biotechnology, Vienna), one of the two or three groups that effectively defined this subfield, it is the natural starting point for today's topic.

**Wnt Signaling Coordinates the Expression of Limb Patterning Genes During Axolotl Forelimb Development and Regeneration** (Lovely, Duerr, Qiu, Galvan, Voss & Monaghan, *Frontiers in Cell and Developmental Biology*, 2022) — https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fcell.2022.814250/full — ~7 min (abstract, introduction, and the results summary). A peer-reviewed, fully open-access (DOAJ-listed) primary study from the Monaghan lab (Northeastern University) using fluorescence in-situ hybridization to map Wnt ligands, receptors, and downstream patterning genes in axolotl limbs, and pharmacological Wnt-ligand-secretion blockade to test what happens without that signal. It is the clearest direct evidence that limb regeneration does not improvise a new program but re-activates the embryonic Wnt patterning circuit nearly gene-for-gene.

**Molecular basis of positional memory in limb regeneration** (Otsuki, Plattner, Taniguchi-Sugiura, Falcon & Tanaka, *Nature*, 2025) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12176643/ — ~6 min (abstract and main findings). A peer-reviewed, open-access-via-PMC primary study, also from the Tanaka lab, published this year, that identifies the actual molecular circuit behind one axis of positional memory: a self-sustaining feedback loop between the transcription factor Hand2 and the morphogen Shh in the posterior axolotl limb. It is the most direct, mechanistic answer so far to a question the Day 1 reading left open — what is positional memory, physically, inside a cell — and a timely complement to the more conceptual CSH Perspectives review.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Dedifferentiation, the first step inside the blastema, is best understood as a controlled and partial unlocking rather than a reset to a blank state. A muscle fiber or a periosteal cell near the wound does not become pluripotent; it loses its quiescence and some of its differentiated machinery, re-enters the cell cycle, and starts behaving like a lineage-restricted progenitor, consistent with Day 1's point that blastema cells mostly stay within their tissue of origin. What triggers this is a wound-induced signaling environment — nerve- and wound-epidermis-derived cues interacting with the same embryonic pathways that originally built the limb. This is the throughline of the Lovely et al. study: when they blocked Wnt ligand secretion in regenerating or developing axolotl limbs, outgrowth stopped, and when they mapped which Wnt ligands, receptors and downstream patterning genes were active, the pattern in the regenerating adult limb closely mirrored the pattern seen during embryonic limb development. In other words, regeneration is substantially an adult re-run of a developmental program, not a separately evolved one — which is also why the core toolkit (Wnt, FGF, BMP) is conserved across species that differ enormously in whether they can actually finish the job.

Positional memory is the second, conceptually harder problem, because dedifferentiated, proliferating cells still have to "know" where they are so the rebuilt structure has the right proportions and segments. The classic evidence for this, reviewed by Otsuki and Tanaka, comes from decades of salamander limb-grafting experiments: a hand-level blastema grafted onto an upper-arm stump does not regenerate an upper arm, it regenerates a hand — and it also induces an "intercalation" response in the host tissue, regrowing exactly the missing intermediate segment, as if both tissues could read and compare positional values. The newt cell-surface protein Prod1 was identified as a strong correlate of proximal identity: cells with more Prod1 behave as more proximal, and retinoic acid signaling, acting partly through Meis transcription factors, can shift a cell's apparent position toward the body (a process called proximalization). This is powerful, reproducible evidence that positional information is a real cellular property, encoded in specific, identifiable molecules, not just a convenient metaphor.

What has been missing until recently is a comparably concrete account for the other limb axis — anterior versus posterior, i.e., which side becomes the thumb and which becomes the little finger. This is exactly what the 2025 Otsuki et al. Nature paper supplies. In the axolotl limb, posterior mesenchymal cells retain a trace of the transcription factor Hand2 left over from embryonic development. After amputation, that residual Hand2 "primes" these cells to switch on Sonic hedgehog (Shh), re-establishing a posterior signaling center the way one existed in the embryo. Once the limb is rebuilt, Shh itself is switched back off — but Hand2 remains on, so the posterior identity is preserved for the next time the limb is injured. The elegant experimental twist is that giving anterior cells a brief, artificial pulse of Shh is enough to install an ectopic Hand2–Shh loop in them, converting them toward posterior identity — but the reverse conversion, from posterior toward anterior, does not work nearly as easily. Positional memory, in other words, is not a fixed label; it is a self-reinforcing molecular loop that can be flipped, but only in one preferred direction, a genuinely new and somewhat counterintuitive finding.

Put together, these three readings sketch a coherent (if still incomplete) mechanistic picture: wound signals trigger lineage-restricted dedifferentiation through the same Wnt/FGF/BMP circuitry used in embryonic limb patterning, while a separate, more durable layer of positional memory — physically instantiated as maintained transcription-factor states like Hand2, and as graded surface and signaling molecules like Prod1 and retinoic acid responsiveness — tells the regenerating tissue what is missing and in which direction to rebuild it. None of this yet explains why mammals cannot run the same program on a whole limb, since mice retain Wnt, FGF, BMP, Hand2 and Shh in their genome and development; the leading hypotheses for that gap (addressed starting Day 3 and expanded through Day 5) concern how reliably mammalian adult cells can re-enter this dedifferentiated, signal-responsive state at all, and whether the right positional-memory signals are even available to be read in an adult mammalian wound.

## Questions to think about

1. The Hand2–Shh loop in axolotls can be flipped from anterior to posterior with a brief pulse of Shh, but not easily the other way. What would an asymmetric, one-directional memory mechanism like this need to look like at the molecular level — and what might make it so much harder to reverse?
2. Lovely et al. found that regenerating axolotl limbs largely reuse the Wnt-driven patterning program from embryonic development. If mammals retain the same Wnt/FGF/BMP toolkit, what does that imply about where to look for the actual bottleneck: in triggering the program at all after injury, in sustaining it long enough, or somewhere else entirely?
3. Prod1 and retinoic acid give the proximo-distal axis a plausible molecular "ruler." Would you expect an equivalent, single dominant signal to exist for the anterior-posterior axis (now partly explained by Hand2–Shh) and the dorso-ventral axis, or is positional memory likely to be organized differently axis by axis?
