# The regeneration paradox — Day 5/7: Controversies — the fibrosis trade-off, the immune system's role, and competing evolutionary explanations

## Context

Days 2 and 3 described a shared molecular toolkit (Wnt, FGF, BMP; dedifferentiation; positional memory) that axolotls and zebrafish use to rebuild lost structures, and Day 4 showed that mammals retain fragments of that toolkit — just gated into a narrow positional window (the digit tip) or redirected into a non-structural form (liver mass restoration). That leaves the question this week has been circling without yet confronting head-on: *why*, mechanistically and evolutionarily, does the default mammalian response to injury default to fibrosis — a scar — rather than to any of the regenerative routes described earlier in the week?

Today looks at three pieces of that puzzle that are each still actively debated rather than settled. First, whether scarring and regeneration are best understood as a trade-off — two outcomes of the same wound-healing process, diverging at an identifiable point — or as more loosely related phenomena. Second, the specific and counter-intuitive role of the immune system: inflammation was long treated as regeneration's enemy, but direct experimental evidence says the relationship is more complicated. Third, competing evolutionary accounts of why regenerative capacity was apparently lost repeatedly across the vertebrate lineage leading to mammals and birds — accounts that do not all point to the same culprit, and do not obviously agree with each other once you compare the limb/appendage literature with the central-nervous-system literature.

None of today's three readings resolves these debates; the goal is to see clearly what is actually in dispute, and to connect it back to the digit-tip and liver cases from Day 4 — both of which, this reading will argue, can be read as supporting evidence for an immune/inflammation-centered account.

## Today's readings (3 items, ~21 min total)

**Finding Solutions for Fibrosis: Understanding the Innate Mechanisms Used by Super-Regenerator Vertebrates to Combat Scarring** (Durant & Whited, *Advanced Science*, 2021) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8336523/ — ~8 min (abstract, introduction, and the sections comparing the inflammatory and remodeling phases). A peer-reviewed, fully open-access review from Harvard's Department of Stem Cell and Regenerative Biology, published in the gold-open-access journal *Advanced Science*. It walks through each stage of mammalian wound healing (hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, remodeling) side by side with the equivalent stage in scar-free healers, to locate where the two paths diverge, and argues this comparison is the most direct route to anti-fibrotic therapies.

**Macrophages are required for adult salamander limb regeneration** (Godwin, Pinto & Rosenthal, *PNAS*, 2013) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3677454/ — ~7 min (abstract, results summary, and discussion). A peer-reviewed primary study, freely available via PMC under PNAS's public-access policy, from the Rosenthal lab (then at the Australian Regenerative Medicine Institute). It is the key causal experiment behind today's immune-system discussion: systemically depleting macrophages in axolotls (via clodronate liposomes) during the early post-amputation window did not stop wound closure, but it did permanently abolish limb regeneration, producing fibrotic scar tissue instead — and the capacity returned once macrophage populations recovered and the stump was re-amputated.

**Loss of the ability to regenerate body appendages in vertebrates: from side effects of evolutionary innovations to gene loss** (Zaraisky, Araslanova, Shitikov & Tereshina, *Biological Reviews*, 2024) — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/brv.13102 — ~6 min (abstract and discussion, accessible sections; the journal is a Wiley subscription title and full-text access could not be confirmed as open). A peer-reviewed invited-review journal of the Cambridge Philosophical Society. The review surveys the comparative evidence that full-body-appendage regeneration is the ancestral vertebrate condition, lost to varying degrees specifically in amniotes (reptiles, birds, mammals), and proposes four evolutionary innovations tied to life on land — advanced adaptive immunity, keratinized skin, whole-body endothermy, and larger body size — as candidate causes, while arguing the underlying genes were lost only much later than the capacity itself.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Durant and Whited's framing is useful mainly for being procedural rather than theoretical: instead of asking "why do mammals scar," they ask "at which of the four canonical phases of wound healing does a scar-forming animal's response first look different from a scar-free regenerator's?" Hemostasis (clot formation) looks broadly similar across species. The clearest divergence they identify is at the inflammatory phase — composition, timing, and resolution of the immune-cell infiltrate — followed by differences in how the extracellular matrix is laid down and remodeled afterward (collagen type and alignment, in particular: scar tissue is dense, cross-linked, and poorly organized, where regenerated or scar-free-healed tissue reconstructs the original matrix architecture). This is a trade-off framing in a weak sense: it treats scarring and regeneration as two possible endpoints of one shared process that branch at a specific, identifiable stage, rather than as entirely separate programs — but the review stops short of claiming scarring is an *adaptation* selected for speed or infection resistance (one hypothesis noted only in passing); it treats that evolutionary question as separate from the mechanistic one, which is exactly why today pairs it with Zaraisky et al.'s evolutionary review.

The Godwin, Pinto and Rosenthal study is the most important corrective in today's set, because it overturns a once-standard assumption. Inflammation was historically treated as regeneration's enemy, on the logic that animals with intense immune responses (like mammals) scar, while animals with weaker immune responses (like salamanders) regenerate — implying that suppressing inflammation should help, not hurt, regeneration. Godwin et al. tested this directly by removing macrophages — not all immune cells, specifically macrophages — from axolotls during the critical early post-amputation period. The result ran opposite to the "inflammation is the enemy" prediction: without macrophages, wounds still closed, but the blastema never formed, and the limb healed over with fibrotic, non-regenerating scar tissue, essentially recreating a mammal-like outcome in an animal that normally regenerates perfectly. When macrophage populations recovered and the stump was amputated a second time, full regenerative capacity returned. This makes macrophages causally necessary for regeneration in this system, not merely associated with it, and it reframes the whole debate: the question is not simply "how much inflammation," but what kind, on what time course, and critically, whether it resolves — since the same immune-cell type that enables the axolotl's blastema is also present, in different proportions and with different resolution dynamics, in the chronic, unresolved inflammation that characterizes mammalian fibrosis.

This reframing connects directly back to Day 4. The digit-tip regeneration described there depends on a Wnt-active nail stem-cell niche, but Takeo et al.'s own data showed that signal also recruits nerves and, implicitly, the local wound-healing environment that supports the blastema — a smaller-scale version of exactly the controlled, resolving inflammatory response Godwin et al. describe in axolotls. And the liver's compensatory hyperplasia, which Day 4 showed uses a completely different mechanism from blastema-based regeneration, is nonetheless also tightly coupled to a specific, resolving macrophage response (clearing necrotic debris, then switching phenotype to support hepatocyte proliferation) in the reviews of that process — suggesting that across every mammalian case this week has found of "almost" or narrowly-gated regeneration, a well-controlled, time-limited macrophage response shows up as a common ingredient, even though the structural mechanism built on top of it differs completely each time.

Zaraisky et al. take the immune angle and try to explain, at the scale of vertebrate evolution rather than individual wound-healing, why amniotes might have systematically lost appendage regeneration at all. Their leading candidate, among four, is specifically the evolution of a more "advanced" adaptive immune system in amniotes compared to fish and amphibians — the same broad category of system Godwin et al. show is a *requirement* for regeneration in a non-amniote. This is not a direct contradiction (one could lose regeneration not because immunity got stronger overall, but because its specific composition or resolution dynamics changed in ways that stopped supporting a blastema while still closing wounds effectively), but it is a tension the review does not fully resolve, and it is one of the field's genuinely open questions: is "more immune defense" the cause of lost regeneration, or is it the *type* of immune response — chronic versus resolving, which Godwin et al.'s experiment suggests is the operative variable — that actually matters, with "more advanced immunity" being a correlate rather than the mechanism? Zaraisky et al.'s other three candidates (keratinized skin, endothermy, larger body size) are presented as working alongside immunity rather than instead of it, and the review's second major claim — that the genes needed for regeneration were lost long *after* the capacity itself disappeared — implies these amniote traits suppressed regeneration functionally first, with genetic decay following passively once the trait was no longer under selection to be maintained.

It is worth noting, without treating it as a fourth formal reading, that the same debate looks different again once you leave limbs for the central nervous system. A 2022 perspective by Seth Blackshaw (Johns Hopkins, *Frontiers in Neuroscience*) argues that for CNS regeneration specifically — not appendages — the most compelling driver of loss across birds and mammals is selection for resistance to the spread of infection inside the skull and spinal cord, since the kind of robust glial scarring (reactive gliosis) that walls off an infection is largely incompatible with the permissive, growth-friendly environment axon regrowth needs. That is a genuinely different mechanism from Zaraisky et al.'s four limb-focused candidates, even though both ultimately implicate the immune system. Taken together, the honest state of the field is that there is no single accepted explanation for "why mammals don't regenerate" — the limb literature and the CNS literature currently favor different, only partially compatible stories, and even within the limb literature, whether immune system changes are *the* cause or *one of several interacting* causes remains unresolved.

## Questions to think about

1. Godwin et al. show that macrophages are necessary for axolotl limb regeneration, while Zaraisky et al. propose that a more advanced immune system is one reason amniotes lost that same capacity. What specific property of an immune response (composition, timing, resolution, cell phenotype) would you measure to tell whether these two findings are actually compatible, versus genuinely in tension?
2. Durant and Whited locate the scarring/regeneration divergence at the inflammatory and remodeling phases of wound healing, but don't commit to an evolutionary story for why mammals' version of that phase differs. If you had to design an experiment connecting their mechanistic account to Zaraisky et al.'s evolutionary one, what would it look like?
3. Blackshaw's infection-resistance hypothesis for CNS regeneration loss and Zaraisky et al.'s four-innovations hypothesis for limb regeneration loss could both be correct for their respective tissues, or one framework could eventually be shown to generalize. What kind of evidence would convince you that vertebrate regeneration loss has one dominant cause rather than several independent, tissue-specific ones?
