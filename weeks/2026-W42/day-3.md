# The regeneration paradox — Day 3/7: Key findings — what single-cell lineage tracing reveals about blastema origins and the limits of plasticity

## Context

Days 1 and 2 left us with a load-bearing but still somewhat abstract claim: blastema cells "mostly remember their tissue of origin" rather than becoming broadly pluripotent, and this constrained dedifferentiation is driven by wound signals re-activating embryonic Wnt/FGF/BMP circuitry. Today's reading asks how researchers actually know this, at the resolution of individual cells, and what the modern single-cell genomics toolkit — lineage tracing combined with single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) — has added to decades of grafting experiments.

The question matters because two very different pictures of the blastema were plausible before these tools existed. One possibility: a rare population of true stem cells, already present in the uninjured limb, wakes up after amputation and builds the blastema. The other: ordinary differentiated cells near the wound (muscle fibers, periosteum, dermal fibroblasts) themselves dedifferentiate into a transient, lineage-restricted progenitor state. These two models have very different implications for regenerative medicine — the first would mean looking for and preserving a hidden stem-cell reservoir, the second would mean learning to safely and transiently reprogram ordinary adult cells on demand. Modern lineage tracing, which permanently and heritably labels a chosen cell population so that every one of its descendants can be identified later even after dedifferentiation and redifferentiation, combined with scRNA-seq, which reads out the full gene-expression state of thousands of individual cells at once, is the tool combination built to distinguish these two models directly.

Today's readings also pick up a thread flagged at the end of Day 1 and Day 2: the cancer connection. If the barrier between a controlled, lineage-restricted blastema and a dangerous, unconstrained proliferative state is partly about how far cells are allowed to drift from their original identity, then precisely measuring the limits of that drift — which lineages mix, which never do, and what happens elsewhere in the body while this is going on — is not just descriptive biology. It is the evidence base for how much plasticity can be safely unlocked.

## Today's readings (3 items, ~21 min total)

**Cells keep a memory of their tissue origin during axolotl limb regeneration** (Kragl, Knapp, Nacu, Khattak, Maden, Epperlein & Tanaka, *Nature*, 2009) — https://doi.org/10.1038/nature08152 — ~7 min (abstract and results overview). The foundational lineage-tracing study behind the claim introduced on Day 1 and Day 2. Using GFP-labeled tissue grafts tracked through amputation, regeneration and redifferentiation, the Tanaka lab showed that most tissue types stay strictly within their lineage in the blastema (muscle-derived and Schwann-cell-derived progenitors build only muscle and nerve sheath, respectively) while dermis is the one notably more flexible tissue, able to contribute to cartilage and tendon in addition to skin. It predates single-cell sequencing but remains the most-cited direct demonstration that the blastema is a mixed pool of restricted progenitors, not a pluripotent one.

**Single-cell analysis uncovers convergence of cell identities during axolotl limb regeneration** (Gerber, Murawala, Knapp, Masselink, Schuez, Hermann, Gac-Santel, Nowoshilow, Kageyama, Khattak, Currie, Camp, Tanaka & Treutlein, *Science*, 2018) — https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaq0681 — ~7 min (abstract, introduction and main results). This study combined genetic (Cre-loxP) lineage tracing of connective-tissue populations with scRNA-seq across regeneration stages, directly testing whether a hidden, pre-existing blastema-like progenitor sits dormant in the uninjured adult limb. It found no such population: mature connective-tissue cells instead converge, after injury, onto a shared, more homogeneous progenitor-like transcriptional state, before diverging again as they redifferentiate — a result that favors the "ordinary cells transiently reprogram" model over the "hidden stem cell" model.

**Transcriptomic landscape of the blastema niche in regenerating adult axolotl limbs at single-cell resolution** (Leigh, Dunlap, Johnson, Mariano, Oshiro, Wong, Bryant, Miller, Ratner, Chen, Ye, Haas & Whited, *Nature Communications*, 2018) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6279788/ — ~7 min (abstract and introduction). A complementary, larger-scale single-cell atlas (over 25,000 cells across epidermal, mesenchymal and hematopoietic lineages) from Jessica Whited's lab at Harvard — the same group whose comparative review opened Day 1's reading list. Published independently and in the same year as the Gerber/Tanaka study, it provides a second, broader map of blastema cell states and is fully open access, making it a good complement for readers who want to check the convergence finding against an independently generated dataset.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

The clearest result to come out of this single-cell work is a direct answer to a question that grafting experiments alone could not fully settle: is there a rare, dedicated stem-cell population already present in the uninjured adult limb, waiting for an injury to activate it? Gerber et al.'s combination of lineage tracing and transcriptomics says no, at least for the connective-tissue compartment that makes up most of the blastema's mass. Instead, ordinary differentiated connective-tissue cells — periosteum, dermis, tendon, each with its own transcriptional signature in the healthy limb — lose that distinct identity after amputation and converge onto a shared, more uniform progenitor-like state, essentially the single-cell signature of what earlier work had only inferred indirectly. This converging population then re-diverges as the new limb forms, splitting back into distinct connective-tissue identities as it redifferentiates. Leigh et al.'s independent, larger-scale atlas of the blastema reinforces this general picture while extending it across more cell types, including epidermal and blood-lineage cells, giving two separately generated datasets that broadly agree on the shape of the transition.

This "convergence" picture refines rather than overturns what Kragl et al. established a decade earlier with a cruder but conceptually cleaner method — direct, physical tracking of GFP-labeled grafts. Kragl's central finding was that this convergence has firm limits: muscle-derived cells, even after dedifferentiating and proliferating in the blastema, only ever re-differentiate into muscle; Schwann-cell-derived progenitors only ever rebuild nerve sheath. Dermis is the interesting exception, the one tissue origin shown to cross into more than one downstream fate (cartilage and tendon as well as skin), but even this flexibility stays within a connective-tissue "family" rather than crossing into, say, muscle or epidermis. Read together, the two generations of evidence converge on a specific, falsifiable version of the plasticity-limits claim: the blastema's apparent homogeneity at the transcriptional level (what Gerber and Leigh's single-cell data show) coexists with strict fate restriction at the lineage level (what Kragl's physical tracking shows) — cells look more alike in the blastema than they will end up being, but they do not actually swap destinies across major tissue boundaries.

Why does this matter beyond satisfying curiosity about mechanism? It is a direct, measured answer to the question Day 1 raised about cancer risk. A regenerating structure needs cells that proliferate rapidly and transiently abandon their differentiated state — exactly the behavior that, unconstrained, defines a tumor. What these lineage-tracing studies show is that axolotl regeneration achieves rapid proliferation without ever fully erasing identity: the "memory" of tissue origin persists through the dedifferentiated state as a kind of safety rail, visible now not just as an outcome (muscle redifferentiates into muscle) but, in the single-cell data, as retained transcriptional signatures that bias and ultimately resolve back toward the original fate. This gives Day 5's planned discussion of the fibrosis/regeneration trade-off, and the broader translational interest in regeneration research, a concrete molecular anchor: the goal is not to unlock pluripotency in mammalian cells, which would be dangerous, but to reproduce this specific, bounded form of plasticity.

A separate and genuinely surprising finding worth flagging, even though it sits slightly outside the blastema itself, concerns how far an injury's effects travel through the body. A 2022 study in *Nature Cell Biology* from Kenneth Poss's lab (Sun et al.) found that cardiac injury in zebrafish does not just trigger local gene-expression changes in the heart: the transcription factor gene *cebpd* is also switched on remotely, in ependymal cells of the brain and tubular cells of the kidney, through a specific hormone-responsive enhancer that the authors showed is normally held in a "poised," rapidly inducible state. Deleting that enhancer region selectively abolished the remote brain and kidney response while leaving the local cardiac response intact, which is about as direct as genetic evidence gets for a programmed, whole-body signaling response to a local injury. It would be an overreach to call this "priming of future regenerative competence" at those distant sites — the study demonstrates a coordinated remote transcriptional and physiological response (affecting fluid regulation between body compartments), not that the brain or kidney themselves become more regeneration-competent as a result. That distinction — a real, measured systemic response to injury, short of a confirmed systemic priming of regenerative capacity elsewhere — is itself a useful illustration of how specific this field's claims have to be: the molecular tools are precise enough now to show that something is happening body-wide after a local injury, even before every function of that signal is pinned down.

Put together, today's readings sharpen rather than simplify the picture from Days 1 and 2. The blastema is not a uniform mystery pool; it is measurably, lineage-by-lineage, a constrained reprogramming event, now visible in single-cell resolution across two independently generated datasets and consistent with fifteen-year-old lineage-tracing results obtained by cruder but complementary methods. And the consequences of a local injury are not necessarily local: at least one clear, mechanistically dissected example shows a wound-induced signal reaching organs on the other side of the body. Both findings push the same way — toward a more precise, more surprising biology than "mammals lack a gene that salamanders have," and toward the harder, more specific question Days 4 through 6 will keep narrowing in on: exactly which parts of this bounded, body-wide regenerative program mammals retain, lose, or never reliably trigger.

## Questions to think about

1. Gerber et al. found that connective-tissue cells converge onto a shared transcriptional state in the blastema, while Kragl et al. found that (muscle and Schwann-cell lineages aside) these same broad categories of cells never actually swap fates. What would it take, experimentally, to show that "transcriptional convergence" and "fate restriction" are two views of the same underlying constraint, rather than two separate, coincidentally compatible phenomena?
2. If a local injury in zebrafish can trigger a specific, genetically traceable response in the brain and kidney, what other "systemic" costs or benefits of regeneration might be hiding in organs that are not the one actually being rebuilt — and how would you design an experiment to tell a true regenerative priming effect apart from a generic stress or immune response?
3. Dermis was the one tissue in Kragl et al.'s data that could cross into more than one fate (cartilage and tendon, beyond skin). What might make connective tissue generally — as opposed to muscle or nerve — more amenable to this kind of expanded plasticity, and does that help explain why so much of the modern single-cell work has focused on connective-tissue populations specifically?
