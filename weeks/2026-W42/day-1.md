# The regeneration paradox — Day 1/7: What regeneration is, and why mammals are the outlier

## Context

Cut the leg off a salamander and, over a few weeks, it grows back: skin, muscle, cartilage, bone, nerve, blood vessels, all correctly patterned and proportioned to the rest of the limb. Cut a fin off a zebrafish and the same thing happens in days. Cut a planarian flatworm in half and each half can regrow into a complete, smaller worm. Humans do none of this. A severed finger does not grow back; a damaged heart does not rebuild lost muscle; most organ injuries heal by fibrosis — the deposition of a disorganized collagen patch, functionally a scar, not a replacement of the lost tissue's original structure.

For most of the history of biology this was treated as a curiosity about a handful of "regenerative" species. The more interesting framing, and the one this week will build on, is the opposite: regenerative capacity is widespread and ancestral across the animal tree, and what needs explaining is why it was reduced, repeatedly, in the lineage that leads to mammals, including us. Hydras, planarians, and many invertebrates can regenerate almost any body part. Among vertebrates, teleost fish (like zebrafish) and salamanders (like the axolotl) retain this ability as adults for whole appendages; lizards can regrow a tail (though an imperfect, cartilage-rod version, not a full vertebral column); even mice and primates, including humans, can regenerate the very distal tip of a fingertip, but only within a narrow developmental window and only distal to the last joint. This "regeneration-competence gradient" across related animals is the central empirical fact the field tries to explain, and it reframes human limb loss not as a biological inevitability but as the product of specific, identifiable trade-offs made somewhere in vertebrate evolution.

The stakes go beyond zoology. Fibrosis — the scarring outcome that substitutes for regeneration in mammals — is itself one of the leading drivers of organ failure and death worldwide (in liver, lung, kidney and heart disease). The molecular program that builds a regenerating structure (rapid, controlled cell proliferation, loss of adult cell identity, re-activation of embryonic patterning genes) is mechanistically close to the program that, when it escapes control, drives cancer. Understanding why some animals can run this program safely and repeatedly, while mammals mostly cannot, is therefore directly relevant to regenerative medicine, wound care, and even oncology — which is why, as we'll see on Day 4, US federal science agencies have made human limb regeneration an explicit research priority. Today's reading lays the groundwork: what regeneration looks like across species, the shared concept — the blastema — that recurs wherever it happens, and the first-pass explanations for why mammals are the exception rather than the rule.

## Today's readings (3 items, ~21 min total)

**Common themes in tetrapod appendage regeneration: a cellular perspective** (Miller, Johnson & Whited, *EvoDevo*, 2019) — https://evodevojournal.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s13227-019-0124-7 — ~8 min (abstract, introduction, and the concluding comparison across systems). A peer-reviewed review, open access via BioMed Central/Springer Nature, that compares salamander limb regeneration, lizard tail regeneration, deer antler replacement, and mouse digit-tip regeneration side by side at the cellular level, explicitly asking what is shared and why mammals are so restricted. Written by researchers in a leading regeneration lab (Whited lab, Harvard), it is a trustworthy synthesis of a genuinely comparative question.

**Toeing the line between regeneration and fibrosis** (Jou & Lehoczky, *Frontiers in Cell and Developmental Biology*, 2023) — https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fcell.2023.1217185/full — ~7 min (abstract and introduction). A peer-reviewed, open-access review focused on the one clear case of epimorphic (structure-replacing) regeneration mammals retain as adults: the regrowth of the distal digit tip after amputation, in mice and in young children, versus the scarring that results from an otherwise identical amputation just a few millimeters more proximal. It is the clearest evidence that mammals are not regeneration-incapable in principle, only in most contexts — and a good preview of Day 4.

**Opportunities for Rapid Advancement of Limb Regeneration: From Animal Models to Humans** (NICHD, National Institutes of Health, workshop page, 2021) — https://www.nichd.nih.gov/about/meetings/2021/021721 — ~6 min. The public event page and agenda for a NICHD-sponsored workshop that brought together regeneration researchers and clinicians to build a research road map toward human limb regeneration. As the US federal institute that funds much of this research, NICHD's own framing of the problem and its priorities is a direct, institutional source for why this question is taken seriously as a biomedical goal, not just a biological curiosity.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Regeneration researchers draw a basic distinction between two kinds of tissue repair. Morphallaxis is the repatterning of existing tissue without much new growth (seen in simple animals like hydra). Epimorphosis is what matters for limbs and organs: the growth of substantial new tissue to replace what was lost, and it is this second kind that nearly all of this week's reading concerns. Across the animal species capable of epimorphic regeneration — salamanders regrowing a limb, zebrafish regrowing a fin, lizards regrowing (a version of) a tail — a common structure appears at the wound site: the blastema, a mass of proliferating, partially dedifferentiated cells that forms within days of injury and acts as the source of all the new tissue. The blastema is not a generic stem-cell clump; lineage-tracing work (which the EvoDevo review surveys) shows that cells largely remember their tissue of origin — a cell that was muscle before amputation mostly stays in the muscle lineage in the blastema — challenging an older, simpler picture in which blastema cells were thought to become broadly pluripotent. What the blastema cells do share is a transient loss of their fully differentiated, quiescent state and a re-activation of signaling pathways (Wnt, FGF, BMP — the subject of Day 2) otherwise mostly silent in the adult, which together drive the proliferation and patterning needed to rebuild the missing structure in correct proportion and orientation.

The comparative lens is what makes the mammalian case puzzling rather than simply expected. Mammals are not uniformly "bad" at this: deer fully regrow a dropped antler each year (though triggered by seasonal hormones rather than injury, so it is a special case); and — critically — mice and humans can regenerate the very tip of a digit, distal to the last joint, provided the nail organ is still present to help organize the new growth. Move the amputation a few millimeters more proximal, past that joint, and the identical animal heals the wound with fibrosis instead: scar tissue, no new bone or nail, no functional replacement. This "positional threshold" for regeneration within a single species and single appendage is one of the strongest clues in the field, because it means the explanation for "why mammals don't regenerate limbs" cannot simply be "mammals lack the genes" — mice carry much of the same toolkit used by salamanders, since these pathways are deeply conserved across vertebrates. Instead, something about the local tissue environment, immune response, or cellular competence determines, case by case, whether an injury resolves toward regeneration or toward fibrosis.

That fibrosis is not a passive failure but an active, evolutionarily favored alternative is an important reframing this week will return to. Scar formation seals a wound fast, controls bleeding, and reduces infection risk — all urgent problems for a warm-blooded animal with fast metabolism and (unlike many regenerating fish and amphibians) no ability to simply regrow a limb slowly over weeks without it mattering for survival in the interim. Several of the species comparisons in today's readings point toward the immune system — particularly the behavior of macrophages and the intensity of the initial inflammatory response — as a key variable that tips an injury toward one outcome or the other; Day 5 will dig into this hypothesis and its competitors in detail.

Finally, it is worth registering why this is an active, well-funded research area rather than settled textbook science. The NICHD workshop reading today makes explicit what the biology implies: if the barrier to human limb regeneration is not a missing gene but a matter of locally re-creating permissive conditions (the right signals, the right immune environment, the right transient dedifferentiation), then in principle it is an engineerable problem, not an immutable one. That optimism, balanced against how little is still understood even in well-studied model species like the axolotl, is the tension running through the rest of this week.

## Questions to think about

1. If mice and humans can already regenerate a digit tip but not a whole limb, what does that suggest about where — anatomically or molecularly — the real bottleneck to regeneration lies, compared to a model where mammals simply "lack the right genes"?
2. Scar formation is fast and protects against infection and blood loss; regeneration is slow and metabolically costly. Under what circumstances might natural selection favor one strategy over the other, and does that help explain why small, cold-blooded, aquatic or semi-aquatic animals (fish, amphibians) are disproportionately represented among strong regenerators?
3. The blastema forms from cells that mostly "remember" their tissue of origin rather than becoming broadly pluripotent. Why might this constrained form of plasticity be safer for the organism than true pluripotency — and how might it connect to the cancer risk of unlocking strong regenerative programs in mammals?

## This week's theme

**The regeneration paradox** — why some animals can regrow limbs and organs and humans (mostly) cannot.

Salamanders can regrow an entire amputated limb; zebrafish regrow fins and heart muscle; planarians can regrow a whole body from a fragment. Humans close most wounds with scar tissue instead, and our one clear exception — fingertip regrowth in young children — only highlights how narrow this capacity is. The puzzle is not that regeneration is rare in nature, but that it appears to have been reduced, repeatedly, specifically in the lineage leading to mammals, while being retained or elaborated elsewhere. This matters beyond zoology: fibrosis, the scarring outcome that substitutes for regeneration in mammals, is a leading driver of organ failure, and the cellular machinery of controlled regrowth is closely related to the machinery of cancer. Modern lineage-tracing and single-cell tools have turned this from a descriptive question into a mechanistic one, with real disagreements still open about why mammals can't use the shared regenerative toolkit as fully as other vertebrates.
