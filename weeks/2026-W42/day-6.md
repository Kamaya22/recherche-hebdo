# The regeneration paradox — Day 6/7: Methods and applications — how researchers manipulate regeneration, and efforts to induce it in mammals

## Context

Over the week, this reading has made a lot of causal claims: that Wnt/FGF/BMP signaling patterns a blastema (Day 2), that blastema cells mostly stay loyal to their tissue of origin rather than freely transdifferentiating (Day 3), that digit-tip and liver regeneration in mammals borrow pieces of the same toolkit in a narrow, gated way (Day 4), and that macrophages are not just associated with axolotl regeneration but *necessary* for it (Day 5). None of those claims follow from simply watching a limb regrow. Each rests on a specific experimental method — a way of intervening in the system, not just describing it — and the methods differ sharply in what kind of claim they can support: necessity (remove X, does regeneration fail?), sufficiency (add X to a non-regenerating context, does regeneration start?), or correlation with an unknown mechanism (X varies with regenerative ability, cause unclear).

Today looks at that toolkit directly, then turns to the applied end of the same spectrum: can any of this be deliberately induced in a mammal, on purpose, for a given injury? The two questions are connected, because every translational attempt at inducing mammalian regeneration is, in effect, a test of whether the mechanistic story built in Days 2–5 is complete enough to reproduce the effect outside its native species. Three readings today: a practical review of the genetic-engineering toolkit now available in axolotls, a primer on using a mammal that already regenerates somewhat (the spiny mouse) as a natural experimental system, and a primary study that tried to induce regeneration in an adult mammalian nervous system by resetting cell age rather than reactivating a developmental program.

This is also a good point to be honest about a limitation of the whole week: most of the readings so far have been reviews and well-replicated primary findings. Today's third reading is a single, highly-publicized primary study whose translational promise is still unproven in humans — a useful case for calibrating how much weight a striking mouse result should carry.

## Today's readings (3 items, ~21 min total)

**The Genetic Odyssey of Axolotl Regeneration: Insights and Innovations** (Faisal, Mehreen, Hays, Yaseen & Liang, *International Journal of Developmental Biology*, 2024) — https://ijdb.ehu.eus/article/240111yl — ~8 min (abstract, and the sections on genome engineering and epigenetic regulation). A peer-reviewed, fully open-access review from the Ocean University of China, published in the University of the Basque Country's *International Journal of Developmental Biology*. It surveys the concrete tools that turned axolotl regeneration from descriptive natural history into causal biology: CRISPR–Cas9 knock-in/knockout to test single genes directly, electroporation-based overexpression or knockdown for faster (if less permanent) manipulation, and epigenomic profiling (DNA methylation, histone marks, miRNA) layered on top of the transcriptomic work described on Day 3.

**Model systems for regeneration: the spiny mouse, *Acomys cahirinus*** (Maden & Varholick, *Development*, 2020) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7055364/ — ~7 min (introduction and the sections on skin, muscle and ear-pinna regeneration). A peer-reviewed Primer, freely available via PMC, from the University of Florida's Department of Biology. Rather than another non-mammalian regenerator, it makes the case for a rodent that already does some of what mammals supposedly can't: scar-free skin healing, full-thickness ear-hole closure, and some muscle regeneration — making it a natural-experiment model for finding out what, specifically, differs between a "regenerating" and a "scarring" mammal, without crossing out of the mammalian lineage at all.

**Reprogramming to recover youthful epigenetic information and restore vision** (Lu, Brommer, Tian, Krishnan, Meer, Wang, Vera, Zeng, Yu, Bonkowski, Yang, Zhou, Hoffmann, Karg, Schultz, Kane, Davidsohn, Korzick, Hogan, Zhou, Rajman, Church, Hochedlinger, Gladyshev, Horvath, Levine, Davidsohn, Yin, Alt & Sinclair, *Nature*, 2020) — https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-020-2975-4 — ~6 min (abstract and the results on axon regeneration and glaucoma; this is a subscription-access Nature research article, so the full text could not be confirmed as freely readable — treat as a report of findings rather than a text to read end-to-end). A peer-reviewed primary study from David Sinclair's lab at Harvard Medical School. Using AAV vectors to deliver three of the four "Yamanaka factors" (Oct4, Sox2, Klf4 — c-Myc was dropped on tumor-risk grounds) to retinal ganglion cells in mouse eyes, the authors report that partial reprogramming restored youthful DNA-methylation patterns, roughly doubled survival of injured neurons, produced about a fivefold increase in axon regrowth after optic-nerve injury, and partially reversed vision loss in both a glaucoma model and in naturally aged mice.

## Guided summary (~8 min)

Faisal et al.'s review is useful mainly as an inventory of what separates "we observed X" from "we showed X is required/sufficient." A transcriptomic finding like the ones in Day 3's readings — gene Y is upregulated in blastema cells — is only a correlation; CRISPR knockout of Y, watched for a failure to regenerate, tests necessity; electroporating extra copies of Y into a wound, watched for an induced response, tests sufficiency. The review notes real practical limits: axolotl genome editing is slow (months per generation, a large and repeat-rich genome that complicates guide design), and the electroporation shortcuts are typically transient and local rather than stable and heritable, so most causal claims in this field rest on a patchwork of both approaches rather than one clean method. This matters for how to read Day 5's macrophage-depletion result: clodronate-liposome depletion is a chemical, reversible, systemic intervention — closer to a pharmacological knockdown than a genetic one — which is part of why it could be done at all in an organism where germline knockouts take so long.

Maden and Varholick's spiny-mouse primer approaches the same necessity/sufficiency problem from a different angle: instead of manipulating genes in a non-mammal and asking why mammals in general can't do the same, it asks why *this* mammal can do a reduced version of what mammals supposedly can't. Acomys closes a full-thickness ear punch without scarring, in a way laboratory mice (like the C57BL6 strains used in most Western biomedical research) do not, and shows some capacity for scar-free skin wound healing and muscle regeneration. Because Acomys is phylogenetically close to standard lab mice, this sets up a genuinely comparative experiment: breed the regenerating and non-regenerating mammals together (or compare their immune and fibrotic responses tissue-by-tissue) and look for the specific variable that tracks with outcome, rather than contrasting a salamander against a mouse across hundreds of millions of years of divergence. The MRL mouse strain, encountered only in passing on Day 5, is the laboratory-mouse version of the same logic: it heals ear-punch wounds with unusually little scarring compared with most inbred strains, and a 2005 forward-genetics study in *Mammalian Genome* (Yu et al.) crossed MRL mice with poor-healing CAST mice and used quantitative trait locus (QTL) mapping — scoring hundreds of offspring for healing outcome and tracing the trait back to specific chromosomal regions — to locate candidate loci on chromosomes 4, 9 and 17, without presupposing in advance which gene or pathway was responsible. That is a third distinct method from the first two: it finds causal regions without first hypothesizing a candidate gene, at the cost of needing large crosses and leaving the actual mechanism to be worked out afterward.

Lu et al.'s study sits at the applied end of this spectrum and is worth reading carefully rather than taking on faith, precisely because it is a single high-profile result rather than a converging literature. It does not reactivate a developmental regeneration program the way Wnt signaling does at the digit tip (Day 4); it instead resets the epigenetic state of mature retinal ganglion cells toward a younger profile using three reprogramming factors, and reports that this alone was enough to roughly double survival after optic-nerve injury and increase regrown axon length roughly fivefold, with measurable, partial reversal of glaucoma-related and age-related vision loss in mice. The mechanistic link the authors propose runs through the DNA demethylases TET1 and TET2 — reprogramming without them did not produce the same regenerative effect in their experiments, which is the closest this study comes to a necessity test within its own system. The obvious cautions: this is one group's result in mice, not yet replicated by independent labs to the same degree as the Day 5 macrophage work; c-Myc was deliberately excluded because the full four-factor cocktail risks tumor formation, so even the "safe" three-factor version is a chemical tightrope between rejuvenation and dysregulated growth; and "partial reprogramming" blurs a distinction this whole week has otherwise tried to keep sharp — it is not clearly either "restoring a regenerative developmental program" (the axolotl/digit-tip framing) or "a wound-healing immune effect" (the Day 5 framing), but a third category: resetting cell age as a lever on regenerative capacity, largely orthogonal to both.

Put together, today's three readings sketch the shape of the field's actual evidentiary pyramid: a non-mammalian model (axolotl) where genetic tools are improving but slow, allowing clean necessity/sufficiency tests one gene at a time; within-mammal comparative models (Acomys, MRL mice) that let forward genetics find candidate loci without first guessing the mechanism; and a small number of attempts to directly induce a regenerative-like outcome in a standard mammalian model, where the translational stakes are highest and the evidence is, correspondingly, the thinnest and least replicated. None of the three methods is sufficient alone — genetic tools in axolotls can show what a gene does in a species that already regenerates, without explaining why mammals lost the response; comparative mammalian models can localize the relevant genomic regions without yet identifying the mechanism; and a single induced-regeneration result in mice, however striking, is not yet evidence that the same intervention generalizes to other tissues, other injuries, or humans.

## Questions to think about

1. Of the three methods described today — CRISPR knockout/knock-in in axolotls, QTL mapping in mouse crosses, and partial reprogramming in mouse retinal cells — which one gives the strongest evidence for a specific causal mechanism, and which gives the strongest evidence that an intervention actually works? Are those ever the same study?
2. Lu et al.'s result has not yet been independently replicated at the scale of the Day 5 macrophage-depletion finding. What would you want to see — in terms of other labs, other tissues, or other injury models — before treating partial reprogramming as a general strategy for inducing mammalian regeneration, rather than an intriguing single result?
3. Acomys and MRL mice both show that "mammals can't regenerate" is not strictly true — some mammals do it better than others, under genetic control. If QTL mapping in MRL x CAST crosses eventually pins down the responsible genes, what would have to be true of those genes' function in standard laboratory mice and humans for that discovery to translate into a therapy, as opposed to remaining a mouse-specific curiosity?
