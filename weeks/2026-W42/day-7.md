# The regeneration paradox — Day 7/7: Synthesis — what's settled, what's still open, and where to go next

## Context

This week started from a simple observation — salamanders regrow limbs, humans mostly don't — and spent six days replacing the obvious wrong explanation ("mammals lack the genes") with a more precise and more interesting one. Day 1 established the comparative baseline: regeneration is ancestral and widespread, and the blastema is the structure that recurs wherever epimorphic regeneration happens. Days 2 and 3 went inside that structure, from the signaling pathways that drive it (Wnt/FGF/BMP, Hand2–Shh) to the single-cell evidence that it is a *constrained* reprogramming event, not a return to pluripotency. Day 4 showed that mammals are not regeneration-null — the digit tip and the liver prove two different, narrower kinds of mammalian regenerative capacity already exist — and Day 5 argued that the immune system, specifically the resolution dynamics of the early macrophage response, is a strong causal candidate for why most mammalian injuries default to fibrosis instead. Day 6 surveyed how all of these claims are actually tested (knockouts, comparative mammalian models, reprogramming) and how far translational attempts have gotten.

Today's job is different from the other six: not to add a new sub-topic, but to pull the week's threads into one picture, be explicit about what is now well-supported versus still contested, and point toward where the field is moving next. Fittingly, this week's "going deeper" reading surfaces a 2026 finding — too recent to have existed when this theme was chosen — that reframes several of the week's open questions around a single, testable variable: oxygen.

## Today's readings (2 items, ~15 min total)

**Species-specific oxygen sensing governs the initiation of vertebrate limb regeneration** (Tsissios et al., *Science*, April 2026; DOI: 10.1126/science.adw8526) — ~8 min (the finding, via the paper's own abstract as indexed by EBSCO, and consistent, independently reported summaries from the Max Planck Society and EPFL, co-institutions of senior author Can Aztekin's lab at the Friedrich Miescher Laboratory). *Note on sourcing*: this week's usual practice has been to read each paper directly; today, a tooling outage on this research session prevented fetching the primary text or the institutions' own press pages directly, so this summary relies on the paper's indexed abstract and on multiple, mutually consistent institutional accounts rather than a direct read — flagged here rather than silently treated as equivalent to the week's other readings. The study compares amputated mouse-embryo and *Xenopus* tadpole limbs cultured outside the body under different oxygen levels, and finds that lowering oxygen, or directly stabilizing the oxygen-sensing transcription factor HIF1A, pushes mouse limb cells toward a regeneration-favoring state (faster wound closure, altered cell mechanics and metabolism); tadpole cells stay in that state even at high oxygen because they express less of the machinery that lets HIF1A be degraded when oxygen is plentiful. It is a strong direct link between a single, manipulable variable and the gap between regeneration-competent and regeneration-incompetent species — with the important caveat, which the coverage is consistent about, that this activates regeneration-associated programs in cultured embryonic tissue, not full limb regrowth in an adult mammal.

**Acute multidrug delivery via a wearable bioreactor facilitates long-term limb regeneration and functional recovery in adult *Xenopus laevis*** (Murugan, Kaplan, Levin et al., *Science Advances*, 2022) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8791464/ — ~7 min (abstract and results, via the paper's PMC record; this week's usual direct-fetch verification was unavailable today for the same tooling reason noted above, so this description also rests on the indexed PMC record and consistent independent reporting, including from the AAAS). A peer-reviewed, open-access primary study from Tufts and Harvard that is this week's clearest demonstration of deliberately overriding the fibrosis default discussed on Day 5: a silicone "BioDome" cap delivered a five-drug cocktail — targeting inflammation, scar-blocking collagen deposition, and pro-growth signaling simultaneously — to amputated adult frog hindlimbs for just 24 hours, after which treated frogs regrew functionally useful, if imperfect, legs over 18 months, with RNA sequencing showing re-activation of the same developmental pathways (Wnt/β-catenin, hedgehog) this week's Day 2 and Day 4 readings described as the core regeneration toolkit.

## Guided summary (~9 min)

**What is now well-supported.** Three claims from this week rest on strong, partly convergent, partly independently replicated evidence rather than a single striking result. First, the molecular toolkit for regeneration — Wnt, FGF, BMP, Shh, and the transcription factors that read and write positional identity (Hand2, Prod1-responsive pathways) — is conserved across species that differ enormously in whether they can finish the job, which is why Day 2 and Day 4 both found the same signaling logic operating in axolotl limbs and in a mouse digit tip. Second, the blastema is not a hidden, pre-existing stem-cell reservoir; it is ordinary differentiated cells transiently and reversibly reprogrammed, and this is now supported by two independent single-cell datasets (Day 3) layered on top of fifteen-year-old physical lineage-tracing (Kragl et al.). Third, macrophages are causally necessary for axolotl limb regeneration, not merely associated with it — Godwin et al.'s depletion-and-recovery experiment (Day 5) is about as direct as a necessity claim gets in this field, and it is hard to square with the old assumption that inflammation is simply regeneration's enemy.

**What is still genuinely contested.** The central "why" questions are not settled, and this week was honest about that at each turn. Why mammalian cells so rarely enter the dedifferentiated, blastema-like state at all is still open — today's oxygen-sensing finding offers a candidate mechanism (species differ in how aggressively they shut off a HIF1A-driven permissive state once oxygen is available) that is new enough to not yet have been stress-tested against the macrophage/inflammation story from Day 5; the two could be complementary layers of the same problem (a cell has to both sense a permissive local environment and receive the right immune signal) or competitors for which one is doing the real causal work. Separately, Day 5 flagged that the limb-regeneration-loss literature (Zaraisky et al.'s four innovations: immunity, keratinized skin, endothermy, body size) and the CNS-regeneration-loss literature (Blackshaw's infection-resistance hypothesis) do not point to the same mechanism, even though both ultimately implicate the immune system — there is currently no single accepted account of why regenerative capacity was lost, only several partially compatible, tissue-specific stories.

**Big open questions, carried forward from the week:**

1. Is the barrier to mammalian limb regeneration best described as a *triggering* problem (the right signal never switches on, as in Takeo et al.'s digit-tip work), a *sustaining* problem (the signal switches on but fades too fast), or an *environmental* problem (oxygen, immune resolution) that operates upstream of both — and can one experiment distinguish all three at once?
2. Does today's oxygen-sensing mechanism interact with, or compete against, the macrophage-dependent mechanism from Day 5? A direct test — manipulating HIF1A stability in a macrophage-depleted axolotl, or vice versa — would be informative and, as far as this week's reading found, has not yet been done.
3. The digit tip, the liver, and the Murugan frog work all achieve some version of "regeneration" by different routes (positional blastema, compensatory hyperplasia, forced multi-pathway activation). Is there one unifying definition of regeneration that should cover all three, or does insisting on one obscure real mechanistic differences that matter for therapy design?
4. Translational work (Day 6's partial reprogramming, today's bioreactor and oxygen studies) is accumulating fast, but almost entirely in single, not-yet-independently-replicated studies. What would it take — in terms of other labs, other species, other injury types — for any one of these approaches to move from "striking result" to "established mechanism," the way the macrophage-necessity finding already has?
5. If regenerative capacity really was lost repeatedly and for different reasons in different mammalian tissues (limb versus CNS, per Day 5), should regenerative medicine expect one general strategy to work across tissues, or should it plan for genuinely tissue-specific solutions from the start?

## Questions to think about

1. Of everything covered this week, which single finding would most change your mind about whether human limb regeneration is a realistic medium-term medical goal — and what result, if it came out next, would change your mind the other way?
2. This week relied heavily on axolotls, zebrafish, and frogs as model systems, with mice and humans entering mainly through narrow exceptions (digit tip, liver). What is lost, scientifically, by learning about human biology mostly through animals that are evolutionarily quite distant from us?
3. Day 5 and today's reading both suggest regeneration loss may have multiple, tissue-specific causes rather than one unifying explanation. If that is correct, does it change how you'd prioritize research funding across limb, organ, and CNS regeneration — toward one shared mechanism, or toward several parallel efforts?
