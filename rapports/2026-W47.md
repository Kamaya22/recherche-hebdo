# Source diversity — The economics and sociology of remote work (2026-W47)

## At a glance

This week's seven readings cited **42 total citations** (21 formal "today's readings" items plus 21 sources mentioned in the body text), resolving to **20 distinct venues**, all of them already present in or newly added to `registry.csv` — no off-registry venues this week. Open-access status breaks down as **28 open / 9 paywall / 5 unknown** (66.7% confirmed open), close to but slightly under the all-time project average of 68.1% reported in `STATS.md`. The single most notable pattern this week is on the peer-review axis rather than the open-access one: **38.1% of citations (16/42) were not peer reviewed** — more than double the all-time project average of 16.5% — a direct consequence of economics research's heavy reliance on working papers (NBER, SSRN) and institutional reports (CRS, BLS, OECD, Federal Reserve) that circulate and get cited well before, or sometimes instead of, formal journal publication.

## Publisher & venue concentration

No single publisher crosses the project's 50% alert threshold, but Springer Nature is this week's largest publisher at 12/42 citations (28.6%) — driven by *Nature* (3), *Nature Cities* (3), *Nature Human Behaviour* (2), *Journal of Economic Inequality* (2), and *Social Indicators Research* (2). That is a notably different concentration pattern than a typical biomedical or ecology week: none of those five Springer Nature venues are the same journal, so the publisher-level concentration is real but not a single-journal echo chamber. The second-largest publisher, the American Economic Association (7/42, 16.7%, via the *Journal of Economic Perspectives* and the *American Economic Review*), reflects this week's reliance on the two flagship outlets of mainstream US economics for its most load-bearing claims (Day 1's and Day 6's JEP reviews; Day 4's and today's AER primary studies) — a reasonable choice for authority and readability, but worth naming as a recurring dependency across the week rather than a one-off.

## Evidence mix

The week was dominated by primary studies (26/42, 61.9%) with a solid review layer underneath (10/42, 23.8%, concentrated in Day 1, Day 3, and Day 6's methods-focused readings) and only a single explicit randomized controlled trial (Day 2's Trip.com study, re-cited on Day 6) — expected for a field where true firm-level RCTs remain rare and difference-in-differences natural experiments do most of the causal work instead. Exactly one citation was formally tagged `preprint` (a Day 6 re-mention of Day 5's SSRN working paper), even though a further 15 citations carry `peer_reviewed: false` without the `preprint` tag — these are mostly institutional working papers (NBER, SSRN) and government/central-bank reports (CRS, BLS, Federal Reserve, OECD) that are better described as "not independently peer reviewed" than as unreviewed preprints, but the inconsistent tagging across days 1–7 (some working papers tagged `primary-study`, one tagged `preprint`) is itself a data-quality note worth flagging below. No meta-analysis or umbrella-review was available or used, consistent with a topic whose causal evidence base (Day 6) is still too young and too heterogeneous in design for one to exist.

## Open access & geography

Open-access share (66.7%) sits close to the all-time baseline, helped by this week's heavy use of institutional and government sources that publish openly by default (OECD, BLS, CRS, Federal Reserve/Liberty Street Economics, NBER, SIEPR) — exactly the "open access rescues an otherwise paywall-heavy field" pattern that mainstream economics journals, several of which remain paywalled (*Journal of Political Economy Microeconomics*, *Social Indicators Research*, *Journal of Econometrics*, and the published *American Economic Review* versions), would not produce on their own. Geographically, publisher country concentrates heavily in the **United States (24/42, 57.1%)** — just under the project's 60% alert threshold, so not flagged, but close enough to name directly, reflecting how much of the English-language empirical literature on remote work is produced by US-based economics departments, government agencies, and the Federal Reserve system. The United Kingdom follows at 8/42 (19.0%, almost entirely Springer Nature's UK registration rather than UK-based research), and **6/42 citations (14.3%) carry a `to-verify` country** in the registry (`journal-of-economic-inequality`, `social-indicators-research`, `review-of-income-and-wealth`) — see Blind spots below.

## Discipline & recency

Discipline spread is, unsurprisingly for this theme, dominated by economics (28/42, 66.7%), with multidisciplinary generalist venues (*Nature*, *Nature Human Behaviour*, *PNAS*) at 16.7%, urban-studies at 7.1% (*Nature Cities*), and sociology at 4.8% (*Social Indicators Research*). One discipline tag is worth flagging as likely stale rather than wrong for this week specifically: `congressional-research-service` carries `discipline: international-law` in the registry (set from an earlier, unrelated CRS report on treaties), which does not describe today's economic-development report — see Recommendations. Recency is strongly weighted toward current research: years range from 2010 (Angrist & Pischke's deliberately foundational methods essay, Day 6) and 2020 (Day 3's earlier UK stigma study) through a dense cluster in 2023–2026 (36 of 42 citations, 85.7%), appropriate for a fast-moving, still-accumulating empirical literature where even "recent" findings (Day 4's AER article, today's AER and Liberty Street Economics readings) are dated 2026.

## Blind spots & data quality

No off-registry venues and no new `to-verify` entries were introduced this week; the three pre-existing `to-verify` venues (noted above) account for 6/42 citations (14.3%) and remain Kamil's call to resolve in `registry.csv`. Two smaller data-quality notes from this week specifically: first, the `congressional-research-service` registry row's `discipline` field (`international-law`) does not match this week's economic-development usage of that venue — a sign the registry's one-row-per-venue model strains when an institution (unlike a single-topic journal) publishes across very different fields, worth a `notes` update rather than a new row. Second, `evidence_type` tagging for non-peer-reviewed working papers was inconsistent across the week — most (NBER, SSRN, BLS, OECD, CRS, Liberty Street Economics) were tagged `primary-study` or `review`, while one SSRN re-mention on Day 6 was separately tagged `preprint` — both are defensible readings of the taxonomy, but a single convention (e.g., reserve `preprint` strictly for arXiv/SSRN/bioRxiv-style unreviewed manuscripts, regardless of institution) would make the `STATS.md` preprint count more interpretable over time. One source family drew zero citations all week: no `megajournal`-type general-science preprint server beyond SSRN/NBER was used, and no `news-perspective` section of a major journal (Nature News, Science News) appeared — not a gap exactly, since this week's topic is squarely economics/sociology rather than natural science, but worth noting for balance.

## Recommendations

1. **Resolve the three long-standing `to-verify` venues this week relied on** (`journal-of-economic-inequality`, `social-indicators-research`, `review-of-income-and-wealth`) — all three are Springer Nature or Wiley journals whose publisher HQ and discipline should be straightforward to confirm, and resolving them would remove 6/42 of this week's citations from the "unresolved" bucket.
2. **Add a `notes` clarification to the `congressional-research-service` registry row** noting that CRS reports span many disciplines (this week: economics; the row's current `discipline: international-law` reflects an earlier, unrelated citation), rather than treating the venue as single-discipline.
3. **Adopt a stricter, written convention for `evidence_type: preprint`** (reserve it for genuinely unreviewed manuscripts on preprint servers, regardless of host institution) to make the inconsistency this week surfaced (one working paper tagged `preprint`, several structurally similar ones tagged `primary-study`) less likely to recur.
4. **For next week's non-economics theme, lean back toward peer-reviewed journal primary sources where available**, since this week's 38.1% non-peer-reviewed share — more than double the all-time average — is a reasonable feature of economics (where working papers and institutional reports are a normal, often-cited part of the literature) but should not become the project's new baseline by default.
5. **Where a US-based institutional source (Federal Reserve, BLS, CRS, NBER) is the most convenient citation, actively look for a non-US counterpart on the same question** (OECD and the EU already served this role well this week for the inequality angle) to keep the publisher-country share comfortably under the 60% alert threshold rather than drifting toward it, as this week's 57.1% US share did.
