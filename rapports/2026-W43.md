# Source diversity — Vaccine hesitancy and trust in science (2026-W43)

## At a glance

Seven readings this week cited **35 sources** across **14 distinct venues** and **12 distinct publishers**. Open-access share was **65.7% (23/35)**, with only 1 citation confirmed paywalled and a notably high 11 citations (31.4%) of unconfirmed access status — well above this project's all-time baseline of 17.8% unknown (per `STATS.md`, last generated 2026-10-24, which itself lags one reading behind this push). The single most notable finding: **PLOS ONE (`plos-one`) is this week's anchor venue, cited 8 times (22.9% of all citations)** — spanning five different, independently chosen papers from five different days (Betsch et al. 2018's 5C scale, Jiménez et al. 2020's omission-bias test, Mønsted & Lehmann 2022's polarization study, the 2017 Pluviano backfire study, and Ecker et al. 2023's backfire replication) — which reflects how much of the open, peer-reviewed primary literature on vaccine psychology specifically funnels through one multidisciplinary megajournal, rather than any single choice this week made.

## Publisher & venue concentration

No publisher approaches this project's 50% concentration-alert threshold. PLOS (via PLOS ONE) leads at 8/35 (22.9%), followed by Springer Nature as a family — split across its two registry entries, "Springer Nature" proper (Journal of Population Economics, Scientific Reports: 4/35, 11.4%) and "Springer Nature (BioMed Central)" (BMC Public Health: 3/35, 8.6%) — MDPI (Vaccines, three separate papers: 5/35, 14.3%), and SAGE Publishing (Perspectives on Psychological Science, Journal of Health Psychology: 3/35, 8.6%). The rest — Elsevier, Frontiers, the American Psychological Association, JMIR Publications, Oxford University Press, the World Health Organization, and the American Academy of Pediatrics — each contribute one or two citations. At the venue level, the week is well spread: no venue besides PLOS ONE was cited more than three times (Vaccines, three distinct papers), and genuinely new venues were still being added on Day 7 (Public Health Reviews), suggesting the week did not settle into a narrow citation habit as it went on.

## Evidence mix

- **Primary studies**: 18/35 (51.4%) — the majority, reflecting that this theme is built on original surveys, qualitative interviews, computational/network analyses, and quasi-experiments (the SB277 natural experiment, the Tuskegee-distance study) rather than clinical trials.
- **Reviews**: 12/35 (34.3%) — including two systematic/scoping reviews read for the first time on Day 7 specifically to broaden the week's geographic and temporal scope.
- **Meta-analyses**: 2/35 (5.7%) — Labbé et al.'s pooled MI-vs-education trial synthesis (Day 5, cited again Day 7).
- **Guidelines**: 2/35 (5.7%) — both from the WHO (the BeSD toolkit), the only non-peer-reviewed sources cited all week.
- **Perspectives**: 1/35 (2.9%) — the Frontiers in Public Health measles-outbreak piece (Day 1).
- **RCTs, cohort studies, umbrella reviews, preprints, books, to-verify**: 0/35.

Zero preprints is a clean result for this theme — nothing cited this week was unreviewed, and the two non-peer-reviewed items (both WHO guidelines) are transparently institutional rather than disguised as research. The complete absence of a directly-cited RCT is worth naming explicitly, though: intervention efficacy (Day 5, Day 7) is a question the literature studies mainly through RCTs, but every RCT-based finding this week arrived pre-digested through a meta-analysis or systematic review (Labbé et al.'s 55 pooled trials; Pennisi et al.'s 59 studies) rather than through a single trial read directly — a reasonable editorial choice for a week already citing two intervention syntheses, but it means this week's reader never saw what one individual vaccination-uptake RCT actually looks like.

## Open access & geography

The 31.4% unknown-access share is this week's clearest data-quality flag, and it is concentrated in a specific, explainable pattern: five of the six "unknown" venues (Vaccine/Elsevier, Journal of Population Economics, Translational Behavioral Medicine, Perspectives on Psychological Science, Journal of Health Psychology, Pediatrics) are hybrid open-access journals where `registry.csv`'s own notes already flag that the specific article's access status was never confirmed, rather than simply unlabelled. None of this week's readings were dropped or miscited over it — it is a verification gap, not a sourcing gap — but it means the week's true open-access share could plausibly sit anywhere between 65.7% and roughly 97% depending on how those six resolve. Geographically, publisher-HQ countries split across the United States (13/35, 37.1%: PLOS, APA, SAGE, AAP), Switzerland (9/35, 25.7%: MDPI, WHO, Frontiers), the United Kingdom (9/35, 25.7%: Springer Nature in both its registry forms, Oxford University Press), the Netherlands (3/35, 8.6%: Elsevier), and Canada (1/35, 2.9%: JMIR). No country crosses this project's 60% concentration threshold, and the Swiss share in particular — unusually high for this project — reflects that the WHO (Geneva), MDPI (Basel), and Frontiers (Lausanne) all happen to be genuinely major players in global-health publishing specifically, not an artifact of this week's search choices.

## Discipline & recency

By registry discipline, "public-health" dominates at 17/35 (48.6%), which is expected and appropriate for the theme; "multidisciplinary" megajournals (PLOS ONE, Scientific Reports) are the second-largest bucket at 10/35 (28.6%) — again a labelling artifact, since every one of those citations is substantively about vaccination behavior. Psychology (4/35, 11.4%), economics (2/35, 5.7%), digital-health (1/35), and pediatrics (1/35) round out the rest. Recency spans a genuinely wide range for a single week: from a 2007 *Vaccine*-journal taxonomy of anti-vaccine reasoning flaws (cited in text, Day 2) and a 2011 qualitative study (Bond & Nolan, Day 2, revisited Day 7) through to a 2026 systematic review read on the final day — 25 of 35 citations (71.4%) are from 2020 or later, so the week leans recent without losing its two oldest, foundational sources along the way.

## Blind spots & data quality

- No venue cited this week carries a `to-verify` flag in `registry.csv`'s discipline or country fields, and none appear in `STATS.md`'s current alerts list — a clean result relative to the project's all-time average (12 citations, 3.7%, carry a `to-verify` country project-wide).
- One pre-existing registry inconsistency, unrelated to this week's additions but surfaced while cross-checking sources: `registry.csv` currently has **two separate rows for the same `id`, `jmir`** (Journal of Medical Internet Research), with different `discipline` values (`digital-health` vs. `health-informatics`) and near-duplicate notes. This does not corrupt this week's citation counts (only one `jmir` row's discipline value is picked up by whichever row the build script reads first), but it is worth collapsing into a single row before it causes a silent discipline-count discrepancy in `STATS.md`.
- The source families explicitly allowed by this project's rules but not used this week are **preprints** (unremarkable to skip here — see Evidence mix above) and **outreach** (Quanta, Knowable, The Conversation) — no lay-researcher-written explainer was cited in any of the seven readings, a recurring gap also flagged in last week's report (2026-W42).
- Methodological note specific to today: WebFetch (direct page retrieval) was unavailable for this entire research session (consistent DNS-level failures across multiple unrelated hosts, confirmed via the environment's own proxy-status check). Day 7's two new sources (Pennisi et al. 2026 and Stuetzle et al. 2025) were therefore verified through WebSearch-returned abstracts and bibliographic details (title, authors, journal, DOI, year, study counts) corroborated across multiple independent search results, rather than by reading the fetched source page directly, as this project's norm otherwise is. This is disclosed here and in `weeks/2026-W43/day-7.md` itself; both sources' core bibliographic facts (DOI, venue, study count, PROSPERO registration number for Pennisi et al.) were independently confirmed across more than one search result before being cited, but a direct read of the full text has not yet happened on this project's side.

## Recommendations

1. **Collapse the duplicate `jmir` row in `registry.csv`** into one, keeping one `discipline` value (either `digital-health` or `health-informatics`) and merging the notes — a quick fix that removes a latent source of silent miscounting.
2. **Re-verify Day 7's two new sources with a direct fetch** once WebFetch is working again, the same follow-up this project did for last week's (2026-W42) Day 7 sources after an identical tooling outage.
3. **Resolve the six `open_access: unknown` entries from this week** (Vaccine, Journal of Population Economics, Translational Behavioral Medicine, Perspectives on Psychological Science, Journal of Health Psychology, Pediatrics) by checking the specific cited articles directly — all six are hybrid-OA journals where the registry already flags the uncertainty, so this is a bounded, finishable task rather than an open-ended one.
4. **Include one outreach/explainer-tier source in a future week** (Quanta, Knowable, The Conversation) — two project reports in a row (2026-W42, 2026-W43) have now flagged this family as unused, which is starting to look like a systematic gap rather than a one-off.
5. **Deliberately cite one individual RCT directly in a future intervention-heavy week**, rather than only through pooled meta-analyses — useful for giving readers a feel for what the underlying primary evidence actually looks like, not just its pooled summary.
