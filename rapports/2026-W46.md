# Source diversity — Bird migration navigation (2026-W46)

## At a glance

This week's seven readings cited **35 total citations** (21 formal "today's readings" items plus 14 sources mentioned in the body text), drawing on **23 distinct venues in `registry.csv`** (no off-registry venues — every citation resolved to an existing or newly-added registry row). Open-access status breaks down as **11 open / 11 paywall / 13 unknown** — i.e. only 31% of citations are confirmed open access, with over a third (37%) of unresolved status, well below the all-time project average of 69.2% open access reported in `STATS.md`. The single most notable pattern: **Nature (the journal) alone accounts for 8 of 35 citations (23%)**, and Springer Nature as a publisher accounts for 11 of 35 (31%) — both above the flag thresholds this project tracks (publisher > 50% is the hard alert threshold, so this week stays under it, but it is the highest single-publisher share of any week so far by a wide margin for a non-biomedical theme).

## Publisher & venue concentration

Springer Nature is this week's dominant publisher at 11/35 citations (31%), driven almost entirely by papers published in *Nature* itself (8 citations: Day 2's cryptochrome and beak-macrophage papers, Day 2's liver-macrophage *Science* companion note aside, Day 5's Mouritsen review, Day 6's electromagnetic-noise paper, and two re-mentions in today's synthesis) plus one *Journal of Comparative Physiology A* review added today. The second-largest publisher, The Company of Biologists (4 citations, all *Journal of Experimental Biology*), is a specialist, non-profit, UK-based publisher — a healthier kind of concentration than a single for-profit conglomerate, but still a repeat venue. No single publisher crosses the 50% alert threshold, but the reliance on *Nature* specifically is worth naming directly: a field whose most-cited single venue is a generalist, high-prestige journal risks over-indexing on whichever findings that journal's editors chose to feature (the liver-macrophage and cryptochrome stories both got *Nature*/*Science* placement partly because they are striking results, not necessarily because they are the most representative ones) rather than the broader specialist ornithology literature.

## Evidence mix

The week leaned heavily on primary research: 21 primary-studies versus 9 reviews and 5 perspectives, with **zero preprints, zero RCTs, and zero meta-analyses** (expected for this field — behavioral/sensory ecology does not generate RCTs or meta-analyses the way clinical research does, and no unreviewed preprint was judged necessary or available at adequate quality). The review share (9/35, 26%) is reasonable for a synthesis-heavy week, concentrated in exactly the places a reader would want signposting: Day 1's Karwinkel et al. review, Day 3's two calibration reviews, Day 5's Pakhomov & Chernetsov and Mouritsen reviews, and today's Granger et al. review. One gap: no meta-analysis or systematic quantitative synthesis was available for the central magnetoreception-mechanism question itself (Day 2's cryptochrome-vs-magnetite debate) — understandably, since the field is too young and too split on mechanism for one to exist yet, but it means that debate rested entirely on individual primary studies and narrative reviews rather than any pooled quantitative synthesis.

## Open access & geography

Open-access share this week (31% confirmed open, versus 69.2% all-time) is the week's clearest weak point, and it is largely a *Nature*/*Science* effect: those venues are reliably paywalled or of unconfirmed status for this project's purposes, and they supplied 10 of this week's 35 citations. Geographically, publisher-country data shows **United Kingdom at 20/35 (57%)** — above the project's 60% publisher-country alert threshold's near-neighbor and worth flagging even though it falls just under it. This is almost entirely an artifact of where large scholarly publishers are legally headquartered (Springer Nature, the Company of Biologists, and the Royal Society are all UK-registered) rather than where the underlying research was conducted — this week's actual research institutions span Germany (Oldenburg, Max Planck), the Netherlands, Sweden, Russia, and the United States, a point the registry's country field (publisher HQ, not research origin) does not capture and that future weeks' interpretation should keep in mind.

## Discipline & recency

Discipline spread this week: multidisciplinary 17 (mostly *Nature*/*Science*/PNAS generalist venues), ornithology 7, physiology 5, ecology 4, evolutionary-biology 1, neuroscience 1. The high multidisciplinary share reflects how much of the most cited magnetoreception work gets published in generalist high-impact journals rather than specialist ornithology or sensory-biology outlets — a real feature of this particular sub-field, not obviously a sourcing error, but it does mean "multidisciplinary" is doing a lot of the classification work this week rather than a more informative discipline tag. Recency: citation years ranged from 1958 (Perdeck's original displacement study, cited for historical grounding) to 2026 (this week's own headline liver-macrophage finding), with a genuine cluster in 2011–2024 and only the single 1958 outlier as a deliberately foundational reference — a healthy mix of foundational and current work rather than over-indexing on either end.

## Blind spots & data quality

No `to-verify` entries and no off-registry venues were introduced this week — every citation resolved cleanly against `registry.csv`, including the one new row added today (`journal-of-comparative-physiology-a`). The main data-quality flag is the **13 citations (37%) with `open_access: "unknown"`** — higher than ideal, and a direct consequence of how hard it is to confirm a specific paywalled-journal article's access status without being able to load the publisher's page directly during research (several status checks this week could only be cross-verified through independent secondary sources rather than the landing page itself). One source family this week never drew on: preprint servers (arXiv, bioRxiv) — appropriate for this particular topic, where the central open questions are resolved (or not) through peer-reviewed primary research and institutional review, but worth noting as a category with zero representation across all seven days.

## Recommendations

1. **Diversify away from single-journal reliance on *Nature*.** With 8 of 35 citations from one journal, future weeks on sensory biology or similarly *Nature*-friendly topics should deliberately seek out specialist venues (as Day 3's *Movement Ecology*, *Journal of Ornithology*, and Day 6's *Ecology and Evolution* did) as the first-choice source, reserving *Nature*/*Science* citations for cases where no comparable specialist source exists.
2. **Resolve open-access status more rigorously before writing "unknown."** With 37% of this week's citations unresolved on access status, a dedicated check (e.g., searching for "DOI + open access" or checking Unpaywall-style aggregators via search, since direct publisher fetches were not reliably available this week) before finalizing each day's citations file would meaningfully improve this project's `STATS.md` open-access metric.
3. **Add at least one fully open-access alternative per paywalled "today's readings" pick where one exists.** Several of this week's paywalled primary papers (e.g., Engels et al. 2014, Kishkinev et al. 2015) have no open preprint or institutional mirror confirmed in `registry.csv`'s notes column; checking for institutional-repository or author-site mirrors (as Day 6 did for the Engels paper) should become a standard step, not an occasional one.
4. **Flag publisher-country as "publisher HQ, not research origin" more explicitly in future weeks' sourcing notes**, since this week's 57% UK concentration is a real number but a misleading proxy for where the underlying science happens — most of this week's actual research groups were German, Dutch, Swedish, Russian, or American.
5. **For topics with a strong mechanism debate and no meta-analysis available (as with this week's magnetoreception mechanism question), consider explicitly naming that gap in the reading itself** — as this week's Day 2 reading did implicitly — so readers understand the evidence base rests on individual studies rather than a pooled synthesis, rather than assuming review-level confidence where none yet exists.
