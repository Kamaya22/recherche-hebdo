# Source diversity — Lithium-ion battery science and the energy storage transition (2026-W44)

## At a glance

Seven readings this week cited **29 sources** (20 formal "today's readings" items plus 9 further studies or reports named in the body text) across **19 distinct venues** and **10 distinct publishers**. Open-access share was **93.1% (27/29)** — well above this project's all-time baseline of 70.4% (per `STATS.md`, last generated 2026-10-31, which lags one reading behind this push) — with only 1 citation confirmed paywalled and 1 of unconfirmed access status. The single most notable finding: this is one of the most open-access weeks this project has run, almost entirely because the week's institutional sources (the IEA, USGS) and specialist chemistry/engineering journals (MDPI's *Batteries*, *Energies*, *Recycling*, *Processes*; the Royal Society of Chemistry's *EES Batteries* and *RSC Sustainability*) publish open by default or were read in fully open-access article form — a markedly different access profile from the mixed-paywall biomedical literature this project more often cites.

## Publisher & venue concentration

No publisher crosses this project's 50% concentration-alert threshold, but MDPI is this week's clear anchor: **9/29 citations (31.0%)**, spread across five differently-titled MDPI journals — *International Journal of Molecular Sciences*, *Energies*, *Processes*, *Recycling*, and especially *Batteries*, which alone was cited 4 times (13.8%) across three distinct, independently-chosen papers (Ohneseit et al.'s 2023 calorimetry study, the 2024 Li-vs-Na comparative review, and Das's 2025 second-life perspective). That concentration is more a reflection of the field than of this week's search habits: MDPI's *Batteries* is, alongside the Royal Society of Chemistry's newer *EES Batteries*, one of only a handful of peer-reviewed journals specializing exclusively in battery science, so a week built around battery engineering was always likely to return to it. Behind MDPI, Springer Nature (10.3%: *Nano-Micro Letters*, *Nature Energy*, *Nature Communications* — three different journals, not one dominant title), the Royal Society of Chemistry (10.3%), Elsevier (10.3%), and the International Energy Agency (10.3%, cited as a single institutional source across three different reports: the 2024 *Batteries and Secure Energy Transitions* outlook, the 2025 *Global Critical Minerals Outlook*, and the 2025 *Global EV Outlook*) round out the rest. No single venue besides *Batteries* was cited more than three times.

## Evidence mix

- **Reviews**: 16/29 (55.2%) — the majority, reflecting that this theme is built on synthesizing a fast-moving engineering and materials-science literature (mechanisms, safety, recycling, next-generation chemistries) rather than reporting single new experiments.
- **Primary studies**: 6/29 (20.7%) — Ohneseit et al.'s accelerating-rate-calorimetry safety data (Day 3, cited again Day 7), Mancini et al.'s cobalt-mining field study (Day 4, cited again Day 7), and Lo et al.'s Indonesian nickel-mining counterfactual study (Day 4, cited again Day 7).
- **Perspectives**: 4/29 (13.8%) — the National Science Review editorial (Day 1, revisited Day 7), the Renais et al. graphite-redox perspective (Day 2), and Das's second-life-batteries perspective (Day 7).
- **To-verify**: 2/29 (6.9%) — both USGS mineral-production statistics pages (Day 4), which report hard production numbers but do not fit this project's evidence-type taxonomy cleanly.
- **Guidelines**: 1/29 (3.4%) — the EU's binding Battery Regulation summary (Day 5), the week's one explicitly legal/policy source.
- **RCTs, cohort studies, meta-analyses, umbrella reviews, preprints, books**: 0/29.

Zero preprints is a clean result: nothing cited this week was unreviewed, in line with this project's strict sourcing rule. The complete absence of RCTs and meta-analyses is expected rather than a gap — this is a materials-science, engineering, and industrial-policy theme with essentially no clinical-trial literature to draw on — but it does mean the week leaned almost entirely on either synthesis (reviews) or a small number of standalone empirical studies, with no pooled quantitative synthesis of primary data anywhere in the week.

## Open access & geography

Besides the one confirmed paywalled citation (Xu et al.'s thermal-runaway-mitigation review, *Renewable and Sustainable Energy Reviews*, Day 3) and one of unconfirmed status (Janek & Zeier's solid-state review in *Nature Energy*, Day 6 — a subscription journal by default, with this specific article's access status unconfirmed), every other citation this week was open access, including every institutional report (IEA, USGS, EU) and every specialist battery journal used. Geographically, publisher-HQ countries split across Switzerland (9/29, 31.0%: MDPI's five journals), the United Kingdom (7/29, 24.1%: Oxford University Press via *National Science Review*, the Royal Society of Chemistry's three journals, and Springer Nature's UK-based *Nature Energy* and *Nature Communications*), the United States (5/29, 17.2%: *One Earth*/Cell Press, USGS, the American Chemical Society), France (3/29, 10.3%: the IEA, Paris-headquartered), the Netherlands (3/29, 10.3%: Elsevier), and 2 citations (6.9%) carrying a `to-verify` publisher country inherited from the registry (the EU regulation's distributed-HQ ambiguity, and *Nano-Micro Letters*' Springer/Shanghai University co-publication). No country crosses this project's 60% concentration threshold, though Switzerland's lead here — as in several of this project's chemistry- and health-themed weeks — is a structural feature of MDPI's and the IEA's roles in this literature rather than a narrow search choice.

## Discipline & recency

By registry discipline, chemistry (8/29, 27.6%) and energy (6/29, 20.7%) together account for just under half of all citations (48.3%), appropriately for a week centered on cell chemistry and energy-storage deployment. The rest spreads genuinely widely for a single theme: multidisciplinary megajournal citations (3/29, 10.3%), geology (2/29, 6.9%: the USGS mineral statistics), resource economics (2/29, 6.9%: *Resources Policy*), environmental science (2/29, 6.9%: *One Earth*), waste management (2/29, 6.9%: *Recycling*), and single citations each in molecular biology, engineering, environmental policy, and materials science. Recency skews toward the present: 16/29 citations (55.2%) are from 2024–2025, and the oldest citation is a 2020 graphite-anode review (Day 2) alongside Grey & Hall's 2020 *Nature Communications* outlook (Day 7, chosen specifically to add a longer-horizon, still-relevant forward look) — a reasonable spread for a fast-moving engineering field, though notably the week never cited anything from before 2020, meaning no deep historical or foundational reference point (e.g., the chemistry's original 1970s–1990s development) was ever directly cited, only mentioned in passing in Day 1's framing.

## Blind spots & data quality

- Two pre-existing `to-verify` registry flags were touched again this week rather than resolved: `eu-battery-regulation`'s publisher country (ambiguous across Brussels/Luxembourg/Strasbourg) and `nano-micro-letters`'s publisher country (ambiguous between Springer and its Shanghai University co-publisher) — both already surfaced in `STATS.md`'s alerts and unchanged by this week's readings, since this project does not edit other days' classifications.
- Both USGS citations (Day 4) carry an `evidence_type` of `to-verify`, since government mineral-production statistics do not map cleanly onto this project's taxonomy (closest to a `primary-study` or a `guideline`-adjacent institutional dataset, but neither fits precisely) — worth a taxonomy discussion rather than a one-off fix.
- The source family explicitly allowed by this project's rules but not used this week is **outreach** (Quanta Magazine, Knowable Magazine, The Conversation) — no lay-researcher-written explainer was cited in any of the seven readings, the same gap flagged in the two preceding weeks' reports (2026-W42, 2026-W43), which is starting to look systematic rather than incidental.
- No first-hand worker or community perspective beyond Mancini et al.'s field-data study on artisanal cobalt mining (Day 4) was cited — the human cost of mineral extraction was covered through quantitative field research and counterfactual analysis, but never through qualitative, first-person-adjacent reporting from a peer-reviewed or institutional source meeting this project's bar.
- Methodological note specific to today: WebFetch (direct page retrieval) was unavailable for this entire research session — every external host attempted returned a DNS-resolution or proxy-connection failure, confirmed via the environment's own proxy-status check, with only the GitHub git-proxy and the WebSearch backend reachable. Day 7's two new sources (Grey & Hall 2020 and Das 2025) were therefore verified through WebSearch-returned bibliographic details (title, authors, journal, DOI, volume/article number, year) cross-checked across multiple independent search results (PMC/NCBI, DOAJ, the University of Cambridge repository, and the Faraday Institution for the Grey & Hall paper; DOAJ and MDPI's own indexing for the Das paper) rather than by reading the fetched source page directly. This matches the approach disclosed in last week's report (2026-W43) during an identical outage, and is also disclosed in `weeks/2026-W44/day-7.md` itself.

## Recommendations

1. **Diversify away from MDPI for next battery- or materials-science-adjacent theme**, by deliberately seeking out ACS, Elsevier (*Journal of Power Sources*, *Joule*), or Wiley venues for at least one reading — MDPI's 31.0% share this week is below the alert threshold but is this project's highest single-publisher concentration in several weeks, and a specialized field returning to the same open-access publisher repeatedly is worth actively counterbalancing rather than just monitoring.
2. **Include one outreach-tier source** (Quanta, Knowable, The Conversation) in an upcoming week — three consecutive project reports (2026-W42, 2026-W43, 2026-W44) have now flagged this family as entirely unused, which should be treated as a standing gap to close rather than a recurring footnote.
3. **Resolve the two long-standing `to-verify` registry rows** (`eu-battery-regulation`, `nano-micro-letters` publisher country) the next time either venue is cited, or independently, since both have now appeared in at least two consecutive weekly reports' alerts.
4. **Decide a standard `evidence_type` for institutional statistical datasets** (USGS-style production figures, and similarly-shaped data from bodies like the IEA or Eurostat) rather than defaulting to `to-verify` each time — a small codebook addition to `data/README.md` would remove a recurring, predictable ambiguity.
5. **Re-verify Day 7's two new sources with a direct fetch** once WebFetch is reachable again, the same follow-up this project applied to 2026-W43's Day 7 sources after an identical tooling outage.
6. **Seek a qualitative, first-person-adjacent source on mining communities** in a future supply-chain- or extraction-themed week — this week's Mancini et al. and Lo et al. studies are rigorous but both report aggregated field or statistical data, not direct worker or community testimony.
