# Source diversity — Exoplanet atmospheres and the search for biosignatures (2026-W37)

## At a glance

This week's seven readings cited **22 sources** across **13 distinct venues** and **9 distinct publishers**. **72.7%** of citations were open access, **77.3%** were peer-reviewed, and no single publisher crossed a third of the week's citations. The single most notable finding is a methodological one, not a scientific one: the week closed (Day 7) on two independent 2026 papers that reprocessed the *same* public JWST dataset for K2-18 b and reached opposite conclusions about the planet's most contested candidate biosignature — a live, current illustration of exactly the retrieval-degeneracy and instrumental-noise problems the week spent Days 2, 4 and 5 explaining in the abstract. No preprints were cited this week; every citation is a peer-reviewed article or an official institutional page.

## Publisher & venue concentration

No single publisher dominated. Springer Nature was the largest single publisher at **5 citations (22.7%)** — spread across four different venues (*Nature*, *Nature Astronomy*, *Nature Reviews Physics*, *Living Reviews in Computational Astrophysics*) rather than concentrated in one journal — well under the 50% concentration threshold this project flags. The American Astronomical Society / IOP Publishing partnership was second at **4 citations (18.2%)**, split evenly between *The Astronomical Journal* and *The Astrophysical Journal Letters*. EDP Sciences (publisher of *Astronomy & Astrophysics*) followed at **3 citations (13.6%)** — all three from A&A itself, since it was this week's single most-cited individual journal (used on Days 3, 4 and 7, each time for a different, independently authored paper on the K2-18 b/TOI-270 d controversy). The remaining citations were spread thinly across the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS, 2), NASA (2), SAGE/Mary Ann Liebert (*Astrobiology*, 2), the US National Academies of Sciences, Engineering and Medicine (2), ESA (1), and Oxford University Press for the Royal Astronomical Society's *MNRAS* (1). At the individual-venue level, no journal was used more than three times, and only *Astronomy & Astrophysics* crossed two uses — a healthy spread for a technical, narrowly-scoped theme.

## Evidence mix

The mix leaned heavily toward primary research rather than synthesis, which fits a fast-moving, still-contested topic: **primary-study, 10 citations (45.5%)**; **to-verify, 4 (18.2%)**; **perspective, 3 (13.6%)**; **review, 3 (13.6%)**; **guideline, 2 (9.1%)**. There were **no meta-analyses, umbrella reviews, RCTs, or cohort studies** — unsurprising for observational astronomy, where those study designs do not apply, but worth naming so the absence reads as domain-appropriate rather than a gap. More notably, **zero preprints were cited**, in a field (exoplanet science) where arXiv preprinting is the default first step before journal publication and often precedes the peer-reviewed version by months; every source used this week was either a published, peer-reviewed article or an official institutional page. This was a deliberate choice given this session's inability to directly fetch and verify arXiv pages (see Blind spots below), not evidence that no relevant preprints exist. The four "to-verify" entries are all official space-agency or academies overview/program pages (NASA ×2, ESA ×1, National Academies ×1) that do not cleanly fit the existing evidence-type taxonomy (they are neither primary studies nor commissioned reviews) rather than genuine classification uncertainty about their content.

## Open access & geography

**72.7% of citations (16/22) were open access**, 9.1% (2) sat behind a paywall (*Nature*'s 2021 CoLD-scale piece — though a free preprint copy is noted in Day 6's reading — and *Nature Reviews Physics*'s 2025 review, which has no gold open-access option for its article type), and 18.1% (4) had unconfirmed open-access status (two *Nature Astronomy* papers and one *MNRAS* and one *Astrobiology* article, all subscription journals with hybrid open-access policies whose specific-article status was not confirmed). This is a strong open-access share, well above the 50% warning threshold, and continues a trend of most flagship astronomy venues (AAS journals, A&A, PNAS) having moved to full open access in recent years.

Geographically, publisher headquarters skewed toward two countries: **United States, 12 citations (54.5%)**, and **United Kingdom, 6 citations (27.3%)**, with the remainder in **France, 4 citations (18.2%)** (EDP Sciences and ESA, both Paris-area). No non-Western research infrastructure appears anywhere in this week's citation list. This is narrower than several recent weeks and is worth flagging on its own terms below.

## Discipline & recency

As expected for a single-topic astronomy week, **astronomy accounts for 63.6% of citations (14/22)**, with **multidisciplinary venues (PNAS, *Nature*, National Academies reports) at 22.7% (5)**, **astrobiology at 9.1% (2)**, and **physics at 4.5% (1)**. Recency was high and appropriately weighted toward the frontier of an active dispute: **59.1% of citations (13/22) were published in 2025 or 2026**, including both of Day 7's papers, which are less than four months old at the time of writing. The week also deliberately reached back for grounding context where it mattered: Meadows et al.'s 2018 review of oxygen false positives (Day 4) and the 2021 CoLD-scale paper plus its 2021/2022 National Academies review (Day 6) are foundational documents for the field's current methodological norms, cited precisely because the recent literature keeps referring back to them.

## Blind spots & data quality

Three things are worth flagging for the record. First, the **US/UK/France concentration** noted above is a real narrowing relative to recent weeks (2026-W33 through W36 all drew on a wider country spread); it reflects where JWST-era exoplanet science is currently concentrated (Cambridge, MIT, NASA, ESA, and the AAS/EDP journal ecosystem) rather than a sourcing shortcut, but it means this week's readings under-represent astronomy being done elsewhere (e.g., Latin American, Asian or Southern Hemisphere ground-based observatory work, which matters increasingly for follow-up spectroscopy). Second, the **four to-verify entries** are all institutional web pages (NASA ×2, ESA ×1, National Academies ×1) that would benefit from a dedicated `institutional-overview` evidence-type category rather than sitting in `to-verify` indefinitely — a possible small addition to the taxonomy in `data/README.md` for Kamil to consider. Third, and specific to this week: this session's environment could not directly fetch arXiv or several journal domains to double-check page content beyond what WebSearch's indexed snippets showed, which is why **no preprints were cited** even though the K2-18 b literature is unusually preprint-heavy — every claim in this week's readings was cross-checked against at least two independent search results before being written up, but this is a narrower verification method than directly fetching and reading the source, and it is worth Kamil's awareness for this particular week.

## Recommendations

1. **Widen the geographic base** for upcoming astronomy-adjacent themes (e.g. gravitational-wave astronomy, already in the backlog) by deliberately seeking out ESO, JAXA, or Southern Hemisphere observatory sources alongside the US/UK/France-heavy JWST ecosystem.
2. **Add an `institutional-overview` evidence type** to `data/README.md`'s taxonomy for official agency pages (NASA, ESA, National Academies) used for orientation rather than as primary findings, to reduce the growing `to-verify` backlog these pages create.
3. **Resolve the two open `to-verify` items already sitting in `registry.csv`** from this week and prior weeks (Advances in Atmospheric Sciences, Ambio, Engineering, European Journal of Social Psychology, per `data/STATS.md`'s alerts) when convenient — they are unrelated to this week's theme but have been outstanding across several reports.
4. **Consider one clearly-labeled preprint per contested-topic week** going forward: the K2-18 b literature in particular moves faster on arXiv than in final peer-reviewed form, and a preprint explicitly marked "not yet peer reviewed" would let a future week capture the very latest state of a live dispute without compromising the sourcing rules — provided the reading environment can fetch and verify it directly.
5. **Revisit the paywalled *Nature Reviews Physics* citation** (Day 5) if a suitable open-access review of high-resolution cross-correlation spectroscopy or line-list science becomes available, since it was this week's only citation with no open-access route at all.
