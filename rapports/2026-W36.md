# Source diversity — The psychology of conspiracy beliefs (2026-W36)

## At a glance

This week's seven readings cited **27 sources** across **20 distinct venues**. **20 of 27 citations (74.1%)** are confirmed open access, 2 (7.4%) sit behind a paywall, and 5 (18.5%) have unconfirmed open-access status — mostly author-archived institutional-repository copies whose publisher-side access tier was not independently checked. **26 of 27 citations (96.3%) are peer-reviewed**; the one exception (a posted reply in an active author dispute) is explicitly flagged as a preprint, not treated as an equal-weight source. The single most notable feature of the week is not any one finding but a recurring shape across three separate topics (precarity → belief, democratic attitudes → belief, and prebunking's cross-cultural transfer): a plausible, popularly assumed causal or generalization claim that failed to hold up, or ran backwards, once a design capable of testing it directly was applied.

## Publisher & venue concentration

No single publisher crossed the 50% alert threshold, but Wiley's various imprints came closest: the *European Journal of Social Psychology* (4 citations), the *British Journal of Social Psychology* (5 citations), and *Political Psychology* (1 citation) together account for **10 of 27 citations (37.0%)**, all published by Wiley on behalf of different scholarly societies (the European Association of Social Psychology, the British Psychological Society, and the International Society of Political Psychology respectively). Springer Nature is the second most-used publisher family (4 citations, 14.8%, across *BMC Public Health*, *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, and *Nature Reviews Psychology*). No other publisher exceeded two citations. This is a moderate concentration worth watching — not because Wiley's science is suspect, but because three of this week's central, unresolved empirical disputes (the precarity debate on days 3 and 5, and the field's flagship theoretical review on day 1) all ran through Wiley-published venues, so a reader relying on this week's readings alone would be leaning heavily on one publisher's editorial and peer-review standards for the week's most contested claims.

The *European Journal of Social Psychology* was this week's single most-cited venue (4 citations) and remains flagged `to-verify` for country in `registry.csv` (its society, the European Association of Social Psychology, does not have a single unambiguous national headquarters the way, say, the British Psychological Society does) — worth Kamil's attention precisely because it is the week's most-used source.

## Evidence mix

Of the 27 citations, **8 (29.6%) are reviews and 2 (7.4%) are meta-analyses** — a reasonable synthesis backbone, anchored by Monday's and Saturday's framing papers and this week's two meta-analyses (Stasielowicz on interventions, day 6; Biddlestone et al. on motives, day 7). **15 (55.6%) are primary studies** — individual experiments, surveys, and panel studies, several of them this week's most consequential sources (the 2026 precarity studies, the Ognyanova PNAS Nexus survey, the Roozenbeek YouTube field trial). **1 citation (3.7%) is a preprint** — a posted author reply in the day 5 precarity dispute, explicitly labeled not peer-reviewed rather than cited as settled fact, exactly as the source-quality rules require. **1 citation (3.7%) is tagged `to-verify` for evidence type** (Adam-Troian et al.'s theoretical model, which does not cleanly fit "primary-study" or "review"). No randomized controlled trials, cohort studies, or clinical guidelines appear this week, which is expected for a social/cognitive psychology theme rather than a clinical one — but it does mean the week's strongest causal claims rest on lab and field experiments and preregistered panel designs rather than the RCT-grade evidence common in health-themed weeks. Given how much of this week's narrative turned on unresolved causal disputes (Wednesday through Friday), a slightly higher share of independent meta-analytic synthesis, sooner in the week, might have flagged the "settled-looking but actually contested" pattern earlier than day 5 did.

## Open access & geography

Open-access share (74.1% confirmed open, excluding the "unknown" category) is solid and consistent with the project's historical baseline (69.6% all-time, per `STATS.md` as of the previous push). The 5 "unknown" citations are concentrated on days 3 and 4, where several sources were accessed as author-archived PDFs on university repositories (Universitat Pompeu Fabra, University of Milano-Bicocca, Griffith University) rather than via the publisher's own open-access page — the archived copy was verifiable and readable, but its formal open-access tier at the publisher was not independently confirmed, which is why it is recorded as "unknown" rather than "open."

Geographically, publisher headquarters this week skew heavily Anglophone: **11 of 27 citations (40.7%) trace to UK-headquartered publishers** and **9 of 27 (33.3%) to US-headquartered publishers**, for a combined 74.1% Anglo-American publisher concentration — before even accounting for the `to-verify` European Journal of Social Psychology (4 citations, 14.8%), which is also published out of the UK/Netherlands via Wiley. This mirrors a pattern flagged directly in today's day-7 synthesis: nearly every *study population* cited this week was drawn from the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, or another Western European country, and the field's own institutional geography (publishers, universities, repositories) reproduces that same concentration rather than counterbalancing it.

## Discipline & recency

Discipline is tightly clustered, which is appropriate for a single-field theme but worth naming: roughly 19 of 27 citations (70%) sit in psychology or social/political psychology specifically, with the remainder split across multidisciplinary megajournals (PNAS Nexus, Heliyon, PLOS ONE, Science Advances — 4 citations), public health (1), criminology (1), and political science (1). No sociology, anthropology, or communication/media-studies venue was cited despite the week repeatedly touching social-network spread (day 1), media environments (day 6), and group-level social structure (day 3) — a plausible gap given how much of that territory a social-psychology framing can cover, but a real one. On recency, the week balances foundational and cutting-edge material well: 3 citations (11.1%) date to 2018, the field's most-cited framing and mechanism papers (van Prooijen & Douglas; van Prooijen & van Vugt; van Prooijen, Douglas & De Inocencio), while 12 of 27 (44.4%) are from 2025-2026, including four separate 2026 studies that directly contest or refine 2023-era theoretical models — a sign of an actively moving field rather than one coasting on older consensus.

## Blind spots & data quality

The clearest data-quality flag is the `european-journal-social-psychology` registry entry: `to-verify` for country, and this week's single most-cited venue (4 citations) — worth resolving given its weight in the week's numbers. A smaller flag: the `british-journal-of-social-psychology` entry carried an `to-verify` evidence-type tag on day 3 (the Adam-Troian theoretical model), left unresolved rather than guessed, per the project's own labeling rules. No citation this week was off-registry. The clearest topical blind spot, already named above, is geographic: every named study population this week was Western (overwhelmingly US, UK, and Germany), a limitation the field's own reviewers (Hornsey et al., cited today) name explicitly as an open gap rather than something this project introduced — but it means a reader should treat this week's empirical claims as claims about WEIRD populations specifically, not about conspiracy belief in general.

## Recommendations

1. **Resolve the `european-journal-social-psychology` country field.** It is the week's most-cited venue and has carried a `to-verify` tag since at least this week's first reading; worth a firm answer (Wiley's registered office, or the European Association of Social Psychology's seat) rather than leaving it open indefinitely.
2. **Watch the Wiley concentration (37% this week) over the coming weeks**, not because of any quality concern, but because three separate society-journal imprints under one publisher carried this week's most contested claims; a future psychology-themed week could deliberately pull from a non-Wiley venue (Frontiers, PLOS, an APA journal, a Cambridge or Oxford title) for at least one contested claim, for independent editorial perspective.
3. **Add a non-Western data point explicitly, when the literature allows it.** This week's day 6 already did this well by surfacing the Indian-language "Bad News" replication failure specifically because it complicated a Western finding; future weeks on topics with an active Global South literature should look for at least one such source deliberately rather than as an incidental find.
4. **Bring in an adjacent discipline** (sociology of belief, media/communication studies, or anthropology of misinformation) for topics like this one where the psychological literature itself repeatedly gestures at social-network and media-environment effects (days 1 and 6) without a dedicated venue from those fields represented among the citations.
5. **Front-load meta-analytic synthesis where a live dispute is likely.** This week's most valuable methodological lesson — that a "settled-looking" causal claim (precarity → belief) was actually a live, contested question — did not fully surface until day 5. Where the day-1 orientation reading already signals field disagreement (as van Prooijen & Douglas's own citing literature does for several mechanisms), pulling a meta-analysis or systematic review a day or two earlier could flag contested claims for the reader sooner.
