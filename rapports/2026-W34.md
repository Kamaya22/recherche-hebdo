# Source diversity — The biology of aging (2026-W34)

## At a glance

This week's seven readings cited **28 sources** (counting each mention separately) drawn from **22 distinct venues**. **64.3%** of citations were open access, **85.7%** were peer-reviewed, and **64.3%** were published in 2023 or later — a genuinely current evidence base, anchored by a handful of older foundational references (the 2009 mouse rapamycin study, the 2013 Horvath epigenetic clock, the 2018–2019 PhenoAge/GrimAge papers) that the week deliberately kept rather than replacing with newer citations, since they are the original sources for tools still in active use. The single most notable finding: publisher concentration is real but not extreme (Springer Nature accounts for 42.9% of citations, just under the 50% alert threshold used in `data/STATS.md`), while evidence-type balance is the week's weaker spot — only 3 of 28 citations (10.7%) are RCTs, reflecting how early-stage human trial evidence still is across rapamycin, metformin, and senolytics.

## Publisher & venue concentration

Springer Nature (across Nature, Nature Reviews Molecular Cell Biology, Nature Reviews Endocrinology, Nature Reviews Genetics, Nature Reviews Drug Discovery, Nature Aging, Nature Communications, Nature Medicine, Genome Biology, and Biogerontology) supplied **12 of 28 citations (42.9%)** — below the 50% single-publisher alert threshold, but still the dominant presence, which is expected given how much of aging biology's highest-profile review literature runs through Nature Portfolio journals. Impact Journals (publisher of *Aging*, source of the PEARL rapamycin trial, PhenoAge, and GrimAge) was the second most-cited publisher at 3 citations (10.7%). No single **venue** was cited more than twice except *Aging* (3 citations, all distinct papers), and 17 of the 22 venues were cited exactly once — a reasonably wide spread of individual journals even though the parent-publisher concentration is real. Geographically, publisher headquarters skewed toward the United Kingdom (57.1%, almost entirely the Springer Nature/Nature Portfolio effect) and the United States (35.7%), with only single citations each from France and the Netherlands — a real concentration, but one that tracks where the most active aging-research publishing actually happens (UK-headquartered Nature Portfolio, US-based NIH/NIA and specialist gerontology journals) rather than an avoidable editorial choice.

## Evidence mix

The week leaned toward synthesis over primary claims: **reviews** were the largest single category (9 citations, 32.1%), followed by **primary studies** (7, 25.0%) and **perspectives/consensus reports** (7, 25.0%). Only **3 citations (10.7%) were RCTs** — the PEARL rapamycin trial, METFORAGING, and the CALERIE/DunedinPACE analysis — which is a fair reflection of the field's actual state rather than a sourcing gap: Day 5 and Day 7 both explicitly discuss how few controlled human trials exist for any of these interventions. No preprints were cited this week; every primary claim traced to a peer-reviewed, published source. Two citations (7.1%) carry an evidence_type of `to-verify`: the NIA Division of Aging Biology overview page (an institutional resource rather than a study, so "evidence type" does not map cleanly onto the taxonomy) and the AFAR TAME trial status page (similarly institutional/programmatic rather than a study). Neither reflects uncertainty about the underlying science — both are administrative/institutional pages correctly flagged rather than force-fit into a evidence category that doesn't describe them.

## Open access & geography

Open access citations (64.3%) outnumbered paywalled ones (17.9%) by a wide margin, with the remainder (17.9%) marked `unknown` — mostly news-feature and perspective pieces (the *Nature* News Feature on the disease debate, the *Biogerontology* evolutionary reappraisal, and this week's own Addie et al. Geroscience Summit synthesis) where access tier could not be confirmed without an institutional login. That open-access share is comparable to the 72.1% all-time figure in `data/STATS.md` (which itself predates this week's citations, per that file's own caveat), suggesting this week did not systematically favor paywalled sources. Geographically, as noted above, publisher headquarters concentrated in the UK and US; no citation this week came from a non-Anglophone-country-headquartered publisher writing in a non-English-first tradition, a real limitation worth naming rather than glossing over — English-language, US/UK-centered publishing dominates this literature broadly, and this week's source pool reflects rather than corrects that.

## Discipline & recency

Citations were dominated by **gerontology** specifically (11 citations, 39.3%) and **multidisciplinary** venues (6, 21.4% — chiefly Cell, Nature, and Nature Communications), with smaller contributions from general-medicine (3), cell-biology (2), pharmacology (2), and single citations each in genetics, genomics, endocrinology, and oncology. That spread is appropriate for a week whose subject is inherently a specialist field (aging biology) that also intersects with cancer biology (senescence's anti-tumor role), pharmacology (drug trials), and genetics (epigenetic clocks). On recency, the week was unusually current: nearly two-thirds of citations (64.3%) were published in 2023 or later, including four from 2026 itself, reflecting how much this field has moved even in the past 18 months (the SenNet senescence atlas, the METFORAGING trial, and this week's own three synthesis sources were all published within the last year). The remaining third supplied necessary foundational grounding — the original hallmarks-of-aging lineage, the 2009 ITP rapamycin mouse result, and the original epigenetic-clock papers — rather than reflecting stale sourcing.

Two pre-existing registry rows remain flagged `to-verify` from earlier weeks (`advances-in-atmospheric-sciences`, `engineering-journal`, per `data/STATS.md`) — unrelated to this week's theme, listed here only because they remain unresolved in the shared registry. No entire class of acceptable source (per `INSTRUCTIONS.md`'s hierarchy) went completely unused this week: peer-reviewed reviews, RCTs, institutional pages (NIA, NIH), and one specialist outreach-adjacent institutional source (AFAR's TAME page) all appear. The one source family never drawn on this week was outreach journalism proper (Quanta, Knowable, The Conversation) — every reading instead came from peer-reviewed literature or official institutional pages, which is defensible for a mechanism-heavy biology theme but worth noting as a pattern.

## Recommendations

1. **Add at least one non-Nature-Portfolio review per week** where the literature allows it — this week's 42.9% Springer Nature share is under the alert threshold but consistently high across recent weeks per `data/STATS.md` (41.0% all-time), so deliberately seeking PLOS, eLife, Cell Press, or society-journal reviews first (before defaulting to Nature Reviews) would diversify the publisher mix further.
2. **Seek a non-Anglophone-publisher source when the theme allows it** — this week had none, and the historical registry shows only isolated non-UK/US entries (Copernicus/Germany, UNESCO/France). A theme like next week's rewilding or gravitational-wave astronomy candidates may offer natural opportunities (European Southern Observatory, Max Planck, CNRS).
3. **Keep prioritizing RCTs where they exist, but flag the gap explicitly when they don't** — this week did this well (Day 5 and Day 7 both name the RCT shortage directly); continue that practice rather than letting reviews and perspectives implicitly stand in for missing trial evidence.
4. **Resolve the two long-standing `to-verify` registry rows** (`advances-in-atmospheric-sciences`, `engineering-journal`) when a future week's theme touches climate or engineering, since they have sat unresolved since before this week's readings.
5. **Watch the news-feature/perspective "unknown" open-access bucket** (17.9% this week) — several of these (Nature News Feature, Biogerontology reappraisal) could likely be confirmed as paywalled or open with one extra verification step next time, tightening the open-access accounting.
