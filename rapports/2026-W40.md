# Source diversity — The sociology of loneliness (2026-W40)

This report covers the seven readings published this week on the sociology of loneliness, based on a direct tally of all seven `data/citations/2026-W40-day-*.json` files (47 citations in total; `data/STATS.md` lags by one reading and is used here only for all-time comparison, not as the source of this week's own numbers).

## At a glance

- **Readings**: 7 (days 1–7, full week). **Citations**: 47 (6–9 per day, counting both formal "today's readings" items and studies named in the body).
- **Open-access share**: 59.6% (28 open / 1 paywall / 18 unknown) — below the project's all-time average of 70.2%, mainly because a large share of Nature-brand and hybrid-access journal articles were conservatively marked "unknown" rather than guessed.
- **Most notable finding**: despite the week's explicit sociological framing ("the sociology of loneliness"), only one dedicated sociology venue was actually cited — *Acta Sociologica* (2 of 47 citations, 4.3%), and only on the synthesis day. The other 45 citations come from psychology, public-health, psychiatry, medicine, and one institutional/government source. The week argued the case for a sociological lens on loneliness while drawing almost all of its evidence from psychological and biomedical literatures.

## Publisher & venue concentration

No single publisher crosses the 50% flag threshold, but two groups dominate: **Springer Nature** (via Nature Portfolio titles — Nature Reviews Psychology, Nature Human Behaviour, Nature Mental Health, Neuropsychopharmacology, Nature News — plus BMC Public Health and Social Psychiatry and Psychiatric Epidemiology) accounts for 12 of 47 citations (25.5%), and **Elsevier** (Psychoneuroendocrinology, Journal of Affective Disorders, SSM – Population Health, Science of the Total Environment, Geriatric Nursing, plus The Lancet Public Health) accounts for 10 of 47 (21.3%). Together these two publishers supply nearly half the week's citations (46.8%). The remainder is reasonably spread: SAGE Publishing 5 (10.6%, via Perspectives on Psychological Science, Assessment, and today's Acta Sociologica), American Psychological Association 4 (8.5%), BMJ Publishing Group 3 (6.4%), World Health Organization 3 (6.4%), and single-digit shares each for the American Medical Association, Frontiers, the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, Taylor & Francis, JMIR Publications, and the Center for Open Science (PsyArXiv). No venue was cited more than 3 times. This is diverse by venue count (18 distinct source IDs across the week) even though two large commercial publishers supply much of the volume — a familiar pattern for biomedical/psychological topics rather than something specific to this week.

## Evidence mix

Meta-analyses dominate (19 of 47, 40.4%), reflecting how heavily Day 3 (mortality/disease risk) and Day 4 (trend data) in particular leaned on large pooled syntheses rather than single studies. Reviews follow (11, 23.4%), then guidelines (5, 10.6% — the WHO Commission report and the Surgeon General's advisory, both institutional and explicitly not peer-reviewed), primary studies (5, 10.6%), perspectives (4, 8.5%), umbrella-reviews (2, 4.3%, from Day 6's "reviews of reviews"), and exactly one preprint (2.1%) — the PsyArXiv construct-map paper used on Day 5, clearly labeled "preprint — not yet peer reviewed" and treated accordingly as a pointer to an ongoing debate, not as settled evidence. Synthesis-level evidence (meta-analyses + reviews + umbrella-reviews) makes up 68.1% of all citations, against primary studies and perspectives at under a third — a sound ratio for a week whose stated goal was separating well-replicated findings from single-study claims. This week's own peer-review rate is 85.1% (40 of 47), close to the project's all-time 84.9%; the 7 non-peer-reviewed citations are the WHO report (3), the Surgeon General's advisory (2), one Nature News piece (1), and the one preprint (1) — each used for exactly the purpose its type permits (institutional context, journalism-level synthesis, or a flagged ongoing debate), not as a stand-in for peer-reviewed evidence.

## Open access & geography

Open access: 28 of 47 citations open (59.6%), 1 paywalled (2.1%, the Cole et al. "conserved transcriptional response" Psychoneuroendocrinology article cited in Day 2's body text), and 18 unknown (38.3%). That "unknown" share is unusually high relative to the project's historical norm and is a genuine data-quality gap this week: several Nature Portfolio articles (Nature Human Behaviour, Nature Mental Health, Nature Reviews Psychology) and a few Elsevier titles were left unconfirmed rather than marked open or paywalled, so the true open-access share for the week could plausibly be meaningfully higher, or lower, than the recorded 59.6%.

Geography is narrow and entirely Global-North: United Kingdom 38.3% (18 citations, mostly Springer Nature's UK registration plus BMJ and Taylor & Francis), United States 29.8% (14, SAGE, APA, AMA, HHS, PsyArXiv), Netherlands 19.1% (9, Elsevier's Amsterdam base), Switzerland 10.6% (5, WHO and Frontiers), and Canada 2.1% (1, JMIR). No citation this week traces to a publisher headquartered outside these five countries. That is a slightly pointed gap given the week's own content: Day 1 and Day 4 both documented large loneliness-data deserts in low- and middle-income countries, yet the week's own source base does not include a single venue from the Global South, or from continental Europe outside the Netherlands and Switzerland.

## Discipline & recency

Discipline spread: psychology 25.5% (12), public-health 23.4% (11), psychiatry 12.8% (6), with smaller shares for neuroendocrinology, multidisciplinary, general-medicine, neuroscience, cardiology (each 2–3 citations), and single citations each for digital-health, environmental-health, gerontology — and sociology at just 4.3% (2), both from today's Acta Sociologica reading. For a week framed as "the sociology of loneliness," the discipline tag confirms the publisher-concentration finding above: the operative lens was overwhelmingly clinical, epidemiological and psychological rather than sociological, with the sociological framing supplied mostly by the agent's own synthesis (Luhmann et al.'s "macro-level factors" argument on Day 1, Van de Velde's explicit field-defining piece today) rather than by a correspondingly wide base of sociology citations.

Recency is healthy: 61.7% of citations (29 of 47) are from 2023–2026, with 2025 the single best-represented year (10 citations, 21.3%). The remaining 38.3% are not stale filler but deliberately foundational anchors — Cacioppo et al.'s 2014 evolutionary theory of loneliness, Holt-Lunstad et al.'s 2015 mortality meta-analysis, Cole's 2015 conserved-transcriptional-response work, and Valtorta et al.'s 2016 cardiovascular meta-analysis — each cited specifically to let later, larger studies (Days 3–4) be checked against the estimates that originally established the field's key numbers.

## Blind spots & data quality

No `to-verify` evidence types and no off-registry citations appear this week: every source_id used across all seven days resolves to a `registry.csv` row, including the three new rows added today for the Surgeon General's advisory, Acta Sociologica, and The Journal of Psychology. The two genuine blind spots are the ones named above: (1) a sociological topic supported by an almost entirely non-sociological citation base, and (2) a geographically narrow, Global-North-only publisher footprint that mirrors exactly the kind of data gap Day 4's own reading (Surkalim et al., 2022) warned about for the underlying loneliness literature itself.

## Recommendations

1. **Pull more directly from sociology and social-policy journals** in future weeks with a social-science framing (e.g., *Journal of Marriage and Family*, *Social Science & Medicine*, *European Sociological Review*, *Sociological Review*) rather than leaning on psychology and public-health venues to carry a sociological argument.
2. **Tighten open-access verification** for Nature-brand and other hybrid-access journals — checking for a visible CC-BY license or an explicit "open access" badge on the article page — rather than defaulting to "unknown," which this week left over a third of citations unclassified on that dimension.
3. **Actively seek at least one non-Anglophone or Global-South source per week** (e.g., an INSERM, CNRS, or regional WHO-office publication, or a study based outside the US/UK/Netherlands/Switzerland/Canada axis) to avoid the same five-country footprint recurring across themes.
4. **Revisit the youth-intervention gap flagged on Day 6** with a dedicated search for loneliness-intervention trials run outside the US/UK, since the existing evidence base for children and adolescents was already found thin in Day 6's reading and is, per this report, also geographically narrow.
5. **Keep the current preprint discipline**: one clearly labeled, appropriately caveated preprint out of 47 citations is a reasonable, transparent use of unreviewed evidence — continue reserving preprints for flagging live debates rather than citing them as settled findings.
