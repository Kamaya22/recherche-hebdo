# Source diversity — The regeneration paradox (2026-W42)

## At a glance

Seven readings this week cited **23 sources** across **18 distinct venues**. Open access share was **70% (16/23)**, with 4 citations behind a paywall and 3 of unconfirmed access status. The most notable finding: **Springer Nature-published venues (Nature, Nature Communications, Nature Cell Biology, EvoDevo, Mammalian Genome) account for 8 of 23 citations (35%)** — well above this project's all-time baseline of 22% for the same publisher (per `STATS.md`, last generated 2026-10-17) — because several of the field's foundational and most recent papers (Kragl et al. 2009, the 2025 Hand2–Shh paper, this week's 2026 oxygen-sensing paper) all happen to sit in Nature-family journals.

## Publisher & venue concentration

No single **venue** dominates — "Nature" the journal itself was cited 4 times (2022, 2009, 2013, 2020 editions) and "Science" twice (2018, 2026), but the next most-cited venue, Frontiers in Cell and Developmental Biology, appears only twice. At the **publisher** level, though, concentration is real: Springer Nature (8/23, 35%), AAAS/Science+Science Advances (3/23, 13%), Frontiers (3/23, 13%), and Wiley across three different imprints (3/23, 13%) together account for 17 of 23 citations (74%). This is higher publisher concentration than the project's all-time average and is this week's single biggest diversity flag — not because any individual source is low-quality (all are peer-reviewed, reputable venues), but because a reader relying only on this week's readings would get a skewed sense of which publishers dominate the field, when in fact the concentration mostly reflects that Nature-family and Science-family journals happen to publish a disproportionate share of axolotl/zebrafish regeneration research specifically, not biomedical research generally.

## Evidence mix

- **Reviews**: 8/23 (35%) — EvoDevo, Frontiers Cell Dev Biol (Day 1), CSH Perspectives, Clinical and Translational Medicine, Advanced Science, Biological Reviews, International Journal of Developmental Biology, Development.
- **Primary studies**: 13/23 (57%) — the majority of citations, reflecting that this theme leans on mechanistic, hypothesis-testing biology (knockouts, lineage tracing, single-cell atlases) rather than clinical-trial evidence.
- **Perspectives**: 2/23 (9%) — the NICHD workshop page (Day 1) and the Frontiers in Neuroscience CNS piece (Day 5).
- **Meta-analyses, umbrella reviews, RCTs, preprints**: 0/23.

This mix is appropriate for a basic-science theme but notably thin on synthesis relative to primary findings — a reader gets more "here is one experiment's result" than "here is the field's consensus," which this week's Day 7 synthesis was written specifically to compensate for. The complete absence of preprints is unremarkable here (unlike, say, a fast-moving physics or AI theme) since developmental biology's publication-to-preprint ratio is naturally low, but it also means no very latest (sub-peer-review) findings were surfaced.

## Open access & geography

Open-access share (70%) is in line with the project's all-time baseline (69.6%), so this week is not an outlier on paywalling. By publisher-HQ country, UK (9/23, 39% — almost entirely Springer Nature and other UK-registered publishers) and the United States (6/23, 26% — AAAS, NIH, Cold Spring Harbor Press, National Academy of Sciences) together account for 65% of citations, with Switzerland (Frontiers, 13%), Germany (6%), and Spain (4%) making up the rest. This is a real Anglophone/transatlantic concentration, though it should be read with a caveat: in this field, the Vienna (Tanaka lab) and Harvard (Whited lab) groups that generated much of the primary research this week publish in UK/US-headquartered journals as a matter of where the field's top venues sit, not because non-Anglophone research was overlooked — several of the actual labs cited (Vienna, China's Ocean University, Germany's pediatric-surgery case series) are themselves outside the UK/US.

## Discipline & recency

By registry discipline, "multidisciplinary" megajournals/flagships (Nature, Science, Nature Communications, PNAS, Advanced Science, Science Advances) account for 10/23 (43%) of citations — a labelling artifact more than a topical one, since every one of those citations is in fact about regeneration specifically; "developmental-biology" is the next largest bucket at 7/23 (30%), with single citations each in cell-biology, surgery, general-medicine, evolutionary-biology, neuroscience, and genetics. Recency is skewed toward the last five years: 13/23 citations (57%) are from 2021–2026, including this week's day-7 addition of a paper published just this month. Four citations (17%) are foundational, pre-2015 work (Kragl et al. 2009, Godwin et al. and Takeo et al., both 2013) deliberately kept in the mix because they are the primary evidence behind claims the field still treats as settled, not because newer alternatives don't exist.

## Blind spots & data quality

- Three citations have unconfirmed open-access status (`unknown`): the 2024 Biological Reviews piece, the 2020 Nature reprogramming study, and the 2005 Mammalian Genome study (the latter mentioned in text only, not independently fetched). Two venues already carry a `to-verify` country in `registry.csv` (Clinical and Translational Medicine, Mammalian Genome) and are flagged in `STATS.md`'s alerts list — unchanged by this week's additions.
- The two source "families" explicitly allowed by this project's rules but never used this week are **preprints** (acceptable but unremarkable to skip, given the field) and **outreach** (Quanta, Knowable, The Conversation) — no lay-accessible, researcher-written piece was cited in any of the seven readings, which is a real gap for a project meant to produce a ~30-minute *reading*, not only a technical digest.
- One methodological note specific to today: the two Day 7 sources (the 2026 Science oxygen-sensing paper and the 2022 Science Advances bioreactor paper) were verified through the papers' own indexed abstracts and multiple, mutually independent and consistent institutional/press accounts, because a tooling outage during this research session prevented directly fetching the primary texts or press pages. This is disclosed in `weeks/2026-W42/day-7.md` itself; it is a lower-confidence verification path than this project's norm of reading the source directly, and is flagged here so it is visible in the week's record, not just in the reading.

## Recommendations

1. **Add an outreach/explainer source in future weeks.** None of this week's 23 citations came from Quanta, Knowable, or The Conversation despite all three being explicitly allowed — worth deliberately including one per week where a good one exists, both for publisher diversity and for a more accessible "way in" for non-specialist readers.
2. **Watch publisher concentration on multidisciplinary topics.** Any week built around a handful of model-organism labs (as this one was) will naturally skew toward whichever 2-3 flagship journals those labs publish in; a useful check going forward is to deliberately look for the same finding reported in a smaller or regional journal when one exists, rather than defaulting to the flagship citation.
3. **Resolve the two long-standing `to-verify` registry rows** (Clinical and Translational Medicine, Mammalian Genome) when convenient — both are now cited by more than one week's reading, which makes a confident discipline/country call more useful in `STATS.md`'s aggregates.
4. **Re-verify the two Day 7 sources once tooling is back to normal**, since they were accepted this week on indirect (multi-source-corroborated) rather than direct verification — a quick direct read would either confirm or require a correction to `weeks/2026-W42/day-7.md`.
5. **Consider a short "evidence mix" note on weeks like this one**, where primary studies outnumber reviews two-to-one — not a problem in itself, but worth being explicit that a mechanistic-biology theme will structurally produce a different evidence mix than a clinical or policy theme, so `STATS.md`'s all-time evidence-type distribution shouldn't be read as a single uniform target.
