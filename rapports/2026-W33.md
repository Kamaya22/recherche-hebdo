# Source diversity — The deep ocean (2026-W33)

*Covers the citation data recorded for this week: `data/citations/2026-W33-day-3.json` through `-day-7.json`. See the data-quality note below on Days 1–2.*

## At a glance

- **Readings with logged citation data**: 5 of 7 (Days 3–7; Days 1–2 have no `data/citations/` file — see Blind spots).
- **Total citations logged this week**: 27, across 18 distinct venues and 12 distinct publishers.
- **Open-access share**: 77.8% (21 of 27) — comfortably above the project's 50% flag threshold.
- **Most notable finding**: no preprints were cited all week, and peer-reviewed sources make up 85.2% of citations — but the four non-peer-reviewed items are exclusively institutional syntheses and progress reports (European Marine Board, Congressional Research Service, IOC-UNESCO ×2), used deliberately for governance and knowledge-gap framing rather than as substitutes for primary science.

## Publisher & venue concentration

Springer Nature is the single largest publisher this week at 9 of 27 citations (33.3%) — spanning *Nature*, *Nature Communications*, *Scientific Reports*, *Nature Ecology & Evolution*, *Nature Reviews Earth & Environment*, and *Advances in Atmospheric Sciences*. That is well short of the project's 50% concentration-alert threshold, and it reflects Nature Portfolio's genuine breadth across sub-journals rather than reliance on one title — no single venue was cited more than 3 times. Behind Springer Nature, the mix is reasonably even: Copernicus Publications (*Biogeosciences*, 3 citations), Elsevier/Cell Press (*Cell*, *Current Biology*, 2), Annual Reviews (2), AAAS (*Science Advances*, 2), UNESCO (2), and Wiley (2), with MDPI, the National Academy of Sciences, the Chinese Academy of Engineering/Elsevier, the European Marine Board, and the Library of Congress each appearing once. Today's reading (Day 7) is the main source of publisher diversification this week, introducing three venues — *Science Advances*, the European Marine Board, and the Congressional Research Service — that had never been cited in this project before.

## Evidence mix

The evidence-type distribution is: primary-study 16 (59.3%), review 8 (29.6%), to-verify 2 (7.4%), perspective 1 (3.7%). There are no RCTs, cohort studies, or meta-analyses this week — expected for a week on environmental/oceanographic science rather than clinical medicine, where that hierarchy doesn't apply the same way. The balance leans toward primary findings (mining-trial results, microplastic surveys, biogeochemical models) anchored by field-defining reviews (the Annual Review of Marine Science pump synthesis, the Nature Reviews Earth & Environment AABW review, today's European Marine Board knowledge-gap brief). No preprints were used at any point in the week — a clean record on the project's strictest sourcing rule.

## Open access & geography

Open access sits at 77.8% (21/27), well above the 50% alert line; the 6 paywalled citations are concentrated in *Cell*, *Current Biology*, and *Nature Reviews Earth & Environment* — all reputable venues cited via their peer-reviewed abstracts/summaries as the source-quality rules allow. Geographically, publisher headquarters skew heavily Anglo-American: United States 37.0% (10/27) and United Kingdom 29.6% (8/27) together account for 66.7% of citations, exceeding the project's 60% single-country-family alert threshold when combined. Germany (3), France (2, both UNESCO/IOC), Switzerland (1), and Belgium (1, this week's new European Marine Board addition) provide the rest. Two citations (advances-in-atmospheric-sciences, engineering-journal) carry a `to-verify` publisher country in the registry, both flagged since their journals are genuinely dual-national (China/international) — unresolved, not misclassified.

## Discipline & recency

Climate-related disciplines dominate (11/27, 40.7%), followed by multidisciplinary megajournal-type venues (9/27, 33.3%), oceanography (3/27, 11.1% — all from institutional sources: IOC-UNESCO and, new this week, the European Marine Board), ecology (2/27, 7.4%), and one citation each in technology and international law (the latter a first for this project, from today's CRS reading). On recency, the week is strongly weighted to current science: 2025–2026 publications account for 59.3% (16/27) of citations, with two foundational older references (2014, mesopelagic biomass and seafloor-biomass climate projections) retained deliberately as baseline studies rather than as evidence of stale sourcing.

## Blind spots & data quality

The most important gap this week is not in the science but in the record-keeping: **Days 1 and 2 of 2026-W33 have no `data/citations/*.json` file**, even though both `day-1.md` and `day-2.md` cite real, verifiable sources (among them Bell et al. 2025 in *Science Advances*, Rabone et al. 2023 in *Current Biology*, Siegel et al. 2023 in *Annual Review of Marine Science*, a 2024 *Science* paper on pressure-adapted phospholipids, a 2014 PNAS TMAO study, and a 2025 *Nature* paper on hadal chemosynthesis). This means every count in this report — and in `data/STATS.md` — undercounts the week's true citation load by roughly 6–8 sources, and *Science* (the flagship journal, distinct from *Science Advances*) is entirely absent from the registry despite being cited on Day 2. This report does not attempt to reconstruct those two files retroactively, since only the agent that wrote each day's reading should log its own citations; flagging the gap here is the appropriate fix so it is visible and can be corrected. Beyond that: the registry now carries two persistent `to-verify` country entries (`advances-in-atmospheric-sciences`, `engineering-journal`) that remain unresolved after two weeks, and `ioc-unesco` has twice been logged with evidence_type `to-verify` for its Seabed 2030 progress announcements — a deliberate, stable choice (institutional press-style updates don't fit `review`/`primary-study`) rather than an oversight, but worth Kamil's confirmation for consistency going forward. Source families never used this week: `institution`-type UN/national-academy sources beyond UNESCO/EMB/CRS (e.g. NASA, ESA, WHO), and `outreach`-type venues (Quanta, Knowable, The Conversation) — despite Day 2's guided summary explicitly recommending the Quanta Magazine coverage of the 2024 *Science* pressure-adaptation paper as an accessible alternative, that outreach piece itself was never logged as a citation.

## Recommendations

1. **Backfill or explicitly write off Days 1–2's citation data.** As it stands, this week's true source count and diversity are understated in every downstream statistic; a short note in `data/README.md` or a one-time backfill (clearly marked as reconstructed) would prevent silent undercounting in future weekly reports.
2. **Resolve the two persistent `to-verify` registry rows** (`advances-in-atmospheric-sciences`, `engineering-journal`) — both have carried unresolved publisher-country flags since last week; a quick manual check would let them drop out of `STATS.md`'s alert list.
3. **Use at least one `outreach`-tier source per week going forward.** This week (and, per Day 2's own text, last week) recommended freely accessible outreach coverage (Quanta Magazine) without ever formally citing and logging it — logging it would diversify the venue-type mix beyond `journal`/`megajournal`/`institution`.
4. **Consider a `law-policy` or `international-law` discipline convention going forward**, now that governance sources (BBNJ/CRS) have entered the registry — today's ad hoc `international-law` label works but should be confirmed as the standing convention rather than reinvented per source.
5. **Keep leaning on institutional syntheses for closing weeks**, as today's European Marine Board and CRS sources did well — but pair them, as this week did, with at least one fresh primary-research source (here, the *Science Advances* exploration-goals paper) so synthesis days don't drift toward secondary-source-only citation.
