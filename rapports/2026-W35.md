# Source diversity — Rewilding (2026-W35)

## At a glance

This week produced 7 readings and 26 logged citations (14 formal "today's readings" items, 12 studies or reports named in the body). 16 of 26 citations (61.5%) were open access, 6 (23.1%) sat behind a paywall, and 4 (15.4%) had unconfirmed access status. 25 of 26 (96.2%) were peer-reviewed; the one exception is the IUCN's 2025 Guidelines for Rewilding, an institutional standard rather than a journal article, correctly labeled non-peer-reviewed rather than miscounted. The single most notable pattern this week is a near-complete absence of one source tier the project's own rules explicitly allow: not one "outreach" venue (Quanta, Knowable, The Conversation) was cited across all seven days, despite ecology being a field with strong researcher-written outreach coverage.

## Publisher & venue concentration

No single publisher imprint crossed the project's 50% concentration threshold. The largest were Wiley (Society for Conservation Biology), 4 of 26 citations (15.4%, all from *Conservation Letters*), and Elsevier proper, 5 of 26 (19.2%, all from *Global Ecology and Conservation* and *Biological Conservation*). But grouped at the corporate-publisher level rather than by imprint, two families did most of the work: Wiley across its various society-journal imprints (British Ecological Society, Society for Conservation Biology, Nordic Society Oikos, Ecological Society of America) supplied 9 of 26 citations (34.6%), and Elsevier across its imprints (plain Elsevier plus Cell Press) supplied 8 of 26 (30.8%) — together nearly two-thirds of the week's sources. That is a real departure from the project's all-time pattern in `data/STATS.md`, where Springer Nature alone accounts for 33% of all-time citations; this week Springer Nature supplied only 3 of 26 (11.5%, via *Nature* and *Ambio*). The shift reflects the field, not a change in sourcing habits — *Conservation Letters*, *Global Ecology and Conservation* and *Biological Conservation* are simply where the discipline's ecology-of-rewilding literature concentrates — but a Wiley-or-Elsevier citation is still the most likely single outcome of picking any source this week, worth watching if next week's theme is also ecology-adjacent.

## Evidence mix

The mix skewed toward synthesis and argument over primary data: 11 of 26 citations (42.3%) were perspectives/commentaries/critiques (expected for a week whose Day 4 was explicitly about open controversies), 7 (26.9%) were reviews, 7 (26.9%) were primary studies (field studies, case comparisons, stakeholder interviews), and 1 (3.8%) was an institutional guideline. No meta-analyses, RCTs or cohort studies appeared, which is unsurprising for a landscape-ecology topic rather than a clinical one — those study types are simply not the field's dominant currency, so their absence is not itself a diversity gap. More notably, no preprint was cited anywhere this week: every empirical or synthesis claim traced to a peer-reviewed, published source, including the live 2025 Yellowstone methodological dispute (Day 2 and Day 4), which was covered through the peer-reviewed original paper and its peer-reviewed comment rather than through any preprint version.

## Open access & geography

Open access ran at 61.5% this week, modestly below the project's 68.2% all-time average; four citations (15.4%) had genuinely unconfirmed status and are worth someone re-checking rather than treating as paywalled by default. Geographically, using the registry's publisher-headquarters field, citations skewed toward the United States (11 of 26, 42.3%) and the United Kingdom (6, 23.1%), with the Netherlands a distant third (5, 19.2%, driven almost entirely by Elsevier's Amsterdam HQ classification for two heavily cited journals). That geography measures where a journal's publisher is incorporated, not where the underlying research or fieldwork happened — this week's actual studies were set in the US (Yellowstone, Minnesota), the UK (Knepp, Boothby), Spain, and cross-border Indigenous territory in North America and Tasmania, which is a more geographically varied picture than the publisher-HQ tally alone suggests. Two citations (7.7%), both from *Ambio*, are logged against a venue still marked `to-verify` for country in `registry.csv`.

## Discipline & recency

69.2% of citations (18/26) came from ecology-classified venues and 23.1% (6/26) from multidisciplinary flagship journals (*Science*, *Science Advances*, *Nature*, *Current Biology*, *BioScience*), with the remaining 7.7% (2/26, both *Ambio*) classified as environmental-science. That is a tight, on-theme discipline spread with essentially no drift into unrelated fields. Recency was skewed heavily toward the present: 12 of 26 citations (46.2%) were from 2025 alone, and 19 of 26 (73.1%) from 2023 or later — appropriate for a week partly organized around a live, unresolved 2025 scientific dispute. But the week also reached back for genuine foundational grounding: Donlan et al.'s 2005 *Nature* Pleistocene-rewilding proposal, the 2006 *Biological Conservation* rebuttal to it, and Hobbs et al.'s 2009 "novel ecosystems" paper in *Trends in Ecology & Evolution* gave Day 4's controversies real historical depth rather than treating them as purely contemporary debates.

## Blind spots & data quality

Three things worth flagging. First, and most concretely actionable: `Ambio`'s registry entry has carried a `to-verify` country flag since it was first added, and this week added two more citations against it without resolving that flag — Kamil may want to confirm whether *Ambio*'s country-of-record should follow its publisher (Springer Nature, UK/Germany) or its sponsoring society (the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, Sweden). Second, the complete absence of any outreach-tier source (Knowable Magazine, The Conversation, university press outreach) this week is a gap the source-quality rules explicitly permit closing — ecology is a field with strong researcher-authored outreach writing, and none was used across seven days. Third, a content-level blind spot rather than a sourcing one: Day 3's own reading (Hart et al.'s scoping review) found the rewilding evidence base itself skewed toward mammals, plants, and a handful of Northern European sites, under-representing invertebrates, fungi, soil processes, and southern/eastern European projects — a bias this week's citations necessarily inherited from the literature rather than introduced independently, but worth remembering if rewilding resurfaces as a theme.

## Recommendations

1. When ecology or conservation themes recur, deliberately look for at least one outreach-tier piece (Knowable Magazine, The Conversation by a researcher) to diversify beyond the journal-only citation pattern seen this week.
2. Ask Kamil to resolve the long-standing `to-verify` country flag on `ambio` in `registry.csv`, since the venue was cited twice more this week without the ambiguity being settled.
3. For a future rewilding-adjacent theme, actively seek sources on southern/eastern European or non-European rewilding projects, and on under-monitored taxa (invertebrates, fungi, soil), to counter the geographic and taxonomic bias Day 3's own reading identified in the field's evidence base.
4. Treat "country" in `registry.csv` as publisher headquarters, not research geography, when reading future weekly reports — this week's apparent US/UK/Netherlands concentration is partly a publisher-incorporation artifact rather than a sign the underlying fieldwork was geographically narrow.
5. Given how much of this week's evidence-quality story (Days 3–5) turned on attribution and counterfactual design, a methods-focused future theme (e.g., causal inference in observational ecology, or replication in field biology) could usefully cite some of the same *Conservation Science and Practice* / impact-evaluation literature from a different angle.
