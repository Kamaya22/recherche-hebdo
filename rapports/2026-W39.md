# Source diversity — Solar geoengineering (2026-W39)

## At a glance

Seven readings, 24 citations across 16 distinct venues and 14 distinct publishers (no venue or publisher repeated more than 4 times). Open-access share: 75.0% (18/24), above the project's all-time average of 72.7%. Zero preprints, zero off-registry or newly flagged `to-verify` venues. The single most notable finding: this week is markedly less peer-reviewed than the project's norm — 62.5% of citations (15/24) came from peer-reviewed journals, versus an 86.3% all-time average — because the topic itself (funding, experiments, controversy, governance) is disproportionately covered by institutional reports and journal news/perspective sections rather than primary research articles. That is a property of the subject, not a lapse in source quality: every non-peer-reviewed source this week was a national academy, a UN body, a federal science agency, or a nonpartisan legislative research service — the top tier of the acceptable-institutions list, not a downgrade to blogs or general press.

## Publisher & venue concentration

No single publisher dominated. Springer Nature led with 4 of 24 citations (16.7%), followed by Wiley/AGU journals with 3 (12.5%) and two publishers tied at 2 each (Copernicus Publications; IOP Publishing; Cambridge University Press). Every other publisher — Annual Reviews, the Royal Society of Chemistry, USGS, NOAA, WMO, AAAS, the US EPA, the US National Academies, UNEP, the Royal Society, and the Library of Congress — contributed exactly one citation. This is a healthier spread than the project's cumulative picture, where Springer Nature alone accounts for 21.9% of all citations to date; this week's Springer Nature share (16.7%) sits comfortably below that, and no venue was reused more than twice (Earth's Future, the Journal of Geophysical Research: Atmospheres via Wiley/AGU, Copernicus's Atmospheric Chemistry and Physics, and IOP's Environmental Research Letters each appeared twice).

## Evidence mix

The evidence-type balance skews toward synthesis and policy analysis rather than primary data: 3 reviews (12.5%), 9 perspective pieces (37.5%), 5 guideline-type institutional reports (20.8%), and 7 primary studies (29.2%) — no meta-analyses, no RCTs or cohort studies, and no preprints. That mix is appropriate rather than concerning: solar geoengineering has almost no field-deployment data to generate clinical-trial-style evidence, so the literature is genuinely dominated by modeling papers (classified here as primary studies), review syntheses, and institutional/policy assessments. The near-absence of primary studies on the governance side specifically (all three of today's new sources are perspective- or guideline-type, none primary-study) simply reflects that governance proposals are not the kind of claim a primary study produces.

## Open access & geography

Open access reached 75.0% (18 open, 2 paywalled — Science and Nature Reviews Earth & Environment — and 4 unknown, mostly Cambridge Core and Springer Nature articles whose specific access tier could not be confirmed from the citation alone). Geographically, publishers split almost evenly between the United States (10/24, 41.7%) and the United Kingdom (10/24, 41.7%), with the remainder in Germany (Copernicus, 2), Switzerland (WMO, 1), and Kenya (UNEP, 1). Combined, US and UK publishers accounted for 83.3% of this week's sources — a concentration that, notably, is exactly the pattern Wednesday's Stephens & Surprise reading (Day 5) identified as a structural feature of the solar-geoengineering research field itself, not an artifact of how this project happened to search. The only Global South-headquartered institutional voice cited this week was UNEP (Nairobi); no citation this week originated from a Global South-based journal or research institution, and the week's discussion of African, South Asian, or Latin American perspectives (the AMCEN rejection, monsoon-region concerns) came entirely through Global North-published venues reporting on them, mentioned in Day 7's text but not sourced directly to a Global South venue.

## Discipline & recency

Climate-discipline venues dominated as expected for the topic (14/24, 58.3%), with multidisciplinary megajournals and news sections next (4/24, 16.7%) and a meaningful international-law slice (2/24, 8.3%: Transnational Environmental Law and the Congressional Research Service) reflecting this week's governance focus — a discipline that barely registers in the project's all-time distribution (1 citation before this week). Ecology, geology, environmental-policy and environmental-science each contributed a single citation. Recency was high: all but one source (the 2011 USGS Volcano Watch piece, used specifically to explain the volcanic analogy rather than as a claim about current science) were published 2014 or later, and 8 of 24 were from 2024–2026, including a Royal Society briefing published in November 2025 and an Environmental Research Letters review from 2026 — about as current as peer-reviewed literature gets.

## Blind spots & data quality

No `to-verify` or off-registry items were introduced this week; both new venues (Transnational Environmental Law, the Royal Society) were added to `registry.csv` with a confirmed publisher and country. Project-wide, `STATS.md` (as last regenerated, before this week's later readings) still lists five older to-verify venues from other weeks' themes and ten to-verify evidence-type citations — none from this week, but still open items for Kamil to resolve. Two open-access statuses this week (Transnational Environmental Law, Annual Review of Environment and Resources) were marked "unknown" rather than guessed, per the project's own preference for honesty over false precision; both are hybrid-access venues where the specific article's status could not be confirmed without full-text access. The clearest blind spot is geographic, as noted above: this week's sourcing, like the field it describes, is Global-North-heavy, and no Global South-based venue was cited even once across all seven days.

## Recommendations

1. **Diversify geography deliberately next time a governance-heavy theme comes up.** This week's 83.3% US/UK publisher share mirrors a real feature of the field, but future weeks on similarly Global-North-dominated topics should actively search for venues based in Asia, Africa, or Latin America (e.g. TWAS-affiliated outlets, regional academies) even when the underlying research literature is thin there.
2. **Resolve the two "unknown" open-access flags** (Transnational Environmental Law, Annual Review of Environment and Resources) by checking each article's specific access page directly, rather than leaving them unresolved indefinitely.
3. **When a week leans heavily on institutional/perspective sources** (as this one did, at 58.3% combined guideline + perspective), consider pairing at least one such source each day with a primary modeling or empirical paper where one exists, to keep the peer-reviewed share closer to the project average.
4. **Track the UNEA resolution and SGRG Platform stories forward.** Both were live, unresolved processes as of this reading (the SGRG Platform's formal launch was slated for July 2026); a future short update, if this theme resurfaces, could cite their outcomes directly rather than the process-in-progress sources used here.
5. **Consider a dedicated future week on international environmental-law governance mechanisms** (the Vienna Convention/Montreal Protocol model, the London Convention/Protocol, the CBD's de facto moratorium precedent) — this week's international-law sources were strong but narrowly scoped to solar geoengineering specifically, and the broader comparative-governance literature was only referenced, not directly read.
